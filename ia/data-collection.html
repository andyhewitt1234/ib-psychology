1 What IB Requires — Criterion C 6 marks

Criterion C assesses three components:

Create One Data Collection Tool

The tool must measure behaviour relevant to the aim/RQ. Acceptable examples include, but are not limited to: questionnaire, Likert-type scale, observation checklist, interview schedule.

Explain the Decisions Made When Creating It

This may include identifying/operationalizing variables, types of questions/items, measures used, links to existing measures, and how the tool measures what it is intended to measure.

Discuss Potential Challenges

Identify and explain challenges that could affect data collection or findings. Choose challenges relevant to your proposed investigation.

For the strongest descriptor (5–6) the tool must be appropriate AND effective, decisions must be EXPLAINED and relevant, and challenges must be EXPLAINED and relevant.

This is NOT a checklist requiring every possible challenge. Students should choose challenges relevant to their proposed investigation.
2

When does IB require at least five items?

FIVE-ITEM MINIMUM

A data collection tool used for:

  • survey/questionnaire
  • structured interview
  • controlled observation

must contain a minimum of five items. For example, a questionnaire must contain at least five questions/items.

Do NOT extend this rule to every possible data collection tool. The five-item minimum applies specifically to survey/questionnaire, structured interview, and controlled observation.
Appendix: A copy of the data collection tool should be provided in the appendix. Students are NOT required to provide completed additional materials such as informed consent forms or standardised instructions for this criterion.

5

What do you actually need to measure?

The data collection tool must grow out of the aim/RQ. Do NOT start by writing random questionnaire questions. Start by asking: What information would I need in order to address my aim/RQ?

Aim / RQ The question your tool must serve
↓
Target behaviour / variable / construct What specifically needs to be captured?
↓
What could actually be recorded? Observable actions, self-report responses, measurable outcomes
↓
Data collection tool The instrument that captures the data
Do not start with the tool and work backwards. Start with the aim/RQ and let it dictate what must be measured, which then dictates the tool.
6

What needs to be measured?

For each fictional aim/RQ, identify the most relevant behaviour, variable or construct that would need to be captured by the data collection tool.

“To investigate whether hours of social media use per day are associated with self-reported sleep quality among students aged 15–17.”
CORRECT. The aim directly names the two variables: social media hours and sleep quality. The tool must capture both.
“To explore the experiences of students who recently moved schools and how they describe their sense of belonging in a new class.”
CORRECT. The aim seeks personal accounts of belonging and transition. The tool must capture subjective experiences, not grades or friendship counts.
“To count how often students check their phones during 30 minutes of silent study.”
CORRECT. The aim measures observable frequency of a specific behaviour. The tool must record phone checks as they happen.
“To compare motivation before and after a two-minute mindfulness break at the start of lessons.”
CORRECT. The aim compares motivation before vs after. The tool must capture motivation at both time points to allow comparison.
“To investigate whether students conform to an incorrect group answer in a judgement task.”
CORRECT. The aim measures conformity — whether the participant goes along with the group. The tool must record the participant’s actual answer.
“To measure whether perceived social connectedness is associated with well-being across a large student sample.”
CORRECT. The aim names two self-reported constructs. The tool must measure both social connectedness and well-being.
7

Turn an idea into something you can measure

Psychological constructs can be abstract. A data collection tool needs to translate the relevant idea into something that could actually be asked, rated, observed or recorded. This process is operationalization.

Abstract Construct

stress, trust, safety behaviour, motivation, social connectedness, attention

Measurable Indicator

What specific behaviour, response or rating captures this idea?

Item / Question / Category

The actual thing the tool asks or records

Abstract construct The psychological idea
↓
Measurable indicator What specific thing captures this?
↓
Item / question / observation category What the tool actually asks or records
Operationalization is a Criterion C design decision. Do not duplicate the Research Methods unit. Teach it specifically as a decision you make when creating your tool.
8

Operationalized or still vague?

For each statement, decide: is the construct operationalized (translated into something measurable) or still too vague?

“Measure academic stress.”
STILL TOO VAGUE. “Academic stress” is an abstract construct. What would you actually ask or record? How would participants respond?
“Use several items asking participants to rate specific experiences associated with academic workload and perceived pressure on a 5-point scale.”
OPERATIONALIZED. The construct is translated into measurable items, a defined response format, and specific aspects of the experience.
“Assess motivation.”
STILL TOO VAGUE. Motivation for what? Measured how? What would participants actually do or report?
“Record whether each participant chooses to continue working during a free period or switches to a leisure activity, measured as a binary choice.”
OPERATIONALIZED. The abstract idea of motivation is translated into a specific observable behaviour with a clear recording method.
“Use an observation to measure social behaviour.”
STILL TOO VAGUE. What specific behaviours count as “social behaviour”? How would they be identified and recorded?
“Define social behaviour as: initiating conversation, responding to a peer, or joining a group activity. Record the frequency of each during a 20-minute break period.”
OPERATIONALIZED. The abstract construct is broken into specific observable categories with a defined recording method.
9

Learn from real IA marking — operationalization

Examiner lesson

A strong Criterion C does not merely name a construct. It shows HOW the construct becomes measurable. The official high-scoring example explained how abstract constructs were translated into measurable behaviours/items.

Weak vs strong operationalization

Weak tool idea

“A questionnaire about social connectedness would be used.”

≠

Better operationalization

“A questionnaire would include items asking participants to rate how often they feel close to others, how included they feel in group activities, and how supported they feel by peers — each on a 5-point frequency scale.”

Why is the second version stronger?
IT SHOWS HOW THE ABSTRACT CONSTRUCT BECOMES MEASURABLE. Length alone does not create strength. The second version names specific measurable indicators (feeling close, feeling included, feeling supported) and a defined response format.
10

Choose the right tool

The data collection tool must align with the research method chosen in Criterion B.

Questionnaire / Scale

Useful when standardised self-report responses are needed.

Possible item types: rating scales, Likert-type items, closed questions, appropriate open questions where relevant.

Interview Schedule

Useful when participants need to answer planned questions verbally.

May support: structured interview, semi-structured interview, focus group.

Observation Checklist

Useful when defined observable behaviours need to be recorded.

Consider: operational definitions, recording system, whether behaviours can be reliably identified.

Other Appropriate Tool

Only where appropriate to the selected method and aim. The tool must capture the relevant behaviour or outcome.

The data collection tool must align with the research method from Criterion B. If your method is an observation, you need a tool suited to observation. If your method is an interview, you need an interview schedule or similar tool.
11

Does the tool match the method?

For each scenario, decide: is the proposed tool a good fit or a mismatch?

“Method: semi-structured interview. Tool: an interview schedule with core questions and prompts.”
GOOD FIT. An interview schedule is the natural tool for an interview method — it provides planned questions and prompts.
“Method: naturalistic observation. Tool: an observation checklist with operationalized behavioural categories.”
GOOD FIT. An observation checklist with defined categories is the appropriate tool for recording observed behaviours.
“Method: survey/questionnaire. Tool: an online questionnaire with Likert-scale items about social media habits.”
GOOD FIT. A questionnaire with defined items is the standard tool for a survey method.
“Method: interview. Tool: only an observation checklist.”
MISMATCH. An interview method requires a tool that captures verbal responses — an observation checklist records behaviours, not spoken answers.
“Method: controlled observation. Tool: a single open-ended question asking participants to describe their behaviour.”
MISMATCH. A controlled observation requires defined behavioural categories to record, not a self-report question. The tool must match what the method actually captures.
“Method: experiment. Tool: a questionnaire measuring happiness.”
POTENTIAL MISMATCH. An experiment typically requires a tool that captures the outcome variable under controlled conditions. A happiness questionnaire may not match the experimental aim — the tool must measure what the experiment actually tests.
12

Tool design by method

Different methods require different tool-design considerations. Focus on what matters for your proposal.

Questionnaire / Survey

Consider: wording, response format, scale design, whether items actually measure the intended construct, balance/coverage, possible response bias.

Structured Interview

Consider: at least five items, standardised wording, logical question sequence, clarity, whether questions address the aim.

Semi-Structured Interview

Consider: core interview questions, useful prompts/probes, flexibility, avoiding leading questions, how responses address the aim.

Focus Group

Consider: discussion questions, prompts, question order, group dynamics, possibility of conformity or social desirability.

Controlled Observation

Consider: at least five items/categories, operational definitions, recording system, whether behaviours can be reliably identified.

Naturalistic Observation

Consider: observable behavioural categories, operational definitions, practical recording approach.

Experiment

Consider: the tool must capture the relevant behaviour/outcome. This may involve a measurement tool, recording sheet, rating measure, task outcome measure, or other suitable tool.

Do NOT force every student through irrelevant information. A student doing a questionnaire does not need observation guidance. Focus on the tool design that matches your method.
13

Good item or bad item?

For each item, question or observation category, decide: is it useful or does it need work?

Questionnaire item: “Do you feel stressed?”
NEEDS WORK. This is vague — stressed about what? How often? A double-barrelled or undefined question. What does “stressed” mean in this context?
Questionnaire item: “How often do you feel overwhelmed by academic workload?” (1 = Never, 5 = Very often)
USEFUL. Specific construct (academic workload), defined response format, clear wording.
Interview question: “Don’t you think social media is bad for young people?”
NEEDS WORK. This is a leading question — it suggests the answer the researcher expects. Participants may give socially desirable responses.
Interview question: “How would you describe your experience of using social media for school-related purposes?”
USEFUL. Open, non-leading, relevant to the aim, invites detailed response.
Observation category: “Student appears happy.”
NEEDS WORK. “Appears happy” is not observable — it requires interpretation. What specific behaviour would you record? Smiling? Laughing? Engaging with peers?
Observation category: “Frequency of initiating conversation with a peer during break time.”
USEFUL. Observable behaviour, defined context, countable.
Questionnaire item: “Do you agree that studying is important and that you try hard?”
NEEDS WORK. This is a double-barrelled question — it asks about two things (studying importance AND trying hard) in one item. Participants may agree with one part but not the other.
Observation category: “Number of times a student checks their phone during a 30-minute study period.”
USEFUL. Clearly defined, observable, countable, with a specified time frame.
14

One item ≠ a whole construct

Complex constructs may need several items or indicators to capture them effectively. A single question may not adequately represent a broad construct such as stress, trust, motivation, or social connectedness.

Think about coverage: do the items collectively capture important parts of what is being measured?

Do NOT invent a fixed number of items beyond the official five-item rule where it applies. The point is thoughtful coverage, not a magic number.
15

Does this tool cover the construct?

A fictional construct: social connectedness among students who recently moved schools. Which set of questionnaire items provides stronger coverage?

Set A: “Do you feel connected to other students?”
Set B: “How often do you feel close to classmates?” • “How included do you feel in group activities?” • “How supported do you feel by peers when you need help?” • “How often do you eat lunch with classmates?” • “How comfortable do you feel joining class discussions?”
SET B. Set A is a single broad question. Set B covers multiple dimensions of social connectedness: closeness, inclusion, support, shared activities, and participation. This provides much stronger coverage of the construct.
Consider: redundancy (are items repeated?), missing dimensions (is something important left out?), irrelevant items (does anything not serve the construct?), and appropriate breadth (is the coverage broad enough without being unfocused?).
16

How will participants respond?

Response format should fit what the researcher is trying to measure. Do NOT imply Likert scales are always best.

Likert-type responses

Participants rate agreement or frequency on a defined scale. Useful for measuring attitudes, perceptions, self-reported behaviours.

Frequency scales

Participants indicate how often something occurs. Useful for measuring behaviour patterns.

Categorical choices

Participants select from defined categories. Useful for demographic data or binary choices.

Open responses

Participants answer in their own words. Useful for rich qualitative data, but harder to compare across participants.

Behavioural counts / presence

The researcher records how often something happens or whether it occurs. Useful for observation data.

17

Which response format makes the most sense?

For each scenario, choose the most appropriate response format.

“Measuring how strongly students agree that school belonging is important to them.”
LIKERT-TYPE SCALE. Agreement strength is best captured on a rating scale, not a binary choice (which loses nuance) or an open response (which is harder to compare).
“Recording how often students check their phones during silent study.”
BEHAVIOURAL COUNT. The aim measures frequency of an observable behaviour. A count directly captures this; a Likert scale would rely on self-report estimates.
“Understanding students’ personal experiences of transition to a new school.”
OPEN-ENDED VERBAL RESPONSES. The aim seeks depth of personal experience. Open interview questions allow participants to describe their experiences in their own words.
“Measuring how often students experience specific symptoms of academic stress.”
FREQUENCY SCALE. The aim measures how often something occurs. A frequency scale (Never / Occasionally / Sometimes / Often / Always) directly captures this.
“Recording whether a student initiates conversation with a peer during a break period.”
PRESENCE/ABSENCE RECORDING. The aim measures whether a behaviour occurs. A binary record (yes/no) is the most direct and reliable method.

Don’t just show the tool — explain why you built it this way

Criterion C requires students to explain decisions made when creating the tool.

Design decision What did you choose to do?
↓
Why I made it What was the reasoning?
↓
How it helps measure what I intend What does this decision achieve?

Examples of decisions could include:

Operationalizing a construct

Why did you translate this abstract idea into these specific indicators?

Choosing a question type

Why did you choose closed vs open, or Likert vs frequency?

Including multiple indicators

Why did you use several items rather than one question?

Using prompts in an interview

Why did you include these prompts? How would they help explore the aim?

Adapting an existing measure

Why did you draw on this existing tool? How does it serve your aim?

Do NOT tell students they must make every one of these decisions. The point is to explain the decisions that matter for your specific tool and aim.
19

Description or explanation?

For each statement, decide: is the decision described or explained?

“I used a five-point Likert-type scale.”
DESCRIBED. This tells us what was used but not why. There is no reasoning or connection to the aim.
“A five-point scale was used because the aim compares responses across two conditions, and a defined numeric scale allows the researcher to calculate mean scores for each condition.”
EXPLAINED. This connects the scale choice to the aim (comparison across conditions) and explains why it helps (allows mean calculation).
“Open-ended questions were included.”
DESCRIBED. This states a fact about the tool but gives no reasoning. Why open-ended questions? How do they serve the aim?
“Open-ended questions were included because the aim explores personal experiences of transition, and participants may describe aspects the researcher has not anticipated.”
EXPLAINED. This connects the question type to the aim (exploring personal experiences) and explains the methodological advantage (captures unanticipated aspects).
“Five behavioural categories were defined for the observation.”
DESCRIBED. This states what was done but not why these five categories or how they relate to the aim.
“Five behavioural categories were defined because the aim measures social interaction during break time, and these categories capture the key observable behaviours that represent social engagement.”
EXPLAINED. This connects the categories to the aim (social interaction) and explains why these specific behaviours were chosen (they represent social engagement).
20

Does it actually measure what you think?

A tool can look professional while failing to measure the intended construct effectively. Ask yourself: If someone scores high / answers yes / is observed doing this, what can I actually conclude that the tool has measured?

Measurement validity check

This is about whether the tool genuinely captures what you claim it measures — not just whether it looks well-designed.

Measures it or misses it?

Questionnaire item: “How often do you feel close to your classmates?” (1 = Never, 5 = Very often) — measuring social connectedness.
MEASURES IT. This directly asks about a key dimension of social connectedness (feeling close to peers) with a defined response format.
Questionnaire item: “Do you like school?” — measuring social connectedness.
MISSES IT. Liking school is not the same as feeling connected to classmates. This item measures a different construct.
Observation category: “Number of times a student initiates conversation with a peer during break” — measuring social interaction.
MEASURES IT. Initiating conversation is a direct observable indicator of social interaction.
Observation category: “Student seems friendly” — measuring social interaction.
MISSES IT. “Seems friendly” is subjective interpretation, not an observable behaviour. The observer cannot reliably record this.
Interview question: “Can you describe a specific moment when you felt included in a class activity?” — measuring belonging.
MEASURES IT. This asks for a concrete example of belonging, directly relevant to the construct.
Questionnaire item: “Do you enjoy psychology lessons?” — measuring academic motivation.
MISSES IT. Enjoying one subject is not the same as general academic motivation. This item is too narrow and may not capture the intended construct.
21

Existing measures / materials

IB guidance allows students to discuss links to existing measures or materials where relevant. If students adapt or draw on an existing measure, they should explain the decision and how it relates to what they intend to measure.

Do NOT imply students must use an existing standardised measure. Do NOT tell them inventing a new tool is automatically better. Do NOT fabricate real measures or citations.

If adapting an existing measure

Explain: which measure, why it is relevant, what changes you made, and how the adapted version serves your aim.

If creating a new tool

Explain: why an existing measure did not fit, or why creating a bespoke tool was more appropriate for your aim.

Why did a real example reach 6/6?

The official high-scoring Criterion C demonstrated several methodological strengths. Here are the general lessons — not the student’s exact wording.

Measured both variables

The tool captured data relevant to both variables in the proposal, not just one.

Explained operationalization

The student explained how abstract constructs were translated into measurable behaviours/items.

Explained scoring approach

The student explained how responses would be scored or recorded, not just what the items were.

Addressed participant bias

The student discussed design decisions intended to reduce participant bias, connecting these to features of the tool.

Discussed relevant challenges

Challenges such as social desirability and acquiescence bias were discussed specifically in relation to the questionnaire design.

These are methodological lessons, not templates to copy. Apply the principles to your own tool and aim.

24

Relevant or generic challenge?

For each scenario, decide: is this challenge relevant to this proposal or generic / not particularly relevant?

“Order effects in a repeated-measures experiment comparing two revision methods.”
RELEVANT. In a repeated-measures design, the same participants do both conditions. Order in which conditions are experienced could affect results.
“Social desirability in a questionnaire about sensitive personal experiences.”
RELEVANT. Sensitive topics increase the chance participants give socially desirable rather than honest responses.
“Interviewer influence in a semi-structured interview about personal experiences.”
RELEVANT. In an interview, the researcher’s tone, wording and probing can influence how participants respond.
“Observer interpretation in an observation of social interaction.”
RELEVANT. Observing social behaviour involves interpretation. If behavioural categories are ambiguous, different observers may record differently.
“Practice effects where participants only perform one task once.”
NOT PARTICULARLY RELEVANT. Practice effects require repeated exposure to a task. If participants do the task once, this challenge does not apply.
“Fatigue in a very short 5-item questionnaire.”
NOT PARTICULARLY RELEVANT. Fatigue is unlikely in a very short questionnaire. This challenge is more relevant to lengthy surveys.
“Participant variability in an independent-measures experiment with two different classes.”
RELEVANT. Two different classes may differ in ability, motivation or other variables. This is a genuine threat to internal validity.
“Researcher bias in a questionnaire study where the researcher does not interact with participants during data collection.”
NOT PARTICULARLY RELEVANT. If the questionnaire is anonymous and self-administered, the researcher has limited opportunity to influence responses during collection.
25

Identifying ≠ explaining

A challenge is identified when you name it. It is explained when you show why it could occur and how it could affect the data.

Weak

“Social desirability bias may occur.”

≠

Stronger thinking

WHY might social desirability occur here? WHICH responses might it affect? HOW could that distort the data?

Challenge Name the issue
↓
Why it could occur Connect to your proposal
↓
How it could affect the data Explain the impact on findings

Identified or explained?

“Response bias could be an issue.”
IDENTIFIED ONLY. This names the challenge but says nothing about why it could happen or how it could affect the data.
“Response bias could occur because participants may answer in a way they think is socially acceptable rather than truthful. This could inflate agreement with positive statements and reduce the validity of the findings.”
EXPLAINED. This explains WHY (social acceptability) and HOW (inflate agreement, reduce validity) — connecting the challenge to the data.
“Observer bias could affect the data.”
IDENTIFIED ONLY. This names the issue but does not explain how observer expectations could influence recording or which data could be affected.
“Observer bias could occur because the researcher expects students who moved schools to show less social interaction. This could lead the observer to interpret ambiguous behaviours as less social, affecting the accuracy of the recorded data.”
EXPLAINED. This explains the source of bias (researcher expectation), how it could influence recording (interpret ambiguous behaviours), and the effect on data (accuracy).
“Order effects might be a problem.”
IDENTIFIED ONLY. This names the challenge but does not explain why order effects could occur in this specific design or how they could affect results.
“Order effects could occur because participants completing the music condition first may become fatigued by the time they do the silence condition, potentially reducing concentration scores in the second condition and creating a false difference between conditions.”
EXPLAINED. This explains the mechanism (fatigue from first condition), the direction of effect (reduce concentration in second), and the consequence (false difference).
26

Spot the response-bias risk

Where relevant, certain tool-design features can create response-bias risks. Identify the risk in each example.

Questionnaire item: “I always treat everyone fairly, regardless of their background.”
SOCIAL DESIRABILITY. This item describes a socially desirable behaviour. Participants may agree because it sounds like the “right” answer, not because it is true.
Questionnaire: All items are positively worded and use agree/disagree scales.
ACQUIESCENCE TENDENCY. When all items are worded in the same direction, participants who tend to agree may score high on everything, regardless of their actual views.
Questionnaire item: “How many hours did you sleep last night?” (0–12 scale)
NO PARTICULAR RISK. This is a factual recall question with a numeric scale. There is little social pressure to answer in a particular way.
Interview question: “How often do you help classmates with their schoolwork?”
SOCIAL DESIRABILITY. Helping behaviour is socially valued. Participants may over-report how often they help to present themselves favourably.
Observation category: “Number of times a student picks up their phone during study time.”
NO PARTICULAR RISK. This is an observed behavioural count. Participants may not be aware of what is being recorded, and the observer records what actually happens.
27

Researcher bias in data collection

Criterion C is concerned with potential challenges affecting data collection. Keep the focus on: How could researcher influence affect the collection or quality of the data?

Interviewer wording / tone

How the researcher phrases questions or responds to answers can influence what participants say.

Inconsistent probing

If follow-up questions differ across participants, the data collected may not be comparable.

Observer expectations

What the researcher expects to find may influence how they interpret or record behaviour.

Ambiguous categories

If behavioural categories are not clearly defined, the researcher’s interpretation becomes a source of bias.

28

Method-specific challenge paths

These are possibilities, not required lists. Choose challenges that apply to your proposal.

Experiment

Possible relevant challenges: participant variability, order/practice effects, fatigue, controlling variables, validity of outcome measurement.

Interview

Possible relevant challenges: interviewer influence, response bias, social desirability, question interpretation, consistency of probing.

Observation

Possible relevant challenges: observer bias, unclear operational definitions, reactivity where relevant, recording consistency.

Questionnaire / Survey

Possible relevant challenges: social desirability, acquiescence, unclear items, fatigue/boredom, validity of the measurement tool.

29

Which challenges should you discuss?

A fictional proposal: “To compare concentration during lessons with and without a two-minute mindfulness break, using a controlled observation with two different classes.”

Which 2–3 challenges are most relevant to this proposal?

“Participant variability — the two classes may differ in baseline concentration levels.”
MOST RELEVANT. Using two different classes means pre-existing differences could affect results. This is a key challenge for this design.
“Observer bias — the researcher may interpret concentration behaviours differently across conditions.”
MOST RELEVANT. In a controlled observation, the researcher records behaviour. If they know which condition is which, expectations could influence recording.
“Social desirability — students may try to look more concentrated.”
LESS RELEVANT. While possible, students may not know what is being measured. This is less central than participant variability or observer bias.
“Order effects — doing both conditions may cause fatigue.”
LESS RELEVANT. This is an independent-measures design (two different classes), not repeated measures. Order effects are not applicable.
Relevance matters more than listing every methodological limitation you remember. Choose the 2–3 challenges most directly connected to your specific tool and procedure.

Find the mismatch

Each mini-scenario contains one Criterion C mismatch. Identify it.

“Aim: measuring social connectedness. Tool: a questionnaire about academic motivation.”
MISMATCH. The tool measures academic motivation, but the aim is about social connectedness. The tool must capture the variables the aim requires.
“A controlled observation about social interaction. Tool: a checklist with only 3 behavioural categories.”
MISMATCH. A controlled observation checklist must contain at least five items. Three items does not meet the five-item minimum.
“A one-time questionnaire. Discussion: order effects could affect the data.”
MISMATCH. Order effects require multiple conditions or tasks experienced in sequence. A one-time questionnaire has no ordering issue.
“Tool: a Likert scale measuring stress. Design decision: ‘I used a 5-point scale.’ No further explanation.”
MISMATCH. The student stated what they did but did not explain why. Criterion C requires explanations, not just descriptions.
“A questionnaire about social media habits. Item: ‘What is your favourite colour?’”
MISMATCH. Favourite colour has no connection to social media habits. Every item should serve the aim.

Worked thinking example

ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE — NOT A SOURCE TO CITE.

Fictional proposal

Aim/RQ: To investigate whether hours of social media use per day are associated with self-reported sleep quality among students aged 15–17.

What needs to be measured? Two variables: social media hours and sleep quality

Fictional Criterion C plan

Aim/RQ: To investigate whether background music affects concentration during a timed task.

Tool: A questionnaire asking participants “Do you like music?” and “Are you good at concentrating?”

Design decision: “I used a questionnaire because it is easy to distribute.”

Challenge: “Response bias may occur.”

How many weaknesses can you identify?
SEVERAL WEAKNESSES.
(1) The tool does not measure concentration — it asks about liking music and self-rated concentration, which are unrelated to the aim.
(2) The items are vague and do not operationalize concentration.
(3) The design decision is described (questionnaire because easy) but not explained in relation to the aim.
(4) “Response bias” is identified but not explained — why could it occur here, and how could it affect the data?

Common mistakes that cost marks

Tool does not match the aim

Creating a questionnaire about a topic unrelated to the RQ, or using an observation when the aim requires self-report data.

Vague operationalization

“Measure stress” without translating it into specific items or indicators.

Items unrelated to the construct

Questionnaire or interview items that do not address the target variable.

Unobservable categories

Observation categories that require interpretation rather than recording defined behaviours.

Missing five-item minimum

Failing to meet the minimum where it applies (questionnaire, structured interview, controlled observation).

Tool not in appendix

Forgetting to include a copy of the data collection tool in the appendix.

Showing without explaining

Presenting the tool but not explaining why it was designed that way.

Describing not explaining

“I used a Likert scale” without explaining why it fits the aim.

Assuming professional = valid

A tool can look well-designed while failing to measure the intended construct.

Generic challenges

Naming bias without connecting it to the specific proposal or explaining how it could affect the data.

Every limitation listed

Discussing every possible limitation instead of the most relevant challenges.

Past tense writing

Writing as though data have already been collected rather than proposing a study.
