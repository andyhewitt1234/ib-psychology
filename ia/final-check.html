The IA is a research proposal

This is NOT Criterion E. It is NOT assessed separately. It is a final digital coach you use AFTER completing Criteria A–D.

What you do

You design a research proposal. You write as though the investigation could be conducted.

What you do NOT do

You do not conduct the investigation. You do not have real data, real results, or real findings.

Maximum length

2,200 words for the whole proposal

Total marks

24 marks

Criterion A — Introduction

6 marks

Criterion B — Research Methodology

6 marks

Criterion C — Data Collection

6 marks

Criterion D — Discussion

6 marks

A strong IA is not four unrelated sections

Every methodological decision should connect to the same proposed investigation.

Aim / RQ What are you investigating?
↓
Method + Procedure How would you investigate it?
↓
What is measured What data would you collect?
↓
Data collection tool How would you measure it?
↓
Potential findings What could the data show?
↓
Implications / Bias / Additional method What could it mean? What could affect it? What else could add understanding?
Follow your investigation through the IA. If the investigation changes between sections, something has gone wrong.
2

Before you check anything

Section quality ≠ whole-proposal coherence

You could have four individually detailed sections that still contradict one another.

Example of a broken proposal
  • Criterion A: RQ asks about the relationship between sleep and stress.
  • Criterion B: Student proposes an experiment manipulating sleep.
  • Criterion C: Questionnaire only measures stress.
  • Criterion D: Student claims the experiment proves social media causes stress.

This proposal has lost alignment several times.

Where did the IA break?

For each scenario, identify which transition caused the mismatch.

A: RQ about relationship between exercise and mood. B: Questionnaire about exercise habits and mood. C: Tool only asks about exercise frequency. D: Discusses mood findings.
B → C. The method (questionnaire) fits the RQ, but the tool does not measure mood. The RQ requires data on BOTH variables.
A: RQ about whether video games affect attention. B: Experiment with attention tasks. C: Tool measures attention correctly. D: “The experiment proves video games cause attention deficits.”
C → D. The tool measures attention, but the RQ is about whether video games affect attention. The tool does not measure video game use. Also, D makes a causal claim that goes beyond what the method can establish.
A: RQ about student wellbeing. B: Experiment manipulating wellbeing through an intervention. C: Wellbeing questionnaire. D: Discusses potential wellbeing patterns.
A → B. The RQ asks about wellbeing (implying exploration), but the method proposes an experiment (implying manipulation). These do not align.
A: RQ about social media use and self-esteem. B: Semi-structured interview. C: Questions ask only about social media frequency, not self-esteem. D: Discusses self-esteem patterns.
B → C. The interview could explore both variables, but the questions only measure social media frequency. Self-esteem is never actually measured.

Criterion A — Introduction

Criterion A Final Audit 6 marks
Common A problems
  • topic instead of focused aim/RQ
  • population too broad
  • impact merely mentioned rather than explained
  • research summarized without linking it to the proposal
  • one research piece developed while the second is merely mentioned
  • causal wording that does not match the intended method

Ready or not?

For each mini summary, decide: is there an obvious problem?

Aim: Investigate the relationship between sleep duration and academic stress in IB Diploma students. Research: Two studies on sleep–stress links in adolescents, both linked to the proposed investigation.
READY. Focused RQ, identifiable population, two relevant research pieces linked to the proposal.
Aim: Investigate the effect of social media on teenagers. Research: One study on Instagram and body image, mentioned briefly. Second study not clearly linked to the investigation.
HAS A PROBLEM. RQ is too broad (“teenagers”, “social media”). Second research piece is not clearly linked.
Aim: Explore how Year 10 students experience the transition to IB Diploma. Research: Two qualitative studies on school transition, findings and links clearly explained.
READY. Focused exploration, specific population, two relevant studies linked to the proposal.
Aim: Do exercise and mood relate? Research: One study on exercise and depression, one study on mindfulness and anxiety. Neither clearly connected to the proposed investigation.
HAS A PROBLEM. RQ is vague. Research pieces are not clearly linked to what the student proposes to investigate.
4

Criterion B — Research Methodology

Criterion B Final Audit 6 marks
Do NOT invent a universal IA sample-size requirement. Do NOT import class-practical minimum sample sizes.

Method / procedure mismatch

For each scenario, decide whether the chain actually aligns.

Aim: Explore student experiences of remote learning. Method: Semi-structured interview. Sample: 8 Year 11 students who studied remotely for 6+ months. Procedure: Interviews ask about experiences, challenges, adaptations.
ALIGNED. The method fits the exploratory aim. The sample matches the population. The procedure collects relevant data.
Aim: Investigate whether music affects exam performance. Method: Survey. Sample: 30 students. Procedure: Students report their exam scores and whether they listen to music while studying.
BROKEN. The RQ implies causation (“affects”) but the method (survey) can only show correlation. The aim should use relational language, or the method should be an experiment.
Aim: Compare social interaction in indoor vs outdoor recess. Method: Structured observation. Sample: 20 students. Procedure: Students complete a questionnaire about their recess habits.
BROKEN. The method says observation but the procedure describes a questionnaire. Method and procedure do not match.
Common B problems
  • method named but not justified
  • vague recruitment
  • vague sample
  • missing practical detail in procedure
  • experimental design inconsistent with procedure
  • generic ethics list not connected to this proposal
  • ethical safeguards imported from a different kind of study
  • procedure describes something different from the stated method

Criterion C — Data Collection

Criterion C Final Audit 6 marks
The five-item rule applies ONLY to:
  • survey / questionnaire
  • structured interview
  • controlled observation

Do NOT state that every possible data collection tool requires five items.

Measure it or miss it

For each scenario, does the tool actually measure what the aim requires?

Aim: Investigate the relationship between academic stress and sleep quality. Tool: Five questions measuring perceived stress AND five questions measuring sleep quality.
ALIGNED. The tool measures both constructs. The data could support a relational analysis.
Aim: Investigate the relationship between academic stress and sleep quality. Tool: Five questions only measuring academic stress.
MISALIGNED. The tool only measures one construct. The proposal needs data representing BOTH constructs if it intends to investigate their relationship.
Aim: Explore students' experiences of remote learning. Tool: A checklist counting how many hours students spend online each day.
MISALIGNED. The aim asks about experiences (qualitative), but the tool measures time (quantitative). The tool does not address the aim.

Operationalized or still vague?

For each pair, which version is actually observable/measurable?

“Overeating” vs “Eating more than one standard serving in a single sitting, as defined by the food packaging”
SPECIFIC & MEASURABLE. The second version gives a clear, observable definition that could be consistently applied.
“Social skills” vs “Number of times a student initiates a conversation with a peer during a 20-minute break period”
VAGUE. While the second version is better, “social skills” is still broad. The specific version describes a particular behaviour, not the full construct. Consider whether one behaviour captures the construct sufficiently.
“Academic performance” vs “Average grade across three most recent mathematics tests”
SPECIFIC & MEASURABLE. Clearly defined, observable, and consistently applicable.
Remember: A copy of the data collection tool belongs in the appendix. You are NOT required to provide completed consent forms or standardized instructions as appendix materials.
Common C problems
  • vague operationalization
  • questionnaire items unrelated to construct
  • leading questions
  • double-barrelled questions
  • ambiguous observation categories
  • inferred mental states presented as directly observed behaviour
  • challenge merely named
  • generic limitation pasted into the section
  • data tool placed in appendix but never methodologically explained

Criterion D — Discussion

Criterion D Final Audit 6 marks
The three things you cannot forget
  1. Potential findings → implications for policy/practice
  2. Researcher bias → how it could affect this investigation
  3. Additional method → how it could increase understanding

Part 1 — Potential findings + implications

Part 2 — Researcher bias

Do not let this become a generic list of biases. Every bias discussed must connect to your specific proposed investigation.

Part 3 — One additional method

Which part is missing?

For each mini Discussion example, which requirement is missing or insufficient?

“One potential finding is that students who moved schools report feeling less connected. Researcher bias could enter because I expect negative responses. An observation could add directly observed social behaviour.”
IMPLICATIONS MISSING. Potential findings are discussed, bias is discussed, additional method is discussed, but no implications for policy or practice are mentioned.
“The questionnaire could show a relationship between stress and sleep. Schools could review sleep guidance. An interview could add depth.”
BIAS DISCUSSION MISSING. Findings, implications, and additional method are present, but there is no discussion of researcher bias.
“Observation could show social interaction patterns. Schools could create peer programmes. Researcher expectations could influence coding.”
ADDITIONAL METHOD MISSING. Findings, implications, and bias are discussed, but no additional method is proposed.

The whole-IA alignment test

Does your investigation stay the same from A to D?

Criterion A Aim / RQ
↓
Criterion B Method + Sample + Procedure
↓
Criterion C Operationalization + Data Collection Tool
↓
Criterion D Potential Findings + Implications

Follow the investigation

For each scenario, decide: aligned or broken? If broken, identify where the break occurs.

A: Relationship between exercise and mood. B: Questionnaire about exercise habits and mood. C: Tool measures both variables. D: Relational potential finding.
ALIGNED. The RQ, method, tool, and discussion all address the same relationship between the same two variables.
A: Does social media cause anxiety? B: Survey about social media use and anxiety. C: Tool measures both. D: “Social media causes anxiety.”
BROKEN. The RQ implies causation but the method (survey) can only show correlation. The D section makes a causal claim that the method cannot support.
A: Explore experiences of school transition. B: Semi-structured interviews with 8 students. C: Interview questions about transition experiences. D: Potential themes and school practice implications.
ALIGNED. Exploratory aim, appropriate qualitative method, matching tool, potential findings consistent with the data type.
A: Compare playground behaviour in different weather. B: Structured observation. C: Observation checklist records behaviour. D: Additional method is another observation.
BROKEN. The additional method should be genuinely additional. A second observation of the same type does not add a different kind of understanding.
A: Investigate the effect of background noise on concentration. B: Experiment with noise conditions. C: Tool measures concentration using a memory test. D: “The experiment proves noise reduces concentration.”
BROKEN. The tool measures concentration, but the causal claim in D goes beyond what a single study with one sample can “prove.”
A: Investigate relationship between screen time and sleep. B: Questionnaire. C: Tool only measures screen time. D: Discusses sleep findings.
BROKEN. The tool only measures screen time. The RQ requires data on BOTH variables to investigate a relationship.
8

Language red-flag check

Watch your claims

Check whether your language exceeds what the proposed method can establish.

Supported or overclaimed?

Survey: “Higher stress was associated with poorer sleep.”
SUPPORTED. Relational language is appropriate for a survey. “Associated with” does not imply causation.
Survey: “Stress caused poorer sleep.”
OVERCLAIMED. A survey cannot establish causation. Use relational language.
Interview: “Participants may describe feeling less connected.”
SUPPORTED. This is appropriate potential qualitative language. Interviews can capture how participants describe experiences.
Interview: “The interview proves loneliness causes depression.”
OVERCLAIMED. An interview cannot “prove” causation. This goes far beyond what qualitative data can establish.
Observation: “Students may display more off-task behaviour during unstructured time.”
SUPPORTED. Observations can identify behavioural patterns. “May display” is appropriately tentative.
Observation: “Students behave this way because they lack motivation.”
OVERCLAIMED. Observations capture behaviour, not motivation. Inferring internal states from observation alone is an unsupported inference.
Experiment: “The proposed experiment could show whether background noise affects concentration.”
SUPPORTED. A well-designed experiment with appropriate controls could investigate causation. The language is appropriately tentative (“could show”).
Do not teach students that every experiment automatically establishes causality. Causal interpretation depends on the quality of the design, controls, and procedure.
9

Proposal, not completed study

Proposal language or fake results?

For each statement, decide: is this appropriate proposal language, or does it incorrectly imply the study was conducted?

“We predict that students who moved schools will report lower social connectedness.”
PROPOSAL LANGUAGE. “Predict” is appropriate for a proposal. It describes what the investigation could find.
“The results showed that students who moved schools scored lower on social connectedness.”
FAKE RESULTS. “The results showed” implies the study was actually conducted. In a proposal, use “could show” or “may reveal.”
“One possible pattern is that students using flashcards perform better on vocabulary tests.”
PROPOSAL LANGUAGE. “One possible pattern” appropriately frames this as a potential finding.
“Participants scored an average of 7.2 on the wellbeing scale.”
FAKE RESULTS. This presents specific numerical data as though the study was conducted. A proposal should discuss potential patterns, not invented scores.
“The proposed investigation could find that background noise reduces concentration.”
PROPOSAL LANGUAGE. “Could find” appropriately frames this as a potential outcome.
“Our data demonstrated that exercise improves mood.”
FAKE RESULTS. “Our data demonstrated” implies the study was conducted and data was collected.
Students design the investigation. They do NOT conduct it. Write as though the investigation could be conducted, not as though it has been conducted.

Word count check

Maximum: 2,200 words for the whole proposal.

Do NOT invent criterion-specific word limits. Do NOT prescribe mandatory paragraph counts. Students need to use the available word count strategically across all four assessed sections.

This is a maximum, not a target. A well-written proposal does not need to reach 2,200 words to earn full marks. Quality and coherence matter more than length.

Appendix check
