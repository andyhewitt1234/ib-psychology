Criterion A 6 marks

Criterion A — Introduction

This is the first assessed section of the IA. Your introduction establishes the context and rationale for the proposal: the real-life problem, the population it affects, the two pieces of relevant research, and a clearly stated and focused aim or research question — worth 6 marks.

1 What IB Requires — Criterion A 6 marks

The strongest descriptor (5–6) describes an introduction that:

  • has a clearly stated and focused aim or research question,
  • describes the real-life problem,
  • explains the impact of that problem on the population of interest,
  • includes two pieces of relevant research, and
  • explains and links both pieces to the proposed investigation.
The research component is a brief literature review. You are expected to explain the relevant findings and key conclusions of your two pieces of research. Procedural details of previous research are not required unless those details are directly relevant to your proposed investigation.
Aim or research question? The guidance that explains the structure refers to an aim. The Criterion A assessment descriptor, however, accepts an aim or a research question. You are not required to use one particular format — whichever you use must be clearly stated and focused.
2

What changes as the marks go up?

Do not memorise rubric paragraphs — understand the climb. Each level moves you from stating towards describing, then explaining and finally linking.

State Describe Explain Link
1–2 Lower level
  • Aim/RQ unclear or insufficiently focused.
  • Real-life problem merely stated.
  • Research missing, unclear, insufficiently relevant, or inadequately connected.
3–4 Middle level
  • Aim/RQ clearer but may only be partially focused.
  • Problem described, but the impact on the population may not be adequately addressed.
  • Two pieces of research may be present, but the explanation and linking are incomplete.
5–6 Strongest level
  • Aim/RQ clearly stated AND focused.
  • Problem DESCRIBED.
  • Impact on the population EXPLAINED.
  • TWO pieces of relevant research EXPLAINED AND LINKED to the proposal.
The workload does not change — the quality does. The strongest descriptor does not ask you to write more; it asks you to describe instead of state, to explain instead of describe, and to link each piece of research to your proposal.

4

The real-life problem

A topic is not automatically a real-life problem. A topic names a subject; a real-life problem names a difficulty that actually matters to real people and gives your investigation a reason to exist.

Topic

Sleep

≠

Real-life problem direction

Insufficient sleep among a defined student population may create difficulties with concentration and learning.

Topic or real-life problem?

For each statement below, click the correct classification. Instant feedback explains why.

“Sleep”
TOPIC ONLY. “Sleep” is a subject of study, not a problem. A problem would say whose sleep, what about it, and why it matters.
“Insufficient sleep among a defined senior-secondary student population may create difficulties with concentration and learning.”
REAL-LIFE PROBLEM DIRECTION. A defined population, a named difficulty, and a sense of why it matters — it gives a proposal a reason to exist.
“Music”
TOPIC ONLY. “Music” alone investigates nothing. A problem might be: students listening to music while revising report recalling less the next day.
“Students at our school report difficulty concentrating during lessons after using phones late at night.”
REAL-LIFE PROBLEM DIRECTION. A behaviour (late-night phone use), a population (students at our school) and an outcome (difficulty concentrating) are all named.
“Stress”
TOPIC ONLY. “Stress” is a domain. A problem would name what kind of stress, in whom, and with what effect.
“Students who recently moved schools report feeling left out in their new class.”
REAL-LIFE PROBLEM DIRECTION. A specific situation (moving schools), a specific population and a specific difficulty (feeling left out) that a psychologist could investigate.
5

The population of interest

The population of interest is the group sharing relevant characteristics about whom the researcher wants to develop understanding or draw conclusions/inferences. The problem being investigated must be relevant to that population. (This page reinforces your Plan Your IA work — it does not replace it.)

Think: WHO is affected? WHY is this population relevant to this problem? If you cannot answer both, the population is not doing its job.

Clear enough?

Judge each description: too broad, focused enough, or does not match the problem (for the problems used on this page: school stress, social media and self-esteem, or revision and concentration).

“Teenagers”
TOO BROAD. “Teenagers” is an age band with no boundaries. Which teenagers, in what context, with what relevant behaviour?
“Female students aged 16–17 at our school who use Instagram most days”
FOCUSED ENOUGH. Identifiable age, context and an everyday behaviour that links directly to a social-media and self-esteem problem.
“Adults who drink coffee daily”
DOES NOT MATCH THE PROBLEM. Well-defined, but irrelevant to a school-stress or revision-concentration problem. Define the population by what connects it to your problem.
“Final-year high-school students preparing for university entrance examinations”
FOCUSED ENOUGH. A defined group facing a defined situation — an obvious home for an exam-stress or revision problem.
“All students in the world”
TOO BROAD. “All students in the world” cannot be identified or described. A population of interest needs boundaries you can explain.
“Students at our school who revise for at least two hours on exam-week evenings”
FOCUSED ENOUGH. A defined behaviour and context that connect directly to revision, concentration and sleep problems.
A note on “narrow”: there is no rule that a population must be extremely narrow. The IA needs a population that is clearly defined and logically connected to the problem — clarity and logical fit matter more than extremes of specificity.
6

Describing the problem is not the same as explaining its impact

PROBLEM ≠ IMPACT. Describing the problem is only half the job. You must also explain what the problem means for the population — its consequences — and why that makes the problem important to investigate.

Describes the problem

“Academic stress is common among DP students.”

≠

Explains the impact

What consequences could this problem have for the chosen population? Why does it matter to investigate?

Problem or impact?

Classify each statement as problem only, impact only, or problem + impact.

“Final-year students report feeling anxious several times a week.”
PROBLEM ONLY. The problem (anxiety) is named, but nothing is said about what it does to these students.
“As a result, some students avoid asking questions in class and fall behind on revision.”
IMPACT ONLY. This states a consequence, but on its own there is no problem attached. In your introduction the two need to sit together.
“Students who sleep fewer than 7 hours on weeknights report forgetting studied material, and this lowers their confidence before exams.”
PROBLEM + IMPACT. The problem (sleeping fewer than 7 hours) is stated and its consequences (forgetting material, lower confidence) are explained.
“Appearance-based social comparison is common among students who use image-heavy apps.”
PROBLEM ONLY. We learn the behaviour is common, but not what it does to the students involved.
“Frequent appearances-based comparison with edited images is associated with lower self-esteem among these students, and lower self-esteem is linked to less willingness to take part in class.”
PROBLEM + IMPACT. The comparison is named as the problem, and its consequences for self-esteem and class participation are explained.
“This matters because persistent comparison may reduce the confidence students need to speak up in class.”
IMPACT ONLY. A stated consequence (less confidence), which must be linked to the problem in your introduction — it cannot stand alone.

What is missing?

Each mini-example identifies a problem and a population, but the impact is usually not actually explained. Decide whether the impact has been explained or is still missing.

“Students at our school often find it hard to concentrate while studying.”
IMPACT MISSING. Problem and population are named, but not what poor concentration does to this group. What is the consequence — falling behind? avoiding study?
“Many students report sleeping fewer than 7 hours during exam weeks.”
IMPACT MISSING. We know the sleep pattern, but not what happens to these students because of it. Add the consequence — memory, concentration, mood.
“Students who moved schools mid-year say they feel they do not belong.”
IMPACT MISSING. The feeling is stated, but without a consequence. What does a weak sense of belonging mean for this group — participation, attendance, wellbeing?
“Appearance-based social comparison is frequent among students at our school who use Instagram daily.”
IMPACT MISSING. That the comparison happens is described — what it might do to self-esteem is not. Finish the thought in your introduction.
“Students who skip breakfast before morning tests report higher anxiety, and higher anxiety is linked to recalling less of what they studied the night before.”
IMPACT EXPLAINED. This one goes further — it names the consequence (worse recall) that follows from the problem (anxiety linked to skipping breakfast).
Do not copy this page. These are thinking models. Your introduction must explain the impact for the problem, population and research that you choose.
7

Learn from real IA marking — impact

From the assessed examples

An introduction can correctly identify a real problem and a population and still lose marks if it never explains what that problem actually means for the population. Naming the pieces is not the same as using them.

Which version is stronger?

For each pair, choose the version that better matches the strongest descriptor. Version B always has to justify its extra effort.

A: “Many students spend a lot of time on social media at night.”
B: “Many students spend a lot of time on social media at night, and students who do report poorer sleep and more difficulty concentrating in morning lessons.”
VERSION B. A describes behaviour; B adds what it means (poorer sleep, harder concentration) — an explained impact, matching the strongest descriptor.
A: “Appearance-based social comparison takes place on image-heavy apps.”
B: “Among students who use image-heavy apps daily, frequent appearance-based comparison is associated with lower self-esteem — which matters because self-esteem is linked to willingness to take part in class.”
VERSION B. A states a process; B connects it to the population and explains a real consequence. That is the difference between describing and explaining.
A: “Stress is common in final-year students.”
B: “Stress is common in final-year students, and those reporting high stress also report avoiding revision and feeling unprepared for exams.”
VERSION B. A merely notes stress; B names what it does to this population — the impact the strongest descriptor asks you to explain.
A: “Some students find it hard to belong after moving schools.”
B: “Students who moved schools mid-year often report feeling they do not belong, and a weak sense of belonging is associated with speaking less in class.”
VERSION B. A names a feeling; B links it to a tangible consequence (speaking less) — and that consequence gives the proposal its rationale.
The lesson

Before you move on, your introduction should already be able to answer two questions: what is the problem for this population? and why does it matter? If the second has no answer, the introduction is only half-written.

8

Two pieces of relevant research

Criterion A asks for two pieces of relevant research. You should read broadly around psychological investigations relevant to your real-life problem before deciding which two pieces are most useful. The final two should provide findings and key conclusions that genuinely help you explain or justify your proposed investigation.

Relevance

Does it speak to your real-life problem, your population or your variables?

Findings

Does it report clear findings you can describe in your introduction?

Key conclusions

Do its conclusions help you understand what is known — and what is not?

Connection to your proposal

Can you explain how it informs and justifies what you are proposing?

Do not invent extra requirements. IB does not explicitly require: peer-reviewed sources; empirical studies only; particular publication dates; or particular research designs. A relevant aspect of a relevant piece of research can qualify — focus on relevance, findings, key conclusions and the connection to your current proposal.

9

Relevant enough?

Sharing a broad topic does not automatically make research useful. A piece of research is relevant when it helps establish understanding relevant to the actual proposed investigation — not merely when it is in the same general area.

Imagine this proposed investigation: “To investigate whether appearance-based social comparison is associated with self-esteem among female students aged 16–17 at our school who use Instagram most days.” Classify each piece of research as strongly relevant, partially relevant, or weakly relevant.

A study measuring the association between appearance-based social comparison and self-esteem in adolescent girls who regularly use an image-sharing platform.
STRONGLY RELEVANT. Same variables (comparison, self-esteem), same behaviour (image-sharing) and a closely matched population — its findings and conclusions speak directly to your proposal.
A study of self-esteem in adolescents that does not measure social comparison at all.
PARTIALLY RELEVANT. It tells you about self-esteem in the right age group, but says nothing about the comparison that drives your proposal. It could support part of your rationale — not the whole one.
A study of how video-game practice improves reaction times in young adults.
WEAKLY RELEVANT. A well-designed study can still be the wrong study for your proposal. It does not touch your variables, population or problem.
An investigation of social-comparison theory as it applies to body satisfaction among female adolescents who use social media.
STRONGLY RELEVANT. Its key conclusions about the comparison process and the population give you the mechanism behind your proposed problem — excellent justification material.
Research on romantic-relationship satisfaction and communication styles in married couples.
WEAKLY RELEVANT. Different population, problem and variables. Even though it is psychological research about people, it cannot help you understand or justify this proposal.
A study of general social-media use and wellbeing in a broad sample of adults, without a focus on comparison or on your age group.
PARTIALLY RELEVANT. It shows a general social-media–wellbeing link, but not for your specific behaviour (comparison) or population. Useful background — not a central piece.
10

What should I actually explain about the research?

Criterion A is not primarily asking for a long description of how previous research was conducted. The focus should be on:

Relevant finding(s) + key conclusion + why they matter to the current proposal.

Procedural information should be included only when directly relevant — for example, a sample characteristic or a procedural feature that genuinely changes what the research means for your proposal.

Keep it or cut it?

You are writing up a hypothetical piece of research about memory and revision. Decide whether each detail is worth keeping (keep) or should probably be cut (cut) in Criterion A.

A relevant finding: “Students who revised with flashcards recalled more vocabulary words than students who re-read their notes.”
KEEP. This is the kind of relevant finding Criterion A is looking for — it says what the research actually showed.
A key conclusion: “The researchers concluded that active retrieval during revision supports later recall more than passive re-reading.”
KEEP. A key conclusion is exactly what you should explain — it carries the meaning from the findings forward into your rationale.
An irrelevant participant detail: “The 18 participants were recruited from a university psychology department between March and April.”
PROBABLY CUT. The recruitment window explains nothing about your proposal. Keep design detail only if it changes how you can use the research.
An irrelevant procedural sequence: “Participants completed the task on Tuesday afternoon in a classroom, then again on Friday.”
PROBABLY CUT. Day and location of testing do not matter to a revision-memory proposal unless they directly affect what the finding means for you.
A relevant sample characteristic: “Participants were final-year high-school students in their exam preparation period.”
KEEP. A sample characteristic does matter here — it is what lets you connect this research to your own final-year population.
An exact room or location with no relevance: “Testing took place in Room 214, which had no windows.”
PROBABLY CUT. A location with no bearing on your proposal is procedural noise. Cut it unless it changes what you can claim.
A procedural feature that genuinely affects relevance: “Items were presented one by one on a screen rather than on paper, matching how students revise today.”
KEEP. Some procedural features genuinely affect relevance — here, the screen presentation links the research to modern study habits. Explain it only when it earns its place.
11

Described or explained?

Merely naming research and reporting a result is not necessarily enough. “Explained” versions make the meaning of the finding or conclusion clear. For each pair, decide whether the research is mostly described or explained.

Statement: “Researchers in a fictional study compared students who slept and students who stayed awake, and reported a group difference in recall.”
MOSTLY DESCRIBED. It reports that a difference existed, not what the difference meant — for example, that sleep supported memory and rested students recalled more.
Statement: “Rested participants recalled more studied words than sleep-deprived participants, suggesting that sleep supports the consolidation of studied material.”
EXPLAINED. It reports the finding and its meaning (what sleep does to memory), which is what Criterion A needs.
Statement: “The researchers measured students’ social-media use with a questionnaire and correlated it with self-esteem scores.”
MOSTLY DESCRIBED. This is procedural description — how they measured — with no finding or conclusion. It does not tell the reader what the research showed.
Statement: “More frequent appearance-based comparison was associated with lower self-esteem, meaning that comparison may be one route through which image-heavy apps affect adolescent wellbeing.”
EXPLAINED. The association is reported and its meaning for the wider problem is stated — a finding plus a conclusion, not just a description.
Statement: “A group of students answered questions about their study habits before the exam period.”
MOSTLY DESCRIBED. Who did what is described, but nothing is reported about what was found or concluded — the reader learns nothing they can use.
Statement: “Students who revised little slept more, while frequent revisers reported less sleep — suggesting that high-intensity revision may come at the cost of sleep.”
EXPLAINED. A reported pattern plus an interpretation of what it suggests — precisely the move that turns description into explanation.
12

Don’t stop at the research

Every piece of research you include ends the same way — with your answer to the question: why does this research matter to your proposed investigation?

Research A piece of relevant research
↓
Finding(s) What it actually showed
↓
Key conclusion What it concluded from that
↓
So what for my proposal? Why this research matters to my proposed investigation

Types of connection you can make

  • It provides evidence that makes the new question worth investigating.
  • It identifies a relevant relationship, behaviour or experience.
  • It supports investigating the issue in the selected population.
  • It reveals something the proposed investigation could extend.
  • It identifies an unanswered or insufficiently understood issue.
No one-sentence formula. These are kinds of reasoning, not sentence templates. The connection must be specific to the research you chose and the proposal you are building.
13

Linked or not yet linked?

Reporting a finding is not the same as linking it. A link explains why the finding matters to the new proposed investigation. For each scenario, read the research finding, then the student’s next sentence, and decide: linked to the proposal, or not yet linked.

Finding: research found an association between X and Y.
Student writes: “This shows that X and Y are associated.”
NOT YET LINKED. The student has essentially restated the finding. A stronger response explains why that association matters to the new proposed investigation.
Finding: research found an association between X and Y in adolescents.
Student writes: “Because this association appears in adolescents, investigating it in our school’s older students may show whether the relationship strengthens with age.”
LINKED TO THE PROPOSAL. The finding is used to set up the student’s own investigation — the connection to their population and question is explicit.
Finding: participants who revised with flashcards recalled more words.
Student writes: “This means flashcards work better than re-reading.”
NOT YET LINKED. A conclusion is drawn, but still only about the research itself. Nothing yet explains why this matters to this student’s proposed investigation.
Finding: participants who revised with flashcards recalled more vocabulary.
Student writes: “If retrieval practice supports recall, then our proposed comparison of flashcard and re-reading revision could help identify which method our students should use before exams.”
LINKED TO THE PROPOSAL. The finding is turned towards the student’s own comparison and its purpose for their population — a genuine link, not a restatement.
Finding: more frequent social comparison was associated with lower self-esteem.
Student writes: “Social comparison was linked to self-esteem.”
NOT YET LINKED. Same idea, shorter sentence — still a restatement. There is no step from “the research found” to “therefore my proposal will…”.
Finding: more frequent social comparison was associated with lower self-esteem.
Student writes: “This supports investigating whether the same association appears among our school’s students, who use image-heavy apps daily.”
LINKED TO THE PROPOSAL. The finding is used to justify the student’s own population and question — exactly the “so what for my proposal” move.

The second-research trap

THE SECOND-RESEARCH TRAP. A common weakness in assessed work is doing a strong job with the first piece of research and then merely describing the second. Both pieces need to be explained and linked to the proposed investigation for the strongest descriptor.

Research 1

Relevant finding Key conclusion Explained Linked to proposal

Research 2

Relevant finding Key conclusion Explained Linked to proposal

Both columns must reach the bottom. The second piece is not decoration — it carries real weight in the descriptor, so treat it as seriously as the first.

Which research needs fixing?

Here is a fictional Criterion A plan. Read the two mini research summaries, then answer the questions.

Mini Criterion A (illustrative)

Research 1: “A study of students who revised with flashcards reported higher vocabulary recall than re-reading. The authors concluded that retrieval practice supports recall. Because our class uses both methods before exams, comparing them in our school could show which revision method students should adopt.”

Research 2: “A study looked at how students organise their notes. Participants had their notes categorised, and reported on their study habits.”

Which piece of research needs more work?

Judging the two summaries above — which piece needs more work?
RESEARCH 2. Research 1 has a finding, a conclusion and a link to the proposal. Research 2 has neither a clear relevant finding nor a conclusion, and it is not linked to the proposal at all.
What is missing from Research 2 in the summary above?
THE MAIN GAP IS THE KEY CONCLUSION (AND THE LINK). Research 2 reports what participants did (categorised notes) but no finding, no conclusion, and no connection to the proposed investigation. It needs all four — and the explicit link to the proposal is the one that is most clearly missing.
How to avoid the trap: treat Research 2 as a second full element — relevant finding, key conclusion, explanation, and an explicit connection to your proposed investigation.
16

Aim or research question: refining it for Criterion A

IB accepts an aim or a research question for the assessment descriptor. You are not required to choose one format over the other. What matters is that the form you use is:

CLEAR + FOCUSED + CONSISTENT WITH THE PROPOSED INVESTIGATION.

Your aim/RQ should make the direction of the investigation understandable and connect logically to the problem and population you established in the introduction. As your understanding sharpens, your wording should sharpen with it.

17

Too broad → focused

Refining an aim/RQ for Criterion A means making the wording clear, focused and consistent with the proposed investigation. Judge each example below: focused or needs refocusing? The feedback names exactly what to fix.

“To investigate how social media affects teenagers.”
NEEDS REFOCUSING — POPULATION AND CONSTRUCT. “Social media” is not one variable and “teenagers” is not a defined population. Name the specific behaviour and outcome, and bound the group.
“To investigate whether appearance-based social comparison is associated with self-esteem among female students aged 16–17 at our school who use Instagram most days.”
FOCUSED. Named variables (comparison, self-esteem), a clearly bounded population, and relationship wording that matches a survey method. (This is intentionally the clear, focused example.)
“To investigate memory, sleep and revision habits in students.”
NEEDS REFOCUSING — SEVERAL UNRELATED QUESTIONS COMBINED. Three separate topics in one aim. Cut to a single relationship or comparison you can actually investigate.
“To look into things related to phone use.”
NEEDS REFOCUSING — VAGUE WORDING. “Look into” and “things related to” name no variable, no population and no relationship to measure.
“To investigate the effect of all types of digital media on every aspect of student wellbeing.”
NEEDS REFOCUSING — UNREALISTIC SCOPE. “All types” and “every aspect” cannot be investigated in one proposal. Pick one type of media, one aspect of wellbeing, and one population.
“To investigate whether students in Year 12 who revise with flashcards recall more vocabulary words than students who re-read their notes.”
FOCUSED. One comparison, one measure, one population — and experimental wording that matches an experiment.
18

Words change what your question claims

Words such as effect, cause, increase, decrease, improve, lead to and role can imply cause and effect. The wording of your aim/RQ must accurately represent what your proposed methodology can investigate — a causal claim needs a design that can investigate causality.

What about “role”? The word “role” is not banned — but it can sometimes imply causality depending on how it is used. If you use it, be clear about what kind of relationship you mean.

What does this question imply?

Classify what each aim or research question claims: causal, relational, experiential, or observational.

“Does listening to gentle instrumental audio improve the number of words students recall?”
CAUSAL. “Improve” claims that the audio changes recall. That needs an experimental method — or the wording must change to “associated with”.
“Is time spent on Instagram associated with self-reported self-esteem?”
RELATIONAL. “Associated with” investigates a relationship between two variables — a natural fit for a questionnaire.
“How do students who recently moved schools experience joining an established class?”
EXPERIENTIAL. “Experience” asks for personal accounts — the natural subject of an interview study.
“How often do students check their phones during 30 minutes of silent study?”
OBSERVATIONAL. “How often” asks about the frequency of visible behaviour — recorded as it happens.
“Does increasing daily sleep from 6 to 8 hours increase students’ test scores?”
CAUSAL. “Increasing…increase” claims a change in sleep changes scores. Unless a manipulation is proposed, reword to “associated with”.
“What role does parents’ involvement play in students’ reported motivation?”
RELATIONAL (as used here). “Role” can imply causality, but this wording asks about a contribution between two reported variables — a relationship. State clearly which kind of claim you mean.

Learn from real IA marking — focus

Three anonymised situations inspired by official examiner feedback. For each case, decide what you would fix first.

Case 1

The aim tries to investigate several broad questions at once, and the population is extremely broad.

Lesson: insufficiently focused.
In Case 1, what would you fix first?
REFOCUS THE AIM/RQ. Several broad questions mean nothing can be investigated well. Cut to one focused question, then tighten the population to match it.
Case 2

The aim/research question clearly identifies a specific issue and a relevant population.

Lesson: focused.
In Case 2, what would you fix first?
LEAVE AS IS. A clear issue plus a relevant population is exactly what the descriptor rewards — focus on polishing this, not changing it.
Case 3

The wording implies causation, although the proposed investigation is actually examining a relationship.

Lesson: the wording and methodology do not align precisely.
In Case 3, what would you fix first?
ALIGN THE WORDING WITH THE METHODOLOGY. Use relationship language (“associated with”) unless the design can support a causal claim — the wording and method must say the same thing.

Putting it all together: from research notes → Criterion A

The recommended construction process — turn your planning notes into a written introduction one decision at a time.

Construction process
  1. Establish the real-life problem.
  2. Make the population of interest clear.
  3. Explain how/why the problem affects that population.
  4. Introduce Research 1.
  5. Explain its relevant findings and key conclusion.
  6. Explain why it matters to the proposal.
  7. Introduce Research 2.
  8. Explain its relevant findings and key conclusion.
  9. Explain why it matters to the proposal.
  10. State/refine the focused aim or RQ.
Recommended structure — not an IB-mandated paragraph order. Use the order that lets the elements connect logically and read clearly.

Build it or break it?

Read this miniature fictional Criterion A plan — then decide which parts are solid and which need work.

Miniature plan (fictional, with built-in weaknesses)

Problem: students use phones late at night. Population: everyone at the school. Impact: none stated. Research 1: a study showed late-night phone use was associated with poorer sleep; the concluding point is linked to our proposal about concentration. Research 2: “participants filled in a questionnaire in Room 12 in March and reported their study habits.” Aim: “To investigate whether phone use causes poor concentration and lower grades among all students at our school.”

Solid or needs work?

The population: “everyone at the school”
NEEDS WORK — POPULATION TOO BROAD. “Everyone at the school” spans ages and situations. Define which group and why they are connected to the problem.
The impact of the problem on the population
NEEDS WORK — IMPACT MISSING. No consequence is explained. What does late-night phone use actually do to this group’s concentration, learning or sleep?
Research 1: finding presented and linked to the proposal
SOLID. Research 1 has a relevant finding, a concluding point, and an explicit link to this proposal — the full treatment the descriptor wants.
Research 2: “participants filled in a questionnaire in Room 12 in March and reported their study habits”
NEEDS WORK — MERELY DESCRIBED. This reports procedure only: no relevant finding, no key conclusion, no meaning for the proposal. Research 2 is doing almost nothing here.
The detail “Room 12 in March”
NEEDS WORK — UNNECESSARY PROCEDURAL DETAIL. A room and a month that change nothing for your proposal are exactly the procedural noise Criterion A does not need.
Research 2’s connection to the proposal
NEEDS WORK — NOT LINKED. Nothing connects Research 2 to the proposed investigation. Both pieces must be explained and linked for the strongest descriptor.
The aim’s wording: “phone use causes poor concentration and lower grades”
NEEDS WORK — CAUSAL WORDING, TWO OUTCOMES. “Causes” overpromises what the proposal can show, and two outcomes split the focus. Reword to “associated with” and target one outcome.

Common mistakes that cost marks

  • Aim/RQ too broad — nothing specific to investigate.
  • Population poorly defined — no clear boundaries or no link to the problem.
  • Real-life problem stated without explaining its impact — the “so what” is missing.
  • Two pieces of research included but mostly described — findings and conclusions not really explained.
  • Only Research 1 properly explained — Research 2 left as a summary.
  • Research 2 not explicitly connected to the proposal — it floats.
  • Excessive procedural detail replacing findings/conclusions — method paragraphs instead of meaning.
  • Vague statements such as “this supports my investigation” — without explaining how.
  • Causal wording that does not match the proposed methodology — the question claims more than the design can deliver.
