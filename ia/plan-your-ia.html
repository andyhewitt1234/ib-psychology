Plan Your IA

From an Area of Interest to a Researchable Proposal

This page is preparation — it is not an assessed criterion. Work through it before you start Criterion A (Introduction). By the end you will have developed: an area of interest, a real-life problem, a population of interest, a focused direction, a likely research method, and a provisional aim / research question — the raw material that Criterion A will turn into a written introduction.

1

Choose an area of interest

Start somewhere you are genuinely curious about. An area of interest is broad — it is a domain of psychology, not a question. Good starting areas include:

Social media Memory Stress Sleep Relationships Belonging Learning Acculturation Motivation Body image Attention

The key first move is to understand the difference. “Social media” is an area, not a problem. It is much too broad to investigate, and it gives you no behaviour to study. A researchable proposal starts when you turn an area into something you can actually ask about.

Area or researchable problem?

Click the better classification for each statement. This is a digital coach — it will give you instant feedback.

“Social media”
AREA OF INTEREST. Social media is a domain, not a problem. To research it you must name a behaviour, cognition or emotion connected to social media.
“Difficulty maintaining attention while studying when phone notifications arrive”
RESEARCHABLE PROBLEM DIRECTION. This names real behaviour (maintaining attention) and a specific circumstance (notifications arriving) — something a proposal could investigate.
“Memory”
AREA OF INTEREST. “Memory” alone has no behaviour or variable yet. Which kind of memory? Whose? In what situation?
“Students sleeping less during exam week than during an ordinary week”
RESEARCHABLE PROBLEM DIRECTION. A specific population (students), a specific change (less sleep in exam week) and a comparison — the bones of a proposal.
“Stress”
AREA OF INTEREST. Stress is a broad domain. A problem would say what kind of stress, in whom, and in what situation.
“Feeling more stressed about exams when you compare your revision progress with classmates”
RESEARCHABLE PROBLEM DIRECTION. It connects a specific behaviour (comparing revision) to a specific feeling (exam stress) — a tight, investigable idea.
2

Identify the real-life problem

A proposal investigates a real-life problem: something that actually matters to real people. Push your area of interest until it becomes a specific problem.

Example: narrowing “social media” Social media (area →) social comparison (a process people engage in →) appearance-based social comparison (a specific kind →) appearance-based social comparison may be associated with lower self-esteem (a real-life problem worth investigating).

A good real-life problem names a behaviour, feeling or cognition that could be studied, in people it plausibly affects. A researchable proposal does not need a dramatic problem — many excellent IAs study everyday problems like concentration, homework stress or belonging.

Too broad → narrow it down

For each pair, choose the version that a proposal could actually investigate.

“How does social media affect teenagers?”
NARROWER WINS. “Social media” is a universe of behaviours and “affect teenagers” says nothing about how. A proposal needs a specific behaviour and a specific outcome.
“How does sleep affect people?”
NARROWER WINS. “Sleep” and “people” are too huge. A usable problem names a specific sleep pattern and a specific outcome — for example sleeping fewer than 7 hours and difficulty recalling studied material the next morning.
“Is stress bad?”
NARROWER WINS. “Bad” is not measurable. A usable problem links a specific stressor (self-reported academic stress during mock-exam weeks) to a specific outcome (concentration during lessons).
“Do students who join a new school fit in?”
NARROWER WINS. “Fit in” is vague. A usable problem specifies belonging (perceived sense of belonging in the first term after moving schools) and a behaviour it affects (willingness to answer questions in class).
“What is peer pressure like?”
NARROWER WINS. “Like” is unspoken. A usable problem names self-reported peer influence on a specific decision — for example which subjects students choose in their final DP year.
3

Choose your population of interest

The population of interest is the group of people whose problem you want to understand. In the IA you research a population — a defined group that is relevant to your problem — not necessarily every living person affected by it.

What makes a population useful?

  • Identifiable — you could actually describe who these people are.
  • Relevant — the problem genuinely affects this group.
  • Logically connected — you can explain why this population matters for this problem.
  • Appropriately defined — not loosely broad, but also not pointlessly tiny.
A note on “narrow”: the IA does not require the most extremely narrow population you can imagine. A population should be clearly defined and logically connected to the problem — humdrum definitions like “final-year high-school students” are perfectly workable. Assessed IA examples show that clarity and logical fit matter more than extremes of specificity.

Is the population clear enough?

Judge each description: is it too broad to be useful, or sufficiently focused?

“Students”
TOO BROAD. “Students” spans ages, contexts and countries. It is not clearly identifiable, so it cannot anchor a proposal.
“People”
TOO BROAD. “People” is everyone. A population of interest needs boundaries you can explain.
“Female students aged 16–17 at our school who use Instagram most days”
SUFFICIENTLY FOCUSED. Identifiable age, context and an everyday behaviour that links directly to a social-media problem.
“First-year DP students who moved to a new country within the past 12 months”
SUFFICIENTLY FOCUSED. Clearly identifiable and logically connected to acculturation or belonging.
“Teenagers”
TOO BROAD. “Teenagers” is an age band, not a population connected to a specific problem. Can you say which teenagers and why these?
“Final-year high-school students preparing for university entrance examinations”
SUFFICIENTLY FOCUSED. A defined group facing a defined situation — an obvious home for an exam-stress or revision problem.
4

Connect problem to population

This is essential preparation for Criterion A. The strongest introductions are built on a three-part link: a problem, a population affected by it, and a clear sense of why it matters for that group. You are not writing a polished paragraph yet — you are testing whether the link holds.

Weak thinking: “Social media is bad, and many people use it.”
Stronger thinking: “Among female students aged 16–17 who use Instagram most days, frequent appearance-based social comparison is associated with lower self-esteem — and because self-esteem is linked to wellbeing, the problem matters for this group.”

What is missing?

Each statement below is incomplete. Decide which element is missing: the problem, the population, the impact (why it matters), or nothing (all three are present).

“Students at our school often find it hard to concentrate while studying.”
MISSING: THE IMPACT. We know the problem (hard to concentrate) and the population (students at our school), but not why it matters. What does poor concentration cost this group?
“Appearance-based social comparison is linked to lower self-esteem.”
MISSING: THE POPULATION. A claim with no group attached is floating. Whose self-esteem? Who is doing the comparing?
“Students who moved schools mid-year report feeling they do not belong, and a weak sense of belonging is associated with lower class participation.”
ALL THREE PRESENT. Problem (not belonging), population (students who moved schools mid-year) and impact (lower class participation).
“Final-year students at our school report feeling anxious several times a week.”
MISSING: THE IMPACT. The problem (feeling anxious) and population (final-year students at our school) are named, but not why it matters. What does this anxiety cost the group?
“Dormitory residents sleep fewer than 7 hours on weeknights, and report forgetting studied material the next morning.”
ALL THREE PRESENT. Problem (sleeping fewer than 7 hours), population (dormitory residents) and impact (studied material is not recalled the next morning).
Your IA planning notes
  1. Real-life problem: write one or two sentences describing the problem in your area of interest.
  2. Population of interest: who is affected, and how are they described?
  3. Why it matters: what is the impact for this specific population?
5

Decide what you actually want to know

Before choosing a method you must be precise about the kind of knowledge you are after. Each of these answers calls for a different design:

Relationship Difference between groups Effect Experiences Perceptions / attitudes Observable behaviour Self-reported measures

What are you actually asking?

Decide what kind of information each aim describes.

“To investigate whether students who spend more time on Instagram report lower self-esteem.”
A RELATIONSHIP. One reported variable (Instagram time) is related to another (self-esteem). You are not manipulating anything.
“To investigate whether hearing gentle instrumental audio during study changes how many words students recall.”
AN EFFECT. An independent variable (audio present or not) is applied and its effect on recall is measured.
“To understand how students who recently moved schools experience their first month in a new class.”
EXPERIENCES / PERCEPTIONS. The aim is depth of personal experience, not counting anything.
“To investigate how often students check their phones during 30 minutes of silent study.”
OBSERVABLE BEHAVIOUR. Counting instances of a visible action — a natural fit for observation.
“To find out what students think about the amount of homework they receive.”
ATTITUDES / SELF-REPORT. You are collecting self-reported opinions, typically via questionnaire.
6

Choose one research method

You will investigate your proposal with one of the four methods you studied in the class practicals. This page is only a concise decision guide — your practical lessons developed each method fully.

Experiment

Manipulate an independent variable, keep conditions controlled, and measure its effect. Best for cause-and-effect questions.

Interview

In-depth, open questions with a small number of participants. Best for experiences and perceptions.

Observation

Record behaviour as it happens, with a coding scheme. Best for observable behaviour and frequency.

Survey / Questionnaire

Standardised questions to a sample. Best for attitudes, self-reports and relationships between variables.

Which method fits?

Click the method you would choose. The coach will reveal the BEST FIT and WHY. In some scenarios more than one method is defensible — the feedback will tell you when.

You want to know whether students recall more of a revision list when items are organised into categories rather than presented in random order.
BEST FIT: EXPERIMENT. You are testing an effect — categorised vs random organisation (IV) on recall (DV) — which needs a manipulated independent variable and controlled conditions.
You want to understand, in students’ own words, what it feels like to join a class that already knows each other.
BEST FIT: INTERVIEW. You want the experience in participants’ own words — open questions with a few participants give the depth you need. A questionnaire could capture attitudes, but would lose the personal detail.
You want to measure whether more hours of social media per week are associated with poorer self-reported sleep quality across a large sample of students.
BEST FIT: SURVEY / QUESTIONNAIRE. Two self-reported variables (social-media hours, sleep quality) across a large sample — a questionnaire measures the relationship efficiently and allows a good sample size.
You want to record how often students pick up their phone during 30 minutes of silent study, as it actually happens.
BEST FIT: OBSERVATION. This is about behaviour you can see and count as it happens. A questionnaire would ask people to estimate, which is less accurate than watching.
You want to compare students’ self-reported motivation at the start of lessons, before and after introducing a two-minute mindfulness break.
BEST FIT: EXPERIMENT. Introducing the break is a manipulation — compare motivation before vs after under controlled conditions. Motivation is captured by a self-report questionnaire, which makes survey-by-questionnaire a defensible second choice if you treat the break as the independent variable measured across time.
You want students to describe, in their own words, what influences whether they join class discussions, using real situations they have experienced.
BEST FIT: INTERVIEW. The question asks for personal examples and explanation, which interviews draw out best. A survey with open items could also work and reach more participants — two defensible routes.
7

Make the wording match the method

The question you ask must match the method you choose. When the wording and the method disagree, the proposal looks confused.

Cause / Effect

“Does X affect Y?” — requires an experiment: a manipulated variable and a measured effect.

Relationship

“Is X associated with Y?” — fits a survey / questionnaire with self-reported measures.

Experience

“How do members experience or perceive…?” — fits interviews.

Observable behaviour

“How frequently / in what ways…?” — fits observation.

Watch your verbs. Words like effect, causes, improves and impact imply a causal claim. If your method cannot establish cause, reword to “associated with” or “is related to”. The word “role” is not automatically banned — but state clearly what kind of relationship you mean rather than letting it imply cause.

Does the wording match the method?

Judge each aim-method pair.

Aim: “to investigate whether students who spend more time on Instagram report lower self-esteem.” — Method: survey / questionnaire.
MATCH. “Whether … report” asks about a relationship between two self-reported variables — exactly what a questionnaire measures.
Aim: “to investigate the effect of categorised revision lists on word recall.” — Method: experiment.
MATCH. “Effect” is causal language, and an experiment can support a causal claim.
Aim: “to understand how students who moved schools experience their first month.” — Method: interview.
MATCH. “Experience” signals personal accounts, which interviews are designed to gather.
Aim: “to investigate whether gentle audio improves how many words students recall.” — Method: survey / questionnaire.
MISMATCH. “Improves” claims an effect, but a survey only captures self-reports — it cannot establish that audio improved recall. This aim needs an experiment, or reword to “associated with”.
Aim: “to investigate how often students check their phones during 30 minutes of silent study.” — Method: observation.
MATCH. “How often” asks about frequency of observable behaviour — the natural subject of an observation study.
Aim: “to investigate the effect of sleep on exam marks.” — Method: questionnaire measuring sleep and exam marks.
MISMATCH. “Effect” implies cause, but a questionnaire can only show an association between reported sleep and reported marks. Reword to “associated with” or redesign as an experiment.
8

Write a provisional aim / research question

This is your first attempt — it does not have to be perfect. A strong provisional aim makes clear what you are investigating: the variables, the population, the kind of relationship you expect, and wording that matches your method.

Focused or vague?

Judge each example.

“To investigate the effect of social media on self-esteem.”
VAGUE. No specific variable (“social media” is huge), no population, causal wording, and no clue which method could investigate it.
“To investigate whether appearance-based social comparison is associated with self-esteem among female students aged 16–17 who use Instagram most days.”
FOCUSED. Named variables, clearly defined population, relationship wording that matches a survey method.
“To investigate stuff about memory and revision.”
VAGUE. “Stuff about” names no variable, no population and no relationship to measure.
“To investigate whether students in Year 12 who revise with flashcards recall more vocabulary words than students who re-read their notes.”
FOCUSED. One comparison (flashcards vs re-reading), one measure (vocabulary recall), one population (Year 12) — and an experimental design is implied.
“To see how moving countries affects students’ lives.”
VAGUE. “Lives” is everything — the claim would need a single outcome (belonging? stress? participation?) and a defined population.

Research question troubleshooter

Stuck at any point? Tap the problem that matches yours for concise guidance.

Diagnostic The question is still too broad Name one specific behaviour or variable. If you can not say which behaviour, which outcome and which people, you are not broad — you are unfinished. Revisit Steps 1–3.
Diagnostic My population is unclear Give the group boundaries someone else could recognise: who they are, in what context, with what relevant behaviour. Revisit Step 3.
Diagnostic I do not know which method to choose Let the kind of knowledge decide: effect → experiment; relationship or attitudes → survey; experience → interview; visible behaviour or frequency → observation. Revisit Steps 5–6.
Diagnostic I am trying to investigate too many variables Cut to one comparison or one relationship. A proposal with three effects, four measures and two populations cannot be written up in 2,200 words. Revisit Steps 4–5.
Diagnostic My wording does not match my method Watch for effect, causes, improve, impact. Match causal claims to experiments and association language to surveys. Revisit Step 7.
Diagnostic I am not sure the problem matters to my population State the impact explicitly. If you cannot say why it matters to this group, the introduction has no rationale. Revisit Step 4.
Diagnostic I am not sure it is psychological The proposal must investigate behaviour, cognition or emotion. If your focus is really about an app or a product, ask which psychological process (attention, belonging, self-esteem…) is under study. Revisit Step 1.

10

Remember: you are designing, not conducting

The IA is a research proposal — you design a study, you do not carry it out. Keep your proposal:

  • Realistic — a study that could genuinely be proposed and described.
  • Methodologically coherent — method, variables, population and wording all fit together.
  • Ethically defensible — it passes the gate above.
  • Sufficiently detailed — enough specificity to be written up, run and reported, without real participants or real data.
This is not one of the class practicals, which you actually carry out. Your IA plan is a hypothetical, well-designed study — the plan itself is the assessed work.

Worked development example

Watch how a broad interest becomes a researchable proposal, one decision at a time.

Area of interest Social media
↓
Real-life problem Appearance-based social comparison is common on image-heavy sites
↓
Population of interest Female students aged 16–17 who use Instagram most days
↓
What to know Whether greater comparison is associated with lower self-esteem
↓
Research method Survey / questionnaire — self-report measures of comparison and self-esteem
↓
Provisional aim “To investigate whether appearance-based social comparison is associated with self-esteem among female students aged 16–17 who use Instagram most days.”
Why is this stronger than “How does social media affect teenagers?”
  • Variable: one specific behaviour (appearance-based comparison) replaces an entire platform.
  • Population: clearly defined users replace an unwieldy “teenagers”.
  • Wording: associated with matches the survey method; no false causal claim.
  • Feasibility: self-report scales for both variables could plausibly be proposed.

This is a model of thinking, not a template to copy — build each decision from your area of interest.
