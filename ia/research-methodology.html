1 What IB Requires — Criterion B 6 marks

Criterion B assesses three components:

Research Method

Identify and explain your chosen method.

Procedure

Explain how the investigation would proceed.

Ethical Considerations

Describe relevant ethical issues and how they would be addressed.

For the strongest descriptor (5–6) you must:

  • Explain the research method — why it fits your aim/RQ and population.
  • Explain the procedure — sufficient detail to understand how the study would work.
  • Describe relevant ethical considerations — specific to your proposal.
  • Explicitly link ethical considerations to the investigation — not a generic list.
Procedural decisions to address (where relevant): sampling technique, sample characteristics, experimental design (for experiments), setting, and the step-by-step process/procedure.
Do not invent extra requirements. IB does not require a specific word count, paragraph order, universal sample size, mandatory experimental design for all methods, mandatory deception, or identical ethical issues for every proposal.
2

What changes as the marks go up?

Do not memorise rubric text — understand the climb. Each level moves from naming to explaining to justifying for this proposal.

Name it Explain it Justify it Link it
1–2 Lower level
  • Methodology is largely stated or described.
  • Important procedural information may be missing or unclear.
  • Ethics may be generic or incomplete.
3–4 Middle level
  • Method and procedure are more developed.
  • Relevant ethical considerations are present.
  • But explanation, detail, relevance or explicit links may still be incomplete.
5–6 Strongest level
  • RESEARCH METHOD — EXPLAINED
  • PROCEDURE — EXPLAINED
  • ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS — RELEVANT AND DESCRIBED
  • ETHICS — EXPLICITLY LINKED TO THIS INVESTIGATION
The workload does not change — the quality does. The strongest descriptor does not ask you to write more; it asks you to explain instead of name, to justify instead of describe, and to link every methodological and ethical decision to your proposal.

How to build your research methodology

Work from the top down — each decision feeds the next. Everything you need is on this page.

↓

4

Start with the aim/RQ

Before choosing a method, ask: What does my aim/RQ actually require me to find out? Revisit the aim/RQ you developed in Criterion A.

Do not choose a method simply because you like it. The method must help answer the aim/RQ.

The aim/RQ dictates the type of information needed, which points to the method:

Cause / Difference Between Conditions

Experiment may be appropriate

Detailed Experiences / Perceptions

Interview may be appropriate

Observable Behaviour

Observation may be appropriate

Standardized Self-Report Responses

Survey / Questionnaire may be appropriate

Important: More than one method may sometimes be defensible depending on what the researcher wants to understand. Do not teach that every question has exactly one correct method.
5

Does the method match the question?

For each proposed aim/RQ below, choose the most defensible method. The goal is methodological reasoning, not memorising one-to-one pairings.

“To investigate whether students who revise with flashcards recall more vocabulary than students who re-read their notes.”
EXPERIMENT. You are comparing two conditions (flashcards vs re-reading) on a measured outcome (recall) — a causal question needs a manipulated IV and controlled conditions.
“To understand how students who recently moved schools experience their first month in a new class.”
INTERVIEW. The aim seeks depth of personal experience — open questions with a few participants give the detail a questionnaire cannot.
“To record how often students check their phones during 30 minutes of silent study.”
OBSERVATION. This asks about frequency of visible behaviour as it happens. A questionnaire would rely on estimates, which are less accurate.
“To measure whether hours of social media per week are associated with self-reported sleep quality across a large student sample.”
SURVEY / QUESTIONNAIRE. Two self-reported variables across a large sample — a questionnaire measures the relationship efficiently.
“To compare motivation before and after introducing a two-minute mindfulness break at the start of lessons.”
BEST FIT: EXPERIMENT (survey also defensible). Introducing the break is a manipulation — compare before vs after under controlled conditions. Motivation is captured by self-report, so a questionnaire could also work as a second defensible route.
“To explore what influences whether students join class discussions, using real situations they have experienced.”
BEST FIT: INTERVIEW (survey also defensible). The question asks for personal examples and explanation — interviews draw out depth best. A survey with open items could also work and reach more participants — two defensible routes.
6

Explaining the chosen method

There is a critical difference between naming the method and explaining why it is appropriate.

Weak thinking

“I will use a questionnaire because questionnaires are easy to use.”

≠

Stronger methodological thinking

WHAT does this method allow me to investigate? WHY does that help answer MY aim/RQ? WHY is it appropriate for MY proposed population/problem?

Generic or investigation-specific?

Classify each method justification as generic or specific to the proposal.

“I will use an experiment because experiments allow me to establish cause and effect.”
GENERIC. This is true of any experiment. It does not explain why an experiment fits your specific aim, population or variables.
“A repeated-measures experiment fits my aim because the same students can experience both revision methods (flashcards vs re-reading), controlling for individual differences in memory ability.”
SPECIFIC TO THE PROPOSAL. It names the design, explains why it suits this comparison, and links to the population (students) and aim (comparing two revision methods).
“I will use interviews because interviews give rich data.”
GENERIC. True of any interview. No link to the aim (experiences of students who moved schools) or the population.
“A semi-structured interview is appropriate because my aim seeks detailed personal accounts of transition experiences, and the semi-structured format lets me cover core topics while following up on each student’s unique story.”
SPECIFIC TO THE PROPOSAL. Explains why this interview format serves this aim and population.
“I will use observation because it lets me see behaviour directly.”
GENERIC. No connection to phone-checking behaviour, the silent study setting, or the frequency aim.
“Naturalistic observation during a 30-minute silent study period lets me count phone checks as they happen in the actual context, without relying on self-report.”
SPECIFIC TO THE PROPOSAL. Links method (naturalistic observation), setting (silent study), behaviour (phone checks), and why it beats self-report for this aim.

The four method paths

These cards are application guides — not a duplicate of the Research Methods unit. They help you apply methodological knowledge to your IA proposal.

Experiment

Possible forms: true experiment, quasi-experiment.

Consider: IV and DV, operationalisation, experimental design, allocation, controls/standardisation, setting, how conditions would differ.

Interview

Possible forms: structured, semi-structured, focus group.

Consider: interview format, participant selection, setting, questioning process, consistency/flexibility, interviewer role.

Observation

Possible forms: naturalistic / controlled, overt / covert, participant / non-participant.

Consider: observable behaviour, setting, observer role, recording process, operationalisation of behaviour.

Survey / Questionnaire

Consider: target respondents, administration process, standardised questions/items, response formats, setting/mode of administration.

This section exists to help you APPLY methodological knowledge to your own IA proposal. Do not copy these lists — select only what is relevant to your specific method and proposal.
8

Which version better explains the method?

For each pair, choose the version that better explains the methodological decision.

A: “A semi-structured interview would be used.”
B: “A semi-structured interview would be used because the investigation seeks detailed accounts of participants’ experiences while still allowing the researcher to address the same core topics with each participant.”
VERSION B. A merely names the method; B explains why this interview format serves the aim (detailed experiences + consistency across participants).
A: “An experiment will be conducted.”
B: “A repeated-measures experiment will be used because the same participants can experience both conditions, controlling for individual differences in baseline memory performance.”
VERSION B. A names the method; B explains the design choice and the specific methodological advantage for this comparison.
A: “Observation will be used to watch students.”
B: “Naturalistic observation during silent study periods will be used because the aim measures the frequency of phone-checking behaviour as it occurs in its natural context.”
VERSION B. A is a label; B connects method (naturalistic), setting (silent study), behaviour (phone-checking), and the rationale (natural context vs self-report).
A: “A questionnaire will be distributed.”
B: “An online questionnaire with Likert-scale items will be used because the aim measures the relationship between two self-reported variables (social media hours, sleep quality) across a large sample efficiently.”
VERSION B. A names the method; B explains the administration mode, item format, variables, sample size rationale, and why a questionnaire fits a relationship aim.
A: “A focus group will be run.”
B: “A focus group is appropriate because the aim explores shared perceptions of school belonging among recently arrived students, and group interaction may elicit richer accounts than individual interviews.”
VERSION B. A names the format; B justifies the choice of focus group over individual interview by linking to the aim (shared perceptions) and population (recently arrived students).
A: “A quasi-experiment will be used.”
B: “A quasi-experiment is necessary because random allocation to conditions is not possible in a school setting — existing classes will be assigned to conditions, and pre-test scores will be used to check equivalence.”
VERSION B. A names the method type; B explains why quasi (not true experiment), the allocation constraint, and a concrete step to address it (pre-test equivalence check).
9

Sampling technique: who would take part — and how would they be selected?

Criterion B requires you to explain the proposed sampling technique. Students must distinguish three things:

Population of Interest

The broader group you want to understand (from Criterion A).

Proposed Sample

The specific participants who would actually take part.

Sampling Technique

How those participants would be identified, recruited or selected from the population.

Population of Interest The group you want to understand
↓
Sampling Technique How you select participants from that population
↓
Proposed Sample The participants who would actually take part
Do not simply write “Opportunity sampling would be used.” Explain the proposed process so the reader understands how participants would be identified/recruited, why the technique is appropriate, and how the sample relates to the population of interest.
No universal sample size. The IA does not require a specific number of participants across all proposals. Sample size should be justified in relation to the method, aim and proposed population. Different methods often involve different sample-size considerations — for example, a larger sample may support a questionnaire, while a smaller sample may suit an in-depth interview.
10

Population, sample or sampling?

Classify each statement as a population of interest, sample characteristic, sampling technique, or recruitment / procedure detail.

“All students aged 16–18 at our school.”
POPULATION OF INTEREST. This defines the broad group you want to understand — not the participants you will actually recruit.
“30 female students aged 16–17 who use Instagram most days.”
SAMPLE CHARACTERISTIC. Age, gender, and a relevant behaviour — specific details about who the proposed participants would be.
“Volunteer sampling via a school-wide email invitation.”
SAMPLING TECHNIQUE. Named method (volunteer) and how participants would be reached (email).
“The researcher would contact the school’s pastoral office to request a list of students who transferred mid-year.”
RECRUITMENT / PROCEDURE DETAIL. A concrete step in how participants would be identified and approached.
“Students who recently moved schools and are in their first year at a new school.”
POPULATION OF INTEREST. A defined group sharing relevant characteristics — the broader group you want to understand.
“12 students who moved schools within the past 12 months, aged 15–17.”
SAMPLE CHARACTERISTIC. Specific number, age range, and a relevant membership criterion for the proposed participants.
“Purposive sampling: participants would be selected because they meet the specific criteria of having moved schools recently.”
SAMPLING TECHNIQUE. Named method (purposive) with a rationale linked to the proposal’s criteria.
“An invitation letter would be sent home with students, and parental consent obtained before participation.”
RECRUITMENT / PROCEDURE DETAIL. A concrete recruitment step (letter home) and an ethical safeguard (parental consent).
11

Sample characteristics: relevant or unnecessary?

The proposed sample needs enough relevant detail for the reader to understand who would participate. Relevant characteristics depend on the proposal. Do not add irrelevant demographic detail just to make the section longer.

Fictional proposal: “To investigate whether appearance-based social comparison on an image-sharing platform is associated with self-esteem among female students aged 16–17 who use Instagram most days.”

Which characteristics matter?

For the proposal above, classify each characteristic as relevant or unnecessary.

“Participants would be female students aged 16–17 at our school who use Instagram at least five days per week.”
RELEVANT. Age, gender and usage frequency directly match the population and aim — the reader immediately understands who is in the sample.
“Participants would be right-handed students.”
UNNECESSARY. Handedness has no connection to social comparison or self-esteem in this proposal. Do not add characteristics that do not serve the aim.
“Participants would be students who use an image-sharing platform daily and actively post or view content.”
RELEVANT. This connects directly to the behaviour under investigation (image-sharing use) and ensures the sample is appropriate for the variables.
“Participants would speak English as their first language.”
UNNECESSARY (for this proposal). Unless the investigation specifically involves language or cultural differences in social comparison, this adds no methodological value.
“Participants would have a comparable age range (16–17) to ensure the sample is homogeneous for the developmental stage most relevant to social media use.”
RELEVANT. A narrow age range makes the sample more appropriate for this proposal and justifies why older or younger students were not included.
“Participants would include students from at least five different nationalities.”
UNNECESSARY (for this proposal). Unless cultural diversity is central to the aim, adding a nationality requirement does not serve the investigation.

Learn from real IA marking — practical detail

Examiner lesson

A methodology can sound plausible but still lose strength when important practical details are missing. Examiners have flagged missing details such as: how many participants are proposed, how participants would actually be contacted/recruited, where the research would occur, and what the sequence of the procedure would be.

What would the reader still need to know?

Read this fictional methodology plan. What practical details are missing?

Fictional methodology plan

“A questionnaire would be used. Students would be asked about their sleep habits and academic performance. The data would be analysed to look for a relationship.”

What practical detail is missing from this plan?
ALL OF THESE ARE MISSING. The reader cannot picture the study because: no sample size, no recruitment method, no setting, and no step-by-step procedure. Significant gaps in practical detail can make it difficult for the procedure to meet the strongest descriptor, which requires the procedure to be explained.
The lesson: do not assume the reader can fill in the gaps. Missing important practical details can weaken your explanation of the procedure because the reader may not be able to understand clearly how the proposed investigation would work.
13

Research design — where relevant

FOR EXPERIMENT PROPOSALS

If your method is an experiment, you may need to explain the experimental design. If your method is an interview, observation or questionnaire, this section does not apply to you.

Repeated Measures

Same participants experience multiple conditions. Consider: order effects.

Independent Measures

Different participants experience different conditions. Consider: participant differences.

Matched Pairs

Participants are paired on relevant characteristics. Consider: matching criteria.

Do not tell non-experiment students that they need an experimental design. This section is for experiment proposals only.
14

Which design makes more sense?

For each experimental proposal, choose the most defensible design. The feedback reveals why it may fit, one advantage, and one challenge.

“Comparing recall of vocabulary words after revising with flashcards vs re-reading notes, using the same students in both conditions.”
REPEATED MEASURES. Same students in both conditions controls for individual differences in memory. Advantage: more statistical power. Challenge: order effects — counterbalance the condition order.
“Comparing concentration levels during lessons with and without a two-minute mindfulness break, using two different classes.”
INDEPENDENT MEASURES. Two different classes — the same students cannot be in both conditions simultaneously. Advantage: no carryover effects. Challenge: participant differences between classes.
“Measuring self-reported stress before and after a week of exam revision, using the same participants.”
REPEATED MEASURES. Same students measured before and after — each student acts as their own control. Advantage: controls for individual differences. Challenge: external events during the week could affect results.
“Comparing academic motivation between students who have moved schools and students who have not, matched on age and gender.”
MATCHED PAIRS. Two naturally occurring groups — students who moved vs did not — matched on age and gender to reduce confounding variables. Advantage: controls for key variables. Challenge: finding sufficient matches.
15

Where would the proposed research happen?

The setting should make methodological sense — it should connect to the method and aim. Possible settings include school/classroom, controlled room, natural environment, online environment, or interview location.

Does the setting fit?

For each scenario, decide whether the setting is a good fit or questionable fit.

“A naturalistic observation of phone-checking during 30 minutes of silent study — conducted in the school library.”
GOOD FIT. Silent study happens in the library — the setting matches the natural context of the behaviour being observed.
“A naturalistic observation of phone-checking during silent study — conducted in a busy school canteen.”
QUESTIONABLE FIT. A busy canteen is not a silent study environment — the setting does not match the behaviour you want to observe.
“A semi-structured interview about experiences of moving schools — conducted in a quiet private room at school.”
GOOD FIT. A private room supports sensitive, personal discussion — appropriate for an interview about personal experiences.
“A questionnaire about social media habits — distributed as a paper handout during a sports lesson.”
QUESTIONABLE FIT. A sports lesson is not conducive to thoughtful questionnaire completion — a classroom or online setting would be more appropriate.
“A repeated-measures experiment comparing two revision methods — conducted in a quiet classroom during a scheduled study period.”
GOOD FIT. A study period provides the controlled conditions needed for an experiment — quiet, focused, and comparable across conditions.

Procedure — make it possible to imagine

The procedure should allow the reader to understand how the proposed investigation would work. A useful procedure makes clear, where relevant:

Who The participants
↓
How recruited The recruitment process
↓
Where The setting
↓
What participants would experience The steps they would go through
↓
What the researcher would do The researcher’s role and actions
↓
What would be measured / recorded The data collection process
↓
How the process would end Debriefing, closing, data storage
Recommended procedure structure. IB does not require this exact order. The principle is: a reader should be able to understand how the proposed study could actually be carried out.
17

Too vague or clear enough?

For each fictional procedure, decide: could you actually follow this, or is it too vague?

“A questionnaire would be given to students about sleep and academic performance.”
TOO VAGUE. No sample size, no recruitment, no setting, no sequence, no details about the questionnaire. The reader cannot picture how this would work.
“Participants would be recruited via a school-wide email. They would complete an online questionnaire in their own time, consisting of 10 Likert-scale items about social media habits and 10 items about self-esteem. Responses would be anonymous and stored securely.”
CLEAR ENOUGH. Recruitment, format, content, mode, anonymity and data storage are all explained — the reader can picture how this would work.
“Interviews would be conducted with students about their experiences.”
TOO VAGUE. What kind of interview? How many participants? Where? How recruited? How long? What questions? The procedure needs enough detail for the reader to follow the process.
“8 students who recently moved schools would be recruited through the school pastoral office. Each would participate in a 20-minute semi-structured interview in a private room, exploring their experiences of joining a new class. Interviews would be audio-recorded with consent and transcribed for analysis.”
CLEAR ENOUGH. Sample size, recruitment, format, duration, setting, topic, recording and transcription are all explained — the reader can follow the process.
“An experiment would be conducted to test the effect of music on concentration.”
TOO VAGUE. What kind of music? What concentration measure? How many conditions? What is the IV? What is the DV? Who participates? Where? The reader has no way to picture the study.

Control, standardisation — or confounded?

Control Keeping other variables the same across conditions
→
Standardisation Using the same procedure for every participant
→
Confounding variable An uncontrolled variable that could affect the results

Control and standardisation reduce confounding variables. In a proposal, explain what would be controlled and how.

Which term best fits?

“Both conditions would take place in the same quiet classroom to ensure environment is kept constant.”
CONTROL. Keeping the environment constant across conditions — this controls a variable that could otherwise affect concentration.
“All participants would receive the same questionnaire instructions, read aloud by the researcher, in the same order.”
STANDARDISATION. Using the same procedure for every participant — same instructions, same delivery, same order.
“Participants in the music condition were tested during a free period, while those in the silence condition were tested during a revision lesson.”
CONFOUNDING VARIABLE. Different settings for different conditions — the setting, not the music, could explain any difference in results.

Ethics must match the proposal

Common examiner flag

Students often list ethical safeguards that sound impressive but do not connect to their own proposal. Examiners look for ethics that are specific to the proposed method, procedure and sample.

Generic ethics

“Informed consent would be obtained.” — no mention of who, how, or what it would say.

Imported ethics

A proposal for a questionnaire that says “participants would be debriefed about the true aim of the study” — but questionnaires do not usually involve deception.

Missing ethics

A proposal using interviews with vulnerable students, with no mention of emotional support, right to withdraw, or safeguarding.

Examiner lesson

The strongest ethical discussions connect directly to what the proposal actually involves. Every ethical safeguard you mention should be tied to a real feature of your proposed method, procedure or sample.

21

Core ethical thinking

Every IB IA proposal must consider ethics. The key question is: what ethical issues are relevant to this specific proposal?

Informed consent

Who would give consent? How? What would they be told? Is parental consent needed?

Right to withdraw

Can participants stop at any time? What happens to their data?

Confidentiality & anonymity

How would data be protected? Would responses be anonymous or confidential?

Protection from harm

Are there any emotional, psychological or social risks? How would they be minimised?

These four principles are always relevant. The strength of your ethical discussion comes from how specifically you apply them to your proposal.
22

Generic or actually linked?

For each ethical statement, decide: is it generic (could apply to any study) or linked to the proposal (specific and relevant)?

Fictional proposal: “An investigation into the effect of background music on concentration during a timed puzzle task, using a repeated-measures design with 20 students aged 15–16.”
“Informed consent would be obtained.”
GENERIC. True, but tells the reader nothing about how consent would work for this specific study. Who would consent? How? What would they be told?
“As participants are aged 15–16, parental consent would be sought. A letter would be sent home explaining the study involves listening to music while completing a puzzle task, and that participation is voluntary.”
LINKED TO PROPOSAL. Specific to the age group, specific to the task, explains what the letter would say — directly connected to the proposal.
“Participants would be debriefed after the study.”
GENERIC. Debriefing is standard — but what would the debrief involve? Does it need to address deception? Does it need to explain the true aim? If there was no deception, the debrief may be simple.
“As there is no deception in this proposal (participants would know they are doing a puzzle task with music), the debrief would thank participants and explain the aim of comparing concentration across music conditions.”
LINKED TO PROPOSAL. Connects the debrief to the specific features of the proposal (no deception, specific aim) — this is a thoughtful, proposal-specific ethical safeguard.
“Data would be stored securely and anonymised.”
GENERIC. A standard statement — but how would this work in practice? Would names be replaced with codes? Where would data be stored?
“Each participant would be assigned a numerical code. The key linking codes to names would be stored separately on a password-protected file accessible only to the researcher, and destroyed after marking is complete.”
LINKED TO PROPOSAL. Explains exactly how anonymisation and storage would work for this specific study — concrete, practical, and believable.
23

Ethics scenarios

For each scenario, decide which ethical issue is most relevant.

“You want to survey 14-year-olds about their social media use.”
PARENTAL CONSENT NEEDED. Participants are under 16 — parental/guardian consent is required before participation.
“You plan to ask participants about traumatic personal experiences.”
RISK OF HARM. Sensitive topics can cause emotional distress — the proposal needs to explain how harm would be minimised (e.g., support contacts, right to skip questions).
“You plan to tell participants the study is about ‘problem-solving’ when it is actually about conforming to group pressure.”
DECEPTION INVOLVED. The true aim is hidden from participants — this must be justified and a full debrief must be included in the procedure.
“You are conducting a 45-minute interview and worry participants may want to leave halfway through.”
RIGHT TO WITHDRAW. Longer procedures increase the chance participants may want to stop — the proposal should explain what happens to their data if they withdraw.
24

Deception: when is it used?

Examiner lesson

Deception is not automatically required. It is only appropriate when the true aim of the study would invalidate the results if participants knew (e.g., conformity, obedience, certain memory studies). If your proposal does not involve deception, do not discuss it as though it does.

Deception may be needed

Conformity studies, obedience studies, memory studies where knowing the true aim would change behaviour.

Deception is not needed

Interviews, questionnaires, observations of natural behaviour, experiments where participants know the task.

If deception is not involved: state this clearly. “No deception is involved in this proposal as participants would be fully informed about the task.” This shows ethical awareness without importing requirements from studies that do use deception.
25

Vulnerable populations

Examiner lesson

Some proposals involve participants who may be considered vulnerable — children, young people under 16, students with additional needs, or individuals who may experience emotional distress. These proposals require additional ethical consideration.

Under 16

Parental consent usually required. Assent from the participant also needed.

Emotionally sensitive topics

Safeguarding measures, debrief support, right to skip questions or withdraw.

Additional needs

Accessible materials, adapted procedures, additional support if needed.

Do not simply say “vulnerable populations would be protected.” Explain who is vulnerable in your proposal, why, and what specific measures would be taken.
26

Learn from real IA marking — ethics

Examiner lesson

Examiners have flagged that some ethical discussions are too generic. Students write “informed consent would be obtained” without explaining how, or “participants would be debriefed” without saying what the debrief would involve.

Weak vs strong ethics

Weak ethics

“Informed consent would be obtained. Participants would have the right to withdraw. Data would be kept confidential.”

Strong ethics

“As participants are 14–15, parental consent would be sought via a letter sent home, explaining the study involves a short questionnaire about study habits. Participant assent would also be obtained. Participants would be told they can stop at any time without explanation, and any incomplete data would be destroyed. Names would be replaced with codes; only the researcher would have access to the key.”

The difference: the strong version is specific to the proposal — age, method, procedure, data type. The weak version could apply to any study.
27

Method → Procedure → Ethics: the alignment flow

Method What type of study would it be?
↓
Procedure How would the method actually work?
↓
Ethics What ethical issues arise from the method and procedure?

The three components of Criterion B should align. The method determines the procedure. The procedure determines the ethics. If the method changes, the procedure and ethics should change too.

Do not treat ethics as a separate, generic section. The ethical discussion should emerge from the method and procedure you have already described.
28

Find the mismatch

In each example, something in the methodology is misaligned. Identify the problem.

“Method: interview about personal experiences of anxiety. Ethics: participants would be debriefed about the true aim of the study.”
MISMATCH. If participants were already told the true aim and no deception occurred, a debrief specifically intended to reveal the “true aim” would not normally be needed. The larger lesson: ethical safeguards should match the actual method and procedure — not be imported from studies involving deception.
“Method: questionnaire. Procedure: participants would be observed completing the questionnaire in pairs.”
MISMATCH. The method says questionnaire, but the procedure describes observation. The method and procedure must be aligned — choose one and describe it consistently.
“Method: repeated-measures experiment comparing two conditions. Ethics: participant differences between conditions would be controlled by using different participants.”
MISMATCH. Using different participants in each condition is an independent measures design, not repeated measures. The design description contradicts the method.
“Method: observation in a school. Procedure: the researcher would approach students in the playground and ask them to complete a task.”
MISMATCH. An observation involves watching naturally occurring behaviour — asking participants to complete a task is an experiment or task-based study, not an observation.

Worked thinking example

Read this fictional proposal outline and follow the reasoning process.

Fictional proposal outline

Research question: Does background music improve or reduce concentration during a timed task?

Method: Experiment (repeated measures)

Participants: 20 students aged 15–16 from the same year group

Step-by-step thinking

Step 1: Choose the method An experiment allows comparison of conditions — repeated measures means the same students do both tasks
↓
Step 2: Design Repeated measures — same participants in both conditions. Counterbalance order to reduce order effects
↓
Step 3: Sample 20 students aged 15–16, recruited from the same year group, via a school-wide invitation
↓
Step 4: Setting A quiet classroom — consistent environment for both conditions
↓
Step 5: Procedure Recruit → consent → Condition A (music) → Condition B (silence) or vice versa → debrief. Each condition: 10-minute timed puzzle, same instructions
↓
Step 6: Ethics Parental consent (under 16), right to withdraw, no deception, anonymised data storage. Debrief explains the aim after participation
Notice how each step follows from the previous one. The method determines the design. The design determines the procedure. The procedure determines the ethics. This is alignment.

Build it or break it

For each fictional scenario, decide: is this methodology well-built or does it break at some point?

“A questionnaire about study habits, distributed online to 30 students aged 16–17, with parental consent, anonymous responses, and a debrief explaining the aim.”
WELL-BUILT. Method matches procedure (online questionnaire), sample is appropriate, ethics are linked to the proposal (parental consent for age, anonymity, debrief).
“A repeated-measures experiment comparing two revision methods, using two different classes.”
BREAKS. Repeated measures requires the same participants in both conditions, but two different classes means different participants. This is an independent measures design, not repeated measures.
“An interview about experiences of moving schools, with 8 participants recruited via the pastoral office, in a private room, with consent, right to withdraw, and anonymised transcripts.”
WELL-BUILT. Method, procedure, sample and ethics all align. The pastoral office is a realistic recruitment route. Private room is appropriate for sensitive interviews.
“A naturalistic observation of bullying behaviour in the school playground, with full debrief about the true aim and debriefing of all observed participants.”
BREAKS. A naturalistic observation of bullying cannot involve a debrief of observed participants — they would not know they were being observed. The ethics have been imported from a different type of study.

Common mistakes

Mismatch between method and design

Calling it a “repeated measures” experiment but describing two different classes — that is independent measures.

Generic ethics

“Informed consent would be obtained” with no explanation of who, how, or what they would be told.

Missing practical detail

“A questionnaire would be used” — but no sample size, no recruitment, no procedure, no data protection.

Importing ethics from other studies

Discussing debriefing for a study that involves no deception, or discussing consent for naturalistic observation of public behaviour.

Inventing requirements

Saying “the sample must be 50+” or “every proposal must use an experiment” — IB does not set these requirements.

Inconsistent language

Switching between past tense (“participants completed”) and future proposal language (“participants would complete”).
