1 What IB Requires — Criterion D 6 marks

Criterion D assesses three separate components:

Potential Findings + Policy/Practice

Describe plausible potential findings in detail. Explain realistic implications for policy/practice.

Researcher Bias

Discuss one or more relevant examples of HOW researcher bias may affect the investigation.

One Additional Research Method

Discuss the usefulness of ONE relevant additional method, specifically explaining how it could increase understanding of the area.

A strong Criterion D must address all three components. Omitting any one component will limit the marks available.
2

What changes as the marks go up?

Do not memorise rubric text — understand the climb.

Name it Describe it Explain it Link it
1–2 Lower level
  • POTENTIAL FINDINGS: Findings described, but policy/practice implications not addressed.
  • RESEARCHER BIAS: Bias identified.
  • ADDITIONAL METHOD: Relevant additional method described, but no link to increased understanding.
3–4 Middle level
  • POTENTIAL FINDINGS: Findings described; implications partially addressed.
  • RESEARCHER BIAS: Relevant bias described.
  • ADDITIONAL METHOD: Usefulness discussed, but still no explicit link to increased understanding.
5–6 Strongest level
  • POTENTIAL FINDINGS — DESCRIBED IN DETAIL; IMPLICATIONS EXPLAINED
  • RESEARCHER BIAS — HOW BIAS MAY AFFECT THE INVESTIGATION IS DISCUSSED
  • ADDITIONAL METHOD — USEFULNESS DISCUSSED WITH EXPLICIT REFERENCE TO HOW IT INCREASES UNDERSTANDING

The climb is:

NAME

→

DESCRIBE

→

EXPLAIN EFFECT / VALUE

Show how it matters for this investigation.

3

How to build Criterion D

Work through the three components in a logical order. Each feeds the next.

Potential Findings What could the proposed investigation plausibly find?
↓
What could they mean? Interpret the possible patterns
↓
Policy / practice implication What specifically might change?
↓
Researcher bias How could the researcher affect the investigation?
↓
How could it affect data / interpretation? The effect on the investigation
↓
Additional method What different information could another method provide?
↓
How would that increase understanding? What new questions could it answer?
Recommended planning structure. IB does not require a fixed paragraph order. Use the order that lets the elements connect logically.
4

The study has not happened

Core principle

Students are discussing what the proposed investigation COULD plausibly find. They should not invent actual data. The study has not been conducted.

Potential findings could include:

A relationship

Variables may be associated in a particular direction.

No relationship

The variables may not be associated.

A difference

Groups or conditions may differ in a particular way.

Themes / patterns

Recurring experiences, perspectives, or behaviours may emerge.

Students should not assume their expected finding must occur. A strong Criterion D considers what the data could show — including alternative or mixed findings.
5

Potential finding or fake result?

For each statement, decide: is this an appropriate potential finding or overclaimed / written as an actual result?

“The questionnaire could reveal a negative relationship between hours of social media use and self-reported sleep quality.”
APPROPRIATE. Uses cautious language (“could reveal”), describes a plausible direction, does not invent numbers.
“The questionnaire showed a strong negative correlation (r = −.72, p < .001) between social media use and sleep quality.”
OVERCLAIMED. This invents statistics and presents them as factual results. The study has not been conducted.
“Interviews could surface recurring themes around students feeling disconnected from peers after moving schools.”
APPROPRIATE. Uses method-appropriate language (themes), cautious framing (“could surface”), plausible content.
“The study proved that social media causes sleep problems in teenagers.”
OVERCLAIMED. “Proved” and “causes” are factual claims. A proposal discusses what could be found, not what has been proven.
“The observation could show that students who moved schools initiate conversation less frequently during break time.”
APPROPRIATE. Method-appropriate (observation of behaviour), specific direction, cautious language.
“Our findings conclusively demonstrate that mindfulness breaks improve concentration.”
OVERCLAIMED. “Conclusively demonstrate” presents findings as established fact. Proposal language requires “could” or “may.”
6

Findings must match the method

Potential findings must be the kind of findings the proposed method could actually produce.

Questionnaire

patterns, relationships, distributions, self-reported differences

Interview

themes, recurring experiences, contrasting perspectives

Observation

behavioural patterns, frequencies, differences in observed behaviour

Experiment

differences between conditions or groups where appropriate

Do NOT make students discuss statistical tests unless their proposal genuinely calls for that level of detail.
7

Method-finding match

For each scenario, decide: does the method match the kind of finding described?

“Method: semi-structured interview. Finding: recurring themes around feelings of isolation.”
MATCH. Interviews produce thematic data. Themes of isolation are a plausible interview finding.
“Method: semi-structured interview. Finding: the study could prove a causal relationship between X and Y.”
MISMATCH. Interviews do not “prove” causal relationships. They explore experiences and perspectives, not causal mechanisms.
“Method: questionnaire. Finding: a negative association between social media use and self-reported well-being.”
MATCH. Questionnaires measure associations between self-reported variables. This is a plausible questionnaire finding.
“Method: naturalistic observation. Finding: participants reported higher motivation after the intervention.”
MISMATCH. Observations record behaviour, not self-reported motivation. This finding belongs to a questionnaire or interview.
“Method: experiment. Finding: students in the music condition scored higher on the concentration task than those in silence.”
MATCH. Experiments compare conditions. A difference between conditions is a plausible experiment finding.
“Method: questionnaire. Finding: the observation revealed that students checking phones were less engaged.”
MISMATCH. A questionnaire cannot produce observation data. The finding must match the method proposed.
8

Detail does not mean inventing numbers

WHAT DOES “DESCRIBED IN DETAIL” MEAN?

Detailed potential findings do NOT require students to invent fake:

  • means
  • percentages
  • correlations
  • p-values
  • participant quotes

Instead, detail can come from explaining:

What pattern might emerge

The direction, nature, or shape of the finding

Which variables / groups / behaviours would differ or relate

The specific elements involved

Possible directions and alternative outcomes

What else the data could show

What those outcomes would suggest

The meaning behind the pattern

9

Vague or detailed enough?

For each statement, decide: is this too vague or detailed enough to understand?

“The findings might show something about social media.”
TOO VAGUE. “Something about social media” tells us nothing about what the findings could show.
“The questionnaire could show that students reporting more frequent appearance-based comparison also tend to report lower self-esteem.”
DETAILED ENOUGH. Names the variables, specifies the direction, describes the type of association.
“Interviews could provide useful data.”
TOO VAGUE. “Useful data” tells us nothing about what the data could show.
“Interviews could reveal that students who recently moved schools describe feeling like outsiders during their first month, with some reporting that peer support helped them adjust.”
DETAILED ENOUGH. Describes specific themes, a time frame, contrasting experiences, and a plausible direction.
“The observation could find some interesting results.”
TOO VAGUE. “Interesting results” is meaningless without specifying what behaviour, what pattern, and what direction.
“The observation could show that students who moved schools spend more time alone during break and initiate fewer conversations with peers than students who have been at the school longer.”
DETAILED ENOUGH. Specifies behaviours, groups, comparison, and direction. The reader can picture what the data could show.
10

From finding to implication

A potential finding by itself is not enough. Students must ask: SO WHAT?

Potential finding What could the data show?
↓
What could this suggest? The meaning behind the pattern
↓
Who could use this information? Schools, teachers, counsellors, app designers, etc.
↓
What could change in policy or practice? A specific, justified action

Possible contexts might include school policy, teaching practice, mental-health support, workplace practice, app/platform design, public-health communication, or community programmes — depending on the actual proposal.

Do NOT require both policy AND practice if one relevant implication is better developed. The guide wording is “policy/practice.”
11

Policy / practice — what does that mean?

Policy

Formal guidance, rules, procedures, institutional decisions. Example: “School introduces guidance around...”

Practice

What people or organisations actually do. Example: “Teachers change how they...”

An implication should logically follow from the potential finding. Do not jump from weak evidence to sweeping recommendations.
12

Finding → implication match

For each pair, decide: does the implication logically follow from the potential finding?

Finding: The questionnaire could show that students who use social media more frequently report lower sleep quality. Implication: Schools could review whether current screen-time guidance accounts for the link between social media use and sleep.
LOGICAL. The finding connects social media to sleep. The implication suggests a specific policy response that addresses this link.
Finding: The questionnaire could show that students who use social media more report lower sleep quality. Implication: Schools should ban all mobile phones.
DOES NOT FOLLOW. A blanket phone ban is a disproportionate response to an association between social media use and sleep. The implication must be proportionate to the finding.
Finding: Interviews could reveal that students who moved schools felt excluded during their first month. Implication: Schools could develop a structured induction programme for newly arrived students that includes peer-buddy support.
LOGICAL. The finding identifies a specific problem (exclusion during first month). The implication addresses it with a targeted intervention.
Finding: The observation could show that students who moved schools initiate fewer conversations. Implication: All teachers should receive mental health training.
DOES NOT FOLLOW. Mental health training is too broad a response to a finding about peer conversation. The implication should be more targeted.
Finding: The experiment could show that students who revised with flashcards recalled more vocabulary than those who re-read notes. Implication: Teachers could incorporate flashcard-based revision strategies into classroom practice, particularly for vocabulary-heavy subjects.
LOGICAL. The finding directly supports a specific teaching practice. The implication is proportionate and specific.
Finding: The questionnaire could show no relationship between social media use and well-being. Implication: Schools should introduce new well-being programmes specifically addressing social media harm.
DOES NOT FOLLOW. If the finding shows no relationship, an implication about social media harm does not follow. Consider what a null finding could mean instead.
13

Describing vs explaining an implication

Weak: “Schools could provide training.”

Stronger thinking asks:

WHAT training?

Be specific about the type of intervention

FOR WHOM?

Who would receive it?

WHY would that finding justify it?

Connect the implication to the specific finding

WHAT might it change?

What outcome would it aim for?

Described or explained?

“Schools could change their policy on social media use.”
DESCRIBED. This names a possible change but does not explain what specifically would change, why, or how it would address the finding.
“If the finding showed that students using social media at bedtime reported worse sleep, the school could introduce guidance recommending no social media use in the hour before sleep — a specific, practicable change justified by the link between evening screen time and sleep quality.”
EXPLAINED. Names the specific policy change, connects it to the finding, and explains why it follows.
“Teachers should be more aware of transition issues.”
DESCRIBED. Too vague. What does “more aware” mean? What would teachers actually do differently?
“If interviews revealed that students who moved schools felt excluded, teachers could implement a structured peer-buddy system during the first month — specifically pairing new students with existing classmates to facilitate social integration, directly addressing the isolation described by participants.”
EXPLAINED. Specific action, specific mechanism, direct connection to the finding.
“The findings could be useful for app designers.”
DESCRIBED. This is too vague. What would app designers do differently? What specific change follows from the finding?
“If the data showed that appearance-based comparison on image-sharing platforms was associated with lower self-esteem, app developers could consider adding prompts that interrupt passive scrolling and encourage active, positive engagement — a design change directly targeting the comparison mechanism identified in the findings.”
EXPLAINED. Names a specific design change, explains the mechanism it targets, and connects directly to the finding.

In one official example, several school-practice implications were mentioned but were not developed enough for the top band.

Fictional example

15

Avoid causal overclaims

The implication must respect what the research method can establish.

If a questionnaire finds an association

Do NOT automatically write: “X causes Y.”

If an observation shows a pattern

Do NOT automatically write: “X caused the behaviour.”

If an experiment is appropriately designed

Stronger causal interpretation may be possible, but still avoid sweeping claims beyond the proposal.

Supported or overclaimed?

“If the questionnaire shows a negative association between social media use and sleep quality, schools could review current guidance on screen time before bed.”
SUPPORTED. The implication (review guidance) is proportionate to an association finding. It does not claim causation.
“The questionnaire proves that social media causes sleep problems, so all students should be banned from using phones at night.”
OVERCLAIMED. A questionnaire cannot prove causation. A blanket ban is disproportionate to an association finding.
“If interviews reveal that students who moved schools felt excluded, schools could develop a peer-support programme for newly arrived students.”
SUPPORTED. The finding identifies a specific experience. The programme directly addresses it.
“The experiment conclusively proves that flashcards are the best revision method and all schools must adopt them.”
OVERCLAIMED. “Conclusively proves” and “best revision method” go beyond what a single study with one sample can establish.
“The observation shows that phone checking causes poor academic performance, so phones must be removed from school entirely.”
OVERCLAIMED. An observation shows correlation, not causation. A blanket policy response is disproportionate to an observational finding.
16

Part 2 — How could the researcher affect the investigation?

Criterion D is not asking students merely to define researcher bias. They need to discuss one or more relevant examples of HOW researcher bias could affect their proposed investigation.

Source of researcher bias What is the bias?
↓
Where it could enter Which part of the investigation?
↓
How it could affect what happens The mechanism of influence
↓
How it could influence data / interpretation The consequence for the investigation
17

Researcher bias ≠ participant bias

Classify each statement as researcher bias, participant/response bias, or other methodological issue.

“The interviewer asks follow-up questions differently because they expect a certain answer.”
RESEARCHER BIAS. The researcher’s expectations influence how they probe, which could steer participant responses.
“A participant gives a socially desirable answer rather than a truthful one.”
PARTICIPANT / RESPONSE BIAS. This is a participant tendency, not researcher influence.
“The observer notices behaviours that confirm their expectation more readily.”
RESEARCHER BIAS. Selective attention based on expectations is a researcher bias (confirmation bias in observation).
“A participant becomes tired during a long questionnaire.”
OTHER METHODOLOGICAL ISSUE. Fatigue is a participant experience, not researcher bias. It may affect data quality but is not caused by researcher influence.
“The researcher interprets ambiguous interview responses in line with their expectations.”
RESEARCHER BIAS. The researcher’s interpretation is shaped by expectations, not by the data alone.
18

Where can researcher bias enter?

These are possible examples, not mandatory lists.

Experiment

Expectations affecting instructions or treatment, selective attention to outcomes, interpretation choices.

Interview

Leading tone/wording, inconsistent probing, interpretation of responses.

Observation

Observer expectations, selective recording, ambiguous behavioural coding.

Questionnaire / Survey

Leading item construction, selective interpretation, design choices reflecting researcher expectations.

19

Generic or specific to the proposal?

For each statement, decide: is this a generic bias statement or linked to this investigation?

“Researcher bias could affect the results.”
GENERIC. This could apply to any study. It does not explain how bias could enter this specific investigation.
“Because the researcher expects students using flashcards to perform better, they may unconsciously give clearer instructions to the flashcard condition, creating a confound.”
LINKED. Names the specific bias, explains where it enters (instructions), and identifies the consequence (confound).
“Observer bias is a concern in observation studies.”
GENERIC. This states a general fact about observations but does not explain how it could affect this specific study.
“Because the researcher expects students who moved schools to show less social interaction, ambiguous behaviours such as standing alone at break may be more likely to be coded as ‘isolated’ rather than ‘resting.’”
LINKED. Names the expectation, explains how it could influence coding, and gives a specific example of the effect.
“The researcher’s position could introduce bias.”
GENERIC. Vague. What aspect of the researcher’s position? How would it enter this specific investigation?
“Because the researcher is a teacher at the same school, they may know some participants personally, which could influence how they probe certain responses during interviews.”
LINKED. Names the specific source (teacher-student relationship), explains the mechanism (probing), and connects to the investigation.
20

Identifying bias is not enough

Weak: “Researcher bias could affect the results.”

Weak

“Observer bias may occur.”

≠

Discussion requires

BIAS → HOW IT COULD OCCUR HERE → WHAT PART OF THE INVESTIGATION IT COULD AFFECT → WHAT CONSEQUENCE THIS COULD HAVE

Identified or discussed?

“Social desirability bias may affect the questionnaire data.”
IDENTIFIED ONLY. This names the bias but does not explain how it could occur in this investigation or what effect it could have.
“Social desirability could affect the questionnaire because students may over-report positive behaviours and under-report negative ones, particularly on items about sensitive topics such as substance use. This could inflate agreement with socially desirable statements and reduce the accuracy of the data.”
DISCUSSED. Explains WHERE (sensitive items), WHY (social acceptability), and HOW (inflate agreement, reduce accuracy).
“Interviewer bias could be a problem.”
IDENTIFIED ONLY. Names the issue but gives no explanation of how it could enter this specific interview or what effect it could have.
“Because the researcher expects students who moved schools to report more negative experiences, they may unconsciously probe negative responses more deeply while accepting positive responses without follow-up. This could create an unbalanced picture of transition experiences in the interview data.”
DISCUSSED. Explains the source of bias (expectation), the mechanism (selective probing), and the consequence (unbalanced data).
“Observer bias could influence the observation.”
IDENTIFIED ONLY. This is a label, not a discussion. How could observer bias enter this observation? What behaviours could be affected?
“If the researcher expects students who moved schools to initiate fewer conversations, they may be more likely to code ambiguous social behaviours (such as a brief greeting) as insignificant, while recording the same behaviours by other students as meaningful interactions. This could systematically underestimate social interaction in the target group.”
DISCUSSED. Explains the expectation, the specific coding mechanism, and the consequence for the data.
21

Learn from real IA marking — researcher bias

Examiner lesson

Official examiner feedback distinguishes between:

  • simply describing examples of researcher bias
  • discussing HOW those biases may affect the investigation

A response can identify sensible biases yet remain below the top band if it never explains their likely impact.

What is missing?

“Observer bias could affect the observation because the researcher may have expectations about participant behaviour.”

≠

The effect on this investigation

WHICH behaviours could be affected? HOW? WHAT consequence for the data?

22

Do not turn this into a solutions list

The main Criterion D requirement is to discuss HOW researcher bias may affect the investigation.

Students may mention safeguards where useful, but the page should NOT reduce this component to “bias + solution.”

Bias What is the source of bias?
→
Effect How could it affect the investigation?
Do NOT require discussion of mitigation for every bias. The criterion asks for the EFFECT, not the solution.
23

Part 3 — What could another method add?

Students must choose ONE relevant additional research method. It should be additional to the method already proposed in Criterion B.

Possible methods:

Experiment

controlled comparison, causal evidence

Interview

depth, perspectives, reasoning

Observation

directly observed behaviour, context

Survey / Questionnaire

standardised responses, broader patterns

The purpose is NOT simply to say ‘another method would be better.’ The purpose is to ask: WHAT DIFFERENT KIND OF INFORMATION COULD IT PROVIDE? and HOW WOULD THAT INCREASE UNDERSTANDING OF THE AREA?
24

Not “better” — different

The additional method does not need to replace the original method. It can complement it.

Original method Gives one type of understanding
↓
Additional method Adds another type of understanding
↓
Together Broader / deeper / more contextual / more causal / more directly observable understanding
Do NOT force the word “triangulation” unless it genuinely fits and is explained.
25

What each method can add

Experiment

Can add controlled comparison, stronger causal evidence, or allow manipulation of variables that cannot be changed in a survey.

Interview

Can add depth, personal perspective, explanation, and richer understanding of experiences behind statistical patterns.

Observation

Can add directly observed behaviour, ecological validity, or an account of what actually happens in context rather than what people say.

Survey / Questionnaire

Can add breadth, standardised comparison, or a wider picture across more participants.

These are general possibilities, not a checklist. The key question is: WHAT DIFFERENT INFORMATION could this method give, and HOW would it help you understand the area better?

26

Does the additional method fit?

For each proposed additional method, decide: does it fit this investigation?

The main method is a questionnaire about sleep habits. The additional method is structured observation of bedtime routines at home.
FITS. The questionnaire gives self-report; the observation gives directly observed behaviour in the same area. They complement each other.
The main method is a questionnaire about stress levels. The additional method is semi-structured interviews about stress experiences.
FITS. The questionnaire gives breadth; interviews give depth on the same topic. Good complement.
The main method is a questionnaire about language learning strategies. The additional method is a laboratory experiment measuring reaction times.
WEAK FIT. Reaction times measure something quite different from self-reported learning strategies. The connection to the original question is unclear.
The main method is structured observation of social interaction on the playground. The additional method is a short questionnaire about friendships.
FITS. Observation gives behaviour; questionnaire gives perception. Together they give a fuller picture.
The main method is an interview about student wellbeing. The additional method is an experiment on memory recall.
WEAK FIT. Memory recall is not directly connected to wellbeing. It is hard to see how this increases understanding of the area.
27

Increase understanding — the critical phrase

The criterion requires the student to show how the additional method could increase understanding of the area.

This does NOT mean:

Triangulation alone

Saying “this would triangulate the data” is not enough without explaining what that means for this investigation.

“More data”

The question is not whether there is more data, but whether the additional method gives a different type of understanding.

Comparison alone

Simply saying “the results could be compared” does not explain WHY the comparison would increase understanding.

Usefulness or increased understanding?

“The questionnaire would give broader data, while the interviews would help explain why participants feel that way — giving both breadth and depth to the understanding of transition experiences.”
INCREASED UNDERSTANDING. Explains what different information each method gives and how they together deepen understanding of the area.
“Using both methods would be useful because more data is always better.”
SHOWS USEFULNESS. This says more data is good but does not explain what type of understanding the second method adds.
“The observation would show actual playground behaviour, while the questionnaire would reveal how students perceive their own social skills. This could increase understanding because it would show whether what students say about their social lives matches what they actually do.”
INCREASED UNDERSTANDING. Specifies what each method adds, and explains what the comparison would reveal.
“An interview would be a useful addition to the questionnaire.”
SHOWS USEFULNESS. “Useful” is vague. What specific understanding would the interview add?
“The two methods would triangulate the data, which would strengthen the findings.”
SHOWS USEFULNESS. “Triangulate” and “strengthen” are vague without explaining what specific new understanding the additional method would bring.
“The structured observation would capture actual social behaviour, while the questionnaire would reveal students’ perceptions. This could increase understanding by showing whether students’ self-reported social confidence matches their observable behaviour — a gap that neither method alone could reveal.”
INCREASED UNDERSTANDING. Explains the specific gap each method addresses and what the combination reveals that neither alone could.

Original vs additional — what each brings

For each pair, identify what the original method gives and what the additional method could add.

Original: questionnaire

Breadth, self-report, standardised responses

Additional: interview

Depth, personal reasoning, richer detail about individual experiences

Original: interview

Depth, perspective, reasoning behind behaviour

Additional: questionnaire

Breadth, standardised comparison, wider pattern across participants

Original: experiment

Controlled comparison, potential causal evidence

Ecological validity, directly observed behaviour in natural context

Directly captured behaviour, natural setting

30

Don’t redesign the original study

Part 3 is not asking students to change their proposed method.

Not this

“I should have used an interview instead.”

This

“What additional understanding could ANOTHER method provide?”

31

The three-part alignment

In strong responses, the three parts of Criterion D connect:

1. Potential findings & implications What COULD the data show, and what might it mean?
↓
2. Researcher bias What aspects of the researcher could influence the investigation?
↓
3. Additional method What could another method add to understanding?
These three sections should all relate to the same proposed investigation. They should feel connected, not like three separate tasks.
32

Find the mismatch

For each response, identify what is wrong with the alignment.

The student proposes a questionnaire about sleep. The potential findings discuss sleep. But the researcher bias section discusses leading questions in interviews — and the investigation does not use interviews.
MISMATCHED. The bias discussed is for a method not used in the investigation. Researcher bias must relate to the proposed method.
The student proposes an observation of playground social interaction. The potential findings discuss social patterns. The researcher bias section discusses observer expectations influencing coding. The additional method is a questionnaire about friendships.
ALIGNED. All three parts relate to the same investigation. The bias is relevant to observations. The questionnaire complements the observation.
The student proposes an experiment on memory. The implications discuss educational policy. But the additional method is a questionnaire — and the student does not explain what the questionnaire would add to understanding of memory.
MISMATCHED. The additional method is named but not explained. There is no account of what different information it would provide.
The student proposes a questionnaire about wellbeing. The implications are about wellbeing support. But the researcher bias section only identifies social desirability without discussing HOW it could affect THIS questionnaire.
MISMATCHED. The bias is identified but not discussed in relation to this specific investigation.
33

Worked example — thinking through one proposal

This is a fictional example to show how the three parts connect. It is not a model to copy.

Proposal

An investigation into whether student wellbeing differs between those who have moved school and those who have not, using semi-structured interviews.

Potential findings

One potential finding could be that students who moved school describe feelings of isolation during their first term. A possible implication could be that schools develop a structured induction programme for newly arrived students.

Researcher bias

Because the researcher expects to hear more negative experiences from students who moved, they could unconsciously probe negative themes more deeply while accepting positive responses without follow-up. This could create an unbalanced picture of transition experiences.

Additional method

A structured observation of playground interaction could add directly observed social behaviour, complementing the self-report data from interviews. This could increase understanding by showing whether the social patterns described in interviews match what actually happens in practice.

34

Build it or break it

For each example, decide: does this meet the Criterion D requirements or not?

“A potential finding could be that students who moved report feeling less connected. An implication could be that schools develop peer-support programmes. Researcher bias could enter because I expect negative responses, so I may probe difficult topics more. An additional method could be an observation to see whether described isolation matches actual social behaviour.”
MEETS. All three parts are present, relevant to the same investigation, and show understanding of potential findings, researcher bias, and what the additional method could add.
“My results would be valid and reliable. The data would be accurate. Researcher bias could be a problem. An interview would be a good addition.”
DOES NOT MEET. Too vague. No specific potential findings, no discussion of how bias could affect the investigation, no explanation of what the additional method would add.
“The questionnaire would give valid data. Researcher bias could occur if I ask leading questions. The interview would triangulate.”
DOES NOT MEET. No potential findings or implications. Researcher bias is identified but not discussed. “Triangulate” is used without explaining what that means for this investigation.

37

Common mistakes

  • treating potential findings as actual results
  • making causal claims beyond what the method could establish
  • identifying researcher bias without discussing how it could affect the investigation
  • listing types of bias rather than discussing specific effects
  • saying the additional method would “add more data” without explaining what kind of understanding it would bring
  • comparing methods by saying one is better rather than explaining what different information each provides
  • using “triangulation” without explaining what it means in the specific investigation
  • disconnecting the three parts of the discussion so they feel like separate tasks
