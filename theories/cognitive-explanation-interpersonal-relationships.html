Cognitive Explanation of Interpersonal Relationships OUR CHOSEN MODEL

Cognitive explanations of interpersonal relationships propose that relationships are influenced not only by what other people actually do, but also by how we perceive, interpret, remember, and make judgments about their behaviour. People use existing expectations and schemas to interpret social information, and these interpretations influence the attributions they make about another person's behaviour and therefore how they respond to that person.

What we expect from another person can influence how we interpret their behaviour, and those interpretations can shape how the relationship develops.
Real-life example: A teacher who already believes a student is highly capable hears an unclear answer in class and interprets it as thoughtful but poorly expressed. The same ambiguous answer from a student expected to struggle might instead be read as confusion. The behaviour is ambiguous; the expectation shapes its interpretation.

What does the theory say?

Cognitive explanations of interpersonal relationships propose that relationships are shaped by how people interpret one another. The sequence runs: an existing schema or expectation → encountering another person's behaviour → interpreting that behaviour → making a judgment or attribution → responding to the person. That response may influence later interactions, so the original expectation can be reinforced — or challenged by new information.

Schemas and expectations: Schemas are organised mental frameworks based on previous knowledge and experience. In interpersonal relationships, schemas can include expectations about particular people, social roles, groups, and relationships. These expectations influence what information we notice and how we interpret ambiguous behaviour.

For example, a teacher already believes that a particular student is highly capable. The student gives an unclear answer in class. Because the teacher expects the student to be capable, the teacher may interpret the answer as thoughtful but poorly expressed. If the teacher had expected the student to struggle academically, the same ambiguous answer might instead be interpreted as evidence that the student does not understand. The behaviour itself is ambiguous; the pre-existing expectation influences its interpretation. When expectations systematically favour one interpretation in this way — for instance, noticing mistakes more readily from a student expected to perform poorly — this illustrates Bias in social judgment.

Interpretation becomes judgment through attribution. An attribution is an explanation we make about why someone behaved in a particular way. A dispositional attribution explains behaviour using characteristics of the person. For example: "He ignored my message because he is inconsiderate."

A situational attribution explains behaviour using circumstances surrounding the person. For example: "He may not have replied because he was busy or did not see the message." Neither attribution is necessarily correct; both are interpretations of the same behaviour.

Consider a friend who arrives 30 minutes late. Person A makes a dispositional attribution — "She is selfish and doesn't respect my time" — and becomes angry and distant. Person B makes a situational attribution — "Her bus may have been delayed" — and responds more calmly. Same behaviour → different interpretation → different attribution → different emotional and behavioural response → potentially different effect on the relationship.

Expectations can also become self-reinforcing. A student believes a new classmate is unfriendly and therefore avoids talking to them. The classmate receives little friendliness and does not initiate conversation, which the student interprets as confirmation: "I knew they were unfriendly." The cycle runs expectation → interpretation → behaviour toward the other person → the other person's response → apparent confirmation of the expectation. This is only a possible process, however: expectations do not always become self-fulfilling, because new information can challenge and change schemas.

How does the evidence support it?

Darley and Gross (1983) investigated whether expectations about a child's socioeconomic background would influence judgments of her academic ability. Participants viewed information about a young girl named Hannah, presented as coming from either a relatively high or a relatively low socioeconomic-status context, and then evaluated information about her academic performance. Crucially, participants were given additional ambiguous evidence about her performance, so their earlier expectations could influence how that evidence was interpreted. Because expectations were directly manipulated in a controlled experiment, findings of this kind can support causal claims (Causality) about expectations affecting judgments — although in real relationships influence runs in both directions.

Participants' judgments became more consistent with the socioeconomic expectations created earlier: when ambiguous performance information was available, those led to expect higher ability interpreted Hannah's performance more positively, while those given lower-status expectations interpreted the same or ambiguous evidence less positively. The sequence is prior expectation → interpretation of ambiguous information → judgment of another person. This supports the cognitive explanation because the judgment was shaped not simply by Hannah's behaviour but by the schema through which it was interpreted.

This result should not be oversimplified as "rich Hannah was judged intelligent and poor Hannah unintelligent." The socioeconomic information created expectations, but those expectations became particularly influential when participants had ambiguous behavioural evidence to interpret. That nuance matters: it shows schemas influencing interpretation, rather than merely showing that people hold stereotypes.

Darley and Gross demonstrate that expectations can influence judgments under particular experimental conditions. The study does not prove that schemas determine every interpersonal judgment, that people always follow prior expectations, that expectations cannot change, or that cognition is the only influence on relationships. Relationships are also shaped by actual behaviour, communication, emotion, personality, social context, cultural expectations, and previous interactions — and laboratory judgments of an unfamiliar person are not identical to long-term real relationships.

Strengths

The explanation shows HOW previous knowledge and expectations can influence interpersonal judgments — through schemas, interpretation, and attribution — rather than simply describing differences between relationships. This cognitive mechanism is its central strength.

Research such as Darley and Gross (1983) demonstrates that expectations can systematically influence the interpretation of ambiguous social information, giving the explanation controlled experimental support.

The explanation applies to teachers and students, friendships, romantic partners, workplaces, and first impressions — wherever ambiguous behaviour must be interpreted.

Limitations

A purely cognitive explanation may underestimate emotional, biological, cultural, and situational influences on relationships. This reductionism means cognition alone cannot carry the full weight of real relationship behaviour.

Outside controlled experiments, schemas may influence interactions, but interactions also modify schemas. Expectations influence interactions ↔ interactions modify expectations, so influence in real relationships is reciprocal rather than one-directional.

People do not interpret identical social information in exactly the same way, and the explanation cannot precisely predict how strongly a particular schema will influence a particular person. These individual differences limit what the model can forecast.

Experimental judgments about strangers or hypothetical situations may not capture the complexity, emotional investment, and history of long-term interpersonal relationships. This raises questions about ecological validity.

Several misunderstandings should also be avoided: schemas influence interpretation but do not determine it; an attribution explains behaviour but may not be its true cause; ambiguous behaviour can be read differently by different people; expectations can change with contradictory experience; Darley and Gross does not prove that stereotypes determine behaviour; and cognitive processes are one level of explanation for relationships, not the whole story.

Applications

The explanation can be used to understand first impressions, interpersonal judgments, relationship expectations, and attribution in friendships, romantic relationships, and workplaces. It is a model of how people read one another, not a model of depression.

It helps explain how stereotypes can affect interactions, how ambiguous behaviour gets interpreted, and how self-reinforcing expectations develop — for example, when teacher expectations shape classroom interactions.

A complementary perspective comes from Social Learning Theory, which focuses more strongly on how relationship behaviours are learned by observing models and the consequences of their behaviour. The cognitive explanation focuses more strongly on how expectations, schemas, and interpretations influence judgments and responses within relationships.

To summarise: expectation or schema → interpretation → attribution or judgment → response → effect on the interaction. Attributions may be dispositional, located in the person, or situational, located in circumstances. Expectations can sometimes become self-reinforcing, but new information can change them. Darley and Gross (1983) shows that prior expectations can influence the interpretation of ambiguous evidence. Cognitive factors influence relationships without determining them.
