Berry's Acculturation Model Chosen Model

John Berry's model of acculturation proposes that when people come into continuous contact with a new culture, they adopt one of four strategies based on two questions: Is maintaining my original culture important? Is participating in the new culture important?

Acculturation results in one of four strategies — integration, assimilation, separation, or marginalisation — depending on whether individuals value their original culture and the new culture.
Real-life example: A Japanese student moving to Australia might choose to integrate — keeping Japanese traditions at home while adopting Australian customs at school. Another student might assimilate, abandoning Japanese culture entirely. A third might separate, avoiding Australian culture and sticking only with other Japanese students.

What does the theory say?

Berry's model (1997) proposes that acculturation — the process of psychological and cultural change that occurs when people come into continuous contact with a different culture — can result in four distinct strategies. These strategies are based on two underlying questions:

Question 1: Is it important to maintain my original cultural identity and heritage?

Question 2: Is it important to participate in and adopt the values of the new culture?

The four strategies are:

Integration: Maintaining original culture while also participating in the new culture. Considered the most adaptive strategy, associated with the best psychological outcomes.

Assimilation: Adopting the new culture while abandoning the original culture. The individual rejects their heritage culture and fully embraces the host culture.

Separation: Maintaining the original culture while rejecting participation in the new culture. The individual isolates themselves from the host culture.

Marginalisation: Rejecting both the original culture and the new culture. The individual feels disconnected from both cultures, often due to forced acculturation or discrimination.

How does the evidence support it?

A substantial body of research supports Berry's acculturation model. Berry et al. (2006) conducted a large-scale study of over 5,000 immigrants across 13 countries and found that integration was consistently associated with the best psychological outcomes, including higher self-esteem, lower levels of acculturative stress, and greater life satisfaction. This cross-cultural consistency is particularly significant because it suggests the model generalises across very different cultural contexts.

Ward and Kennedy (1999) investigated acculturative stress and found that individuals who experienced a poorer match between their heritage culture and the host culture reported significantly higher levels of psychological stress. Their research demonstrated that the process of negotiating between two cultures — rather than simply encountering a new one — is a key determinant of adjustment. This supports Berry's proposal that the interaction between heritage and host culture maintenance is central to understanding acculturation outcomes.

Akins et al. (2008) found that immigrants who adopted an assimilation strategy showed higher rates of substance use compared with those who integrated. This challenges the popular belief that fully embracing the host culture is the healthiest approach. Instead, it suggests that losing connection with one's heritage culture carries psychological risks, even when participation in the new culture is high.

Nguyen and Benet-Martínez (2013) conducted a meta-analysis of 83 studies on biculturalism and found a significant positive relationship between bicultural identity — which corresponds to Berry's integration strategy — and both psychological adjustment and sociocultural adaptation. The effect sizes were moderate, providing robust quantitative support for the superiority of integration as an acculturation strategy.

Sam and Berry (2010) reviewed two decades of acculturation research and concluded that the four-strategy model has been replicated across diverse populations, including refugees, international students, and economic migrants. However, they noted that the relative benefits of integration may be moderated by the receptiveness of the host society — when the host culture is welcoming, integration produces the best outcomes, but when discrimination is prevalent, even well-integrated individuals may struggle.

Strengths

One of the principal strengths of Berry's model is its cross-cultural validity. The four-strategy framework has been replicated across 13 countries and diverse immigrant populations, including international students, refugees, and economic migrants. This broad applicability suggests the model captures fundamental psychological processes rather than culture-specific phenomena, making it a robust tool for understanding acculturation worldwide.

The model is practically useful for psychologists, educators, and policymakers. By identifying the two key dimensions — heritage culture maintenance and host culture participation — the model provides a clear framework for designing interventions. For example, schools can create conditions that support integration by celebrating cultural diversity while also facilitating participation in the host culture, rather than implicitly pressuring assimilation.

Berry's model has strong empirical support. The relationship between integration and positive psychological outcomes has been consistently demonstrated across correlational studies, longitudinal research, and meta-analyses. The model also generates testable predictions — for instance, that marginalisation should produce the worst outcomes — which have been confirmed in multiple studies.

The model is also conceptually elegant. By reducing acculturation to two independent dimensions rather than a single continuum, it avoids the oversimplification of earlier models that treated acculturation as a linear process from heritage culture to host culture. This two-dimensional approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of how individuals navigate cultural contact.

Limitations

A significant limitation is that the model is descriptive rather than explanatory. While it identifies four acculturation strategies and their associated outcomes, it does not fully explain why a particular individual chooses one strategy over another. Factors such as personality, age, socioeconomic status, reason for migration, and the receptiveness of the host society all influence acculturation, but these are not integrated into the model itself.

The model oversimplifies the dynamic nature of acculturation. In reality, individuals do not adopt a single fixed strategy; they may shift between strategies depending on context, life stage, and circumstances. A person may integrate at work but separate at home, or may move from assimilation to integration over time as they develop a more secure bicultural identity. The model captures a snapshot rather than a process.

The model underestimates power dynamics between cultures. Berry's framework treats heritage culture maintenance and host culture participation as individual choices, but in practice, the dominant culture often determines the terms of acculturation. Host society discrimination, immigration policies, and structural inequality can force assimilation or make integration impossible, factors the model does not adequately address.

There are also methodological concerns. Much of the research supporting the model relies on self-report measures of acculturation strategy and psychological adjustment, which are subject to social desirability bias and demand characteristics. Cross-sectional designs, which dominate the literature, cannot establish the causal direction of the relationship between acculturation strategy and outcomes.

Applications

Berry's model has direct applications in educational settings. International schools and multicultural classrooms can use the framework to understand students' adjustment difficulties. For example, a student showing signs of separation — avoiding peer interaction and maintaining only heritage-culture friendships — may benefit from structured social activities that facilitate participation in the school culture without requiring abandonment of their heritage identity.

In clinical psychology, the model informs the assessment and treatment of acculturative stress. Psychologists working with immigrant clients can use the four strategies as a framework for understanding the client's cultural adjustment and identifying maladaptive patterns. Marginalised individuals, who have rejected both cultures, may be at particular risk for depression, anxiety, and identity confusion and may require targeted intervention.

The model is also relevant to organisational and workplace settings. Multinational companies can apply Berry's framework to understand the adjustment of employees on international assignments. Supporting integration — through cultural awareness training, mentoring, and inclusive workplace policies — can improve both employee wellbeing and organisational performance.

At the policy level, Berry's model has influenced approaches to immigration and multiculturalism. Countries that adopt multicultural policies — which support both heritage culture maintenance and participation in the broader society — align with the integration strategy. Research consistently shows that such policies are associated with better psychological outcomes for immigrants compared with assimilationist approaches.
