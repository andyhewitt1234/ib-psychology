Expectancy–Value Theory HL

Motivation depends on whether people expect they can succeed and whether they value the activity or its outcome.
Used in: HL Extension
Primary home: HL Extension

What Does the Theory Actually Say?

Expectancy–Value Theory proposes that motivation is influenced by two major considerations: expectancy (how likely do I think I am to succeed?) and value (how worthwhile is this task or outcome to me?). A student is generally more likely to choose, persist with, and invest effort in an activity when they both expect that they can succeed and value the activity or its outcome.

This is not a literal mathematical formula where motivation can be calculated precisely. It is a psychological framework for understanding why people choose some activities, avoid others, and persist differently.

Student A thinks: "I really want a 7 in Psychology, but I don't think I'm capable of getting one." Value is high but expectancy is low, so motivation and persistence may be weakened.

Student B thinks: "I could easily complete this task, but I don't see any reason to do it." Expectancy is high but value is low, so again motivation may be weak.

Student C thinks: "I think I can succeed, and doing well matters to me." Expectancy and value are both high, so stronger motivation, choice and persistence are more likely.

The modern expectancy–value approach is associated particularly with Jacquelynne Eccles and Allan Wigfield, whose educational and developmental framework is the version useful for IB Psychology.

Expectancy for Success

Expectancy refers to a person's belief about how well they are likely to perform on an upcoming task. It is not simply their actual ability. For example, two students may have similar mathematical ability. Student A believes: "I can solve these equations if I work carefully." Student B believes: "I'm terrible at maths. I'll probably fail." Their objective ability may be similar, but their expectations for success differ. Those expectations may influence whether they attempt the task, how much effort they invest, how long they persist, and what they choose in future. Previous experiences of success and failure can contribute to expectancy beliefs, but expectancy alone does not cause performance.

Intrinsic Value

Intrinsic value is how enjoyable or interesting the activity is. For example: "I study psychology because I genuinely enjoy learning why people behave as they do."

Attainment Value

Attainment value is how important succeeding is to the person's identity or sense of self. For example: "Doing well in Psychology matters to me because being a strong psychology student is part of how I see myself."

Utility Value

Utility value is how useful the activity is for future goals. For example: "I don't particularly enjoy statistics, but I need research-method skills for the psychology degree I want to study." Something can therefore have high value without being intrinsically enjoyable.

Cost

Contemporary expectancy–value accounts also consider perceived costs of engaging in an activity, including effort, time, lost opportunities, stress and emotional cost. For example, a student values joining the school basketball team and believes they could succeed, but training would take four evenings every week during IB exam preparation. The perceived cost may reduce their willingness to participate. Value is therefore more complicated than simply asking whether you like something.

How Does It Work?

Past experiences, feedback and social or cultural influences shape a learner's expectancy beliefs and subjective task values. Those beliefs influence which activities are chosen and how much effort and persistence follows. Performance and future experiences then feed back and may change future expectancy and value beliefs.

Past experiences + feedback + social/cultural influences → Expectancy beliefs and task values → Choice of activity → Effort and persistence → Performance and future experiences → Future beliefs may change

For example, a student succeeds on several Psychology essays. Success plus useful teacher feedback may increase expectancy for future success, so the student becomes more willing to attempt difficult essays, and additional practice may contribute to further improvement. Expectancy is one influence among several here: it would be circular to say they succeeded simply because they expected success.

Real-Life Worked Example

Maya is deciding whether to take HL Psychology. Expectancy: she has done well in previous psychology work and believes she can cope with the HL course. Intrinsic value: she enjoys studying human behaviour. Attainment value: being successful academically is important to her identity. Utility value: she wants to study psychology at university. Cost: HL requires additional work and will reduce the time available for another subject she enjoys. EVT predicts that Maya's decision will depend not simply on whether she likes psychology, but on the combination of her expectancy for success, different forms of value, and perceived costs. This is also why motivation can differ even between students with similar ability.

Key Supporting Research

Research

Longitudinal expectancy–value research

Aim: To examine how students' expectancy beliefs and subjective task values relate to later educational choices, persistence and achievement-related behaviour.

Procedure: Researchers following Eccles and Wigfield's framework have measured students' expectancy beliefs and task values and tracked their later course choices, persistence and achievement over time.

Results: Expectancy beliefs and subjective task values have been found to be associated with later choices, persistence and achievement-related behaviour. Expectancy often relates strongly to performance, while value is particularly useful for explaining choices such as whether someone continues studying a particular subject.

Connection: Consistent with the theory, but these associations do not by themselves prove that expectancy or value causes achievement.

What Does the Evidence NOT Prove?

Longitudinal associations cannot establish a simple one-way causal relationship: expectancy often relates strongly to performance, yet past success can also raise expectancy. Value predicts choices, but a correlation between value beliefs and later choices does not prove that the belief caused the behaviour. Expectancy–value relationships therefore support the framework without proving that beliefs alone determine outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

  • Explains individual differences — Students with similar ability may make different choices because their expectations and values differ.
  • Beyond intrinsic/extrinsic — A student may dislike an activity but still value it because it is useful or important.
  • Predictive usefulness — Expectancy and value beliefs have been associated with educational choices, persistence and achievement-related behaviour.
  • Practical usefulness — Low motivation could reflect low expectancy, low value or high perceived cost, which suggest different interventions — simply telling a student to "Try harder" may miss the real problem.

Limitations

  • Measurement — Expectancy and value are often measured using self-report, and responses may be affected by interpretation, social desirability, mood or inaccurate self-perception.
  • Causality — Expectancy and achievement can influence each other: high expectancy may encourage persistence, but past success may increase expectancy, so correlations do not establish a simple one-way causal relationship.
  • Context and culture — What people value is shaped partly by family, peers, educational systems, cultural expectations and available opportunities, so expectancy and value should not be treated as purely individual characteristics.
  • Other influences — Motivation is also influenced by emotion, reinforcement, autonomy, social relationships and environmental constraints: EVT is not a complete explanation of all motivation.

Another Way of Explaining This

Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan) asks largely to what extent psychological needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness are being supported. Expectancy–Value Theory asks largely "Do I think I can succeed?" and "Is this activity worth doing?" For example, a student might feel autonomous and competent when selecting subjects but still avoid Physics because they see little value in it. Another student might highly value Physics because they want to become an engineer but have very low expectancy for success. The theories therefore explain motivation from different perspectives and can be complementary: neither disproves the other.

EVT explains motivation largely from a cognitive perspective because beliefs about expected success and subjective value influence behaviour.

Where Can I Use This?

  • Health & Well-being — A person may be more motivated to exercise when they believe they can maintain the behaviour successfully and value its health benefits.
  • Human Development — Children's educational choices and persistence may be influenced by developing beliefs about competence and the value attached to activities.
  • Human Relationships — People may invest effort in maintaining a relationship when they believe their actions can improve it and they value the relationship.
  • Learning & Cognition — Students may be more likely to choose and persist with learning activities when they expect success and perceive the task as worthwhile.

Scenario Practice

Apply the Theory

Leo is capable of mathematics and usually receives good marks. However, he refuses to take HL Mathematics. He says: "I know I could probably do well, but I don't enjoy it and I don't need it for anything I want to do after school. It would also take time away from Psychology."

Using Expectancy–Value Theory, explain Leo's decision.

Show model answer

Leo's expectancy is relatively high: he believes he could probably do well. However, his intrinsic value is low (he does not enjoy mathematics) and his utility value is low (he does not need it for his future plans), while the perceived cost is high (time taken away from Psychology). High expectancy alone does not necessarily produce strong motivation or choice: Leo's decision follows from the combination of expectancy, value and cost.

Don't Make These Mistakes

Common Misconceptions

  • Expectancy is not actual ability. Someone can be capable but expect failure, or have limited ability but unrealistically expect success.
  • Value does not just mean enjoyment. A task can have high utility or attainment value despite low intrinsic value.
  • High expectancy alone does not guarantee motivation. Someone may believe they can succeed but see little value in doing so.
  • High value alone does not guarantee motivation. Someone may care greatly about an outcome but believe success is extremely unlikely.
  • EVT does not claim motivation is determined by only two variables. Expectancy and value are important influences operating alongside other personal, social and environmental factors.

What Do I Actually Need to Remember?

  1. Expectancy = "Can I succeed?" — a belief, not actual ability
  2. Subjective task value = "Is this worth doing?" — intrinsic, attainment and utility value, plus perceived cost
  3. Expectancy and value influence choices, effort and persistence
  4. Past experience, feedback and social/cultural influences can shape expectancy and value
  5. EVT is probabilistic, not deterministic; correlations do not prove causation
  6. Compare EVT with Self-Determination Theory
