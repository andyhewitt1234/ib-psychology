Bowlby's Theory of Attachment IB REQUIRED

Bowlby's Theory of Attachment proposes that attachment is an innate biological system that evolved to keep infants close to caregivers for protection, and that early attachment experiences create internal working models that shape all subsequent relationships.
Used in: Human Development
Primary home: Context-Specific Models > Human Development

What Does the Theory Actually Say?

John Bowlby (1969, 1973, 1980) proposed that attachment is an innate, evolutionarily adaptive system designed to keep infants close to their caregiver for protection and survival.

Phases of Attachment Development

Phase 1 — Pre-attachment (approximately birth–6 weeks): Infants use behaviours such as crying, smiling, grasping, and orienting toward people to attract caregiver attention. They can recognise familiar people but show relatively limited attachment preference for one particular caregiver.

Phase 2 — Attachment-in-the-making (approximately 6 weeks–6/7 months): Infants increasingly prefer familiar caregivers and become more responsive to the people who regularly care for them. A more selective attachment relationship is beginning to develop.

Phase 3 — Clear-cut attachment (from approximately 6/7 months): A strong attachment to a particular caregiver becomes clearly observable. The infant actively seeks proximity to this attachment figure, may protest when separated, and uses the caregiver as a source of security.

Multiple attachments: As development continues, infants can form attachment relationships with several familiar caregivers, such as another parent, grandparent, sibling, or regular caregiver.

Monotropy: Bowlby proposed that although infants can form multiple attachments, one attachment relationship may have particular importance and may become the infant’s primary attachment. Monotropy does not mean that an infant can attach to only one person.

Internal Working Models

An internal working model is Bowlby’s proposed mental representation of the self, other people, and relationships that develops partly through early attachment experiences. For example, secure attachment can produce models such as “I am worthy of love; others are reliable,” while insecure attachment can produce models such as “Others cannot be trusted.” Repeated experiences with caregivers may contribute to expectations about questions such as: are other people likely to be available when I need them? Can other people generally be trusted? Am I worthy of care and support? What happens when I seek comfort from another person?

These expectations may influence later social behaviour and relationships. However, internal working models are not fixed scripts that determine every future relationship. Later friendships, romantic relationships, family experiences, therapy, and other significant experiences may reinforce or modify earlier expectations. For example, a child whose caregiver usually responds reliably when the child is distressed may develop an expectation that seeking help is worthwhile. Later, when upset at school, the child may therefore be more willing to approach a trusted adult for support. This is a probabilistic influence, not a prediction of exactly how the child will behave in every future relationship.

Critical Period

Bowlby proposed that the first few years of life form an especially important period for attachment development. In his original theory, he described this quite strongly as a critical period. However, later evidence shows that attachment development is more flexible than a strict critical-period interpretation suggests. Early caregiving experiences can have important developmental effects, but later attachment formation and substantial recovery are possible.

It is therefore useful to distinguish Bowlby’s original critical-period proposal from the more cautious modern idea of a sensitive period. A critical period means a relatively narrow window in which development must occur according to the strongest version of the claim. A sensitive period means a period when particular experiences may have especially strong effects, while later development and recovery remain possible: early childhood may be a period during which attachment experiences have particularly strong developmental effects, without being an absolute deadline after which healthy attachment becomes impossible.

For example, a child who experiences severe early deprivation may initially show attachment difficulties but can show considerable recovery after being placed with stable, responsive caregivers.

How Does It Work?

The infant is biologically predisposed to seek proximity with the caregiver. When the caregiver responds consistently and sensitively, secure attachment develops, creating positive internal working models that may influence expectations in later relationships.

Infant proximity-seeking → Caregiver responds → Attachment security established → Internal working model formed → May influence later relationships

Real-Life Worked Example

When 10-month-old Maya sees her mother leave the room, she cries (separation anxiety). When her mother returns, Maya crawls to her and calms down (secure base behaviour). Because Maya’s mother has consistently responded to her needs, Maya has developed a secure attachment and a positive internal working model that will influence how she forms relationships throughout her life.

Key Supporting Research

Study 1

Harlow and Harlow (1962) — Rhesus Monkey Studies

Aim: To investigate whether attachment is based on feeding or comfort.

Procedure: Infant rhesus monkeys were given two surrogate mothers: one wire (with milk) and one cloth (soft but no milk).

Results: Monkeys spent more time with the cloth mother. When frightened, they ran to the cloth mother for comfort.

Connection to Bowlby: Harlow’s rhesus-monkey research showed that infant monkeys strongly preferred a soft surrogate and sought it when frightened, even when another surrogate provided food. The findings challenged a simple feeding-based explanation of attachment and are consistent with Bowlby’s argument that attachment serves important functions beyond food provision, including proximity and security. However, Harlow studied rhesus monkeys, so the findings cannot by themselves establish the mechanisms of human attachment.
Study 2

Ainsworth et al. (1978) — Strange Situation

Aim: To classify infant attachment types.

Procedure: Infants (12-18 months) observed through eight episodes of separation and reunion.

Results: Three types: Secure (65%), Insecure-avoidant (20%), Insecure-resistant (15%). A fourth type, disorganised, was later added.

Connection to Bowlby: The Strange Situation demonstrated systematic individual differences in infants’ attachment behaviour during separation and reunion. These findings are compatible with Bowlby’s broader attachment framework and provided a way of observing and classifying patterns of attachment behaviour. However, the Strange Situation itself does not demonstrate that caregiver behaviour caused a particular attachment classification, and it does not directly measure an infant’s internal working model.

What Does the Evidence NOT Prove?

The Strange Situation classifies attachment at one point in time in an artificial setting. Classifications may change over time. The Harlow studies involved monkeys, limiting generalisation. Internal working models cannot be directly observed.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

  • Extensive research support — Strange Situation, Harlow’s monkeys, and longitudinal studies converge.
  • Explains relationship continuity — Internal working models explain how early experiences shape adult relationships.
  • Evolutionary basis — Provides a compelling biological foundation for the parent-infant bond.

Limitations

  • Cultural bias — Strange Situation developed with Western samples. Attachment patterns differ across cultures.
  • Overemphasises mother — Modern research shows multiple attachments are common and important.
  • Deterministic — Internal working models are not fixed; later experiences can modify insecure patterns.

Another Way of Explaining This

Ethological theory (Lorenz, 1935) explains attachment through imprinting — a biologically programmed learning process triggered during a critical period. While Lorenz focused on birds, Bowlby adapted the ethological perspective to human attachment, adding the concept of social reciprocity and internal working models.

IB Concept Connections

Bowlby claims early caregiver behaviour causes attachment patterns. The Strange Situation demonstrates that caregiver sensitivity correlates with attachment security, but the causal direction is debated. Temperament (Kagan, 1994) may also influence attachment, creating a bidirectional relationship.

Attachment is measured through behavioural observation (Strange Situation) and self-report (Adult Attachment Interview). The Strange Situation captures only brief behavioural snapshots, while self-report measures may not accurately capture unconscious internal working models.

Where Can I Use This?

  • Health & Well-being — Insecure attachment is linked to higher rates of anxiety, depression, and difficulty forming supportive relationships.
  • Human Development — Central to understanding early development, parenting styles, and the intergenerational transmission of attachment patterns.
  • Human Relationships — Internal working models predict adult romantic relationship styles and parenting behaviour.

Scenario Practice

Apply the Theory

Two-year-old Leo has a cold and is crying. His mother picks him up, comforts him, and speaks soothingly. When she puts him down, he crawls to her legs and holds on.

Using Bowlby’s theory, explain Leo’s behaviour.

Show model answer

Leo is demonstrating secure base behaviour: using his mother as a safe base from which to explore and returning to her when distressed. His mother’s consistent, sensitive response to his distress has established secure attachment. Leo has developed a positive internal working model: “When I am upset, my caregiver will respond.” This model predicts that Leo will be more likely to form trusting relationships and explore his environment confidently.

Don't Make These Mistakes

Common Misconceptions

  • Attachment is not the same as bonding. Attachment is the infant’s side; bonding is the caregiver’s side. They develop at different times.
  • Attachment is not just about the mother. Bowlby acknowledged multiple attachments, though he focused on monotropic attachment.
  • Insecure attachment does not cause psychopathology. It is a risk factor, not a deterministic outcome.

What Do I Actually Need to Remember?

  1. Three phases: indiscriminate → discriminate → specific attachment
  2. Monotropic attachment: primary attachment to one main figure
  3. Internal working models may influence expectations and behaviour in later relationships
  4. Key studies: Harlow (1962) contact comfort; Ainsworth (1978) Strange Situation
  5. Secure (65%), insecure-avoidant (20%), insecure-resistant (15%), disorganised
  6. Critical period: first 2-3 years
  7. Limitation: culturally biased, overemphasises mother
