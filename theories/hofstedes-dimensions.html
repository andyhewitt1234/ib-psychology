Hofstede's Dimensions of Culture OUR CHOSEN MODEL

Hofstede's cultural dimensions theory proposes that national cultures can be distinguished along six key dimensions that shape values, behaviours, and social norms within societies.
Used in: Core Sociocultural Human Relationships
Primary home: Core Sociocultural

What Does the Theory Actually Say?

Geert Hofstede (1980, 2001) developed one of the most influential frameworks for understanding cultural differences. Analysing data from over 116,000 IBM employees across 72 countries, he identified six dimensions:

1. Power Distance (PDI)

The extent to which less powerful members accept unequal power distribution. High PDI cultures (e.g., Malaysia) accept hierarchy; low PDI cultures (e.g., Denmark) strive for equality.

2. Individualism vs. Collectivism (IDV)

Individualist cultures (e.g., USA) emphasise personal achievement. Collectivist cultures (e.g., South Korea) prioritise group harmony and loyalty.

3. Masculinity vs. Femininity (MAS)

Masculine cultures (e.g., Japan) value achievement and assertiveness. Feminine cultures (e.g., Sweden) value cooperation and quality of life.

4. Uncertainty Avoidance (UAI)

High UAI cultures (e.g., Greece) prefer strict rules. Low UAI cultures (e.g., Singapore) tolerate ambiguity.

5. Long-Term vs. Short-Term Orientation (LTO)

Long-term cultures (e.g., China) value perseverance. Short-term cultures (e.g., USA) value tradition and immediate results.

6. Indulgence vs. Restraint (IND)

Indulgent cultures (e.g., Mexico) value enjoyment. Restrained cultures (e.g., Egypt) suppress gratification.

How Does It Work?

The six dimensions function as cultural benchmarks shaped by historical, economic, and social development. National scores represent tendencies, not absolutes. Individuals vary, but national averages reflect shared values that influence institutions, communication, and behaviour.

Historical/cultural development → Shared national values → Scores on six dimensions → Influences institutions, communication, behaviour

Real-Life Worked Example

In a multinational team, a member from a high power distance culture (e.g., Malaysia) may hesitate to challenge their manager, while a low power distance member (e.g., Denmark) openly disagrees. An individualist (e.g., USA) may seek personal recognition, while a collectivist (e.g., South Korea) prioritises group harmony. Understanding these dimensions helps navigate communication differences.

Key Supporting Research

Study 1

Hofstede (1980) — Culture's Consequences

Aim: To identify systematic differences in values across nations.

Procedure: Analysed 116,000+ IBM employees across 72 countries. Factor analysis identified four dimensions.

Results: Four dimensions reliably distinguished national cultures, replicable across time.

Connection: Large sample and temporal replicability provide strong evidence for cultural variation.
Study 2

Hofstede and Bond (1988) — Confucian Dynamism

Aim: To address Western bias by incorporating Eastern values.

Procedure: Analysed the Asian Value Survey, identifying Long-Term Orientation.

Results: LTO correlated with East Asian economic growth and explained patterns the original four could not.

Connection: Demonstrates the framework can expand to include non-Western values.

What Does the Evidence NOT Prove?

Dimensions are aggregate national scores that do not predict individual behaviour. The original IBM data may not represent full national populations. The framework describes patterns but does not explain why cultures develop along these dimensions.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

  • Practical applications — Widely used in international business, education, and cross-cultural psychology.
  • Large-scale empirical basis — One of the most data-driven cultural frameworks available.
  • Predicts real-world patterns — Country scores correlate with measurable outcomes.

Limitations

  • Ecological fallacy — National averages do not predict individual behaviour.
  • Western-centred origins — Original dimensions derived from a Western corporate dataset.
  • Static view — Cultures change over time, but scores are treated as fixed.

Another Way of Explaining This

Trompenaars' model (1997) proposes seven dimensions overlapping with but extending Hofstede. Schwartz (1992) identified seven cultural value orientations through different methodology.

Where Can I Use This?

  • Health & Well-being — Cultural dimensions influence health-seeking behaviour and attitudes to mental health.
  • Human Development — Cultural values shape parenting styles, educational expectations, and socialisation.
  • Human Relationships — Individualism/collectivism influences relationship formation, communication, and conflict resolution.

Scenario Practice

Apply the Theory

A Japanese student moves to Australia and is surprised that students call their teachers by their first names and openly disagree in class.

Using Hofstede's dimensions, explain this cultural difference.

Show model answer

Japan scores high on Power Distance (hierarchical respect for teachers) and Masculinity (achievement-oriented). Australia scores low on Power Distance (informal relationships) and higher on Femininity (cooperation over hierarchy). The discomfort reflects the clash between Japan's hierarchical educational norms and Australia's egalitarian classroom culture.

Don't Make These Mistakes

Common Misconceptions

  • Dimensions do not label countries as good or bad.
  • Individuals are not their country's score. There is enormous variation within every culture.
  • Cultures are not static. Country scores shift over time.

What Do I Actually Need to Remember?

  1. Six dimensions: PDI, IDV, MAS, UAI, LTO, IND
  2. Based on 116,000+ IBM employees across 72 countries
  3. Dimensions are aggregate national scores, not individual predictions
  4. Key study: Hofstede (1980); expanded by Hofstede & Bond (1988)
  5. Limitations: ecological fallacy, Western-centred, static view
  6. Practical applications in business, education, and cross-cultural psychology
  7. Alternative: Trompenaars (1997), Schwartz (1992)
