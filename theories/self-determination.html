Self-Determination Theory HL

Self-Determination Theory proposes that human motivation and well-being depend on the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness.
Used in: HL Extension
Primary home: HL Extension

What Does the Theory Actually Say?

Deci and Ryan (1985, 2000) proposed that humans have three innate psychological needs whose satisfaction is essential for motivation, well-being, and personal growth:

Autonomy

The need to feel that one's actions are self-endorsed and volitional. Not independence, but feeling that behaviour originates from oneself: autonomy does not mean independence or doing everything alone, and a person can willingly follow another person's advice while still experiencing autonomy if they personally endorse the action. Autonomy-supportive environments can foster more autonomous forms of motivation, including intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, and integrated regulation. Autonomous motivation is therefore not the same as intrinsic motivation only. For example, a student may not enjoy studying mathematics but may freely choose to work hard because becoming an engineer is personally important to them. The studying is not intrinsically motivated because the activity itself is not necessarily enjoyable, but the student's motivation can still be highly autonomous because they personally value the reason for studying.

Competence

The need to feel effective and capable of mastering challenges. Developed through success and positive feedback. Optimal challenges and clear feedback support competence.

Relatedness

The need to feel connected to others — to care and be cared for, to belong. Satisfied through warm, supportive relationships.

Types of Motivation

SDT distinguishes a continuum from least to most self-determined: amotivation → external regulation → introjected regulation → identified regulation → integrated regulation → intrinsic motivation.

Amotivation: the person lacks an intention or meaningful reason to act. For example: "I don't see any point in studying psychology, so I probably won't bother." External regulation: behaviour is controlled mainly by external rewards, punishments, or demands. For example: "I study psychology because my parents will punish me if I don't."

Introjected regulation: the pressure has been partly internalised, but behaviour is still controlled by feelings such as guilt, shame, anxiety, or the need to prove oneself. For example: "I study because I'd feel guilty about myself if I didn't." Identified regulation: the person personally recognises and accepts the value of the behaviour. For example: "I don't always enjoy studying, but I study because doing well in this qualification matters to me."

Integrated regulation: the behaviour has become consistent with the person's broader values and sense of self. For example: "I study seriously because being a committed learner fits the kind of person I want to be." Intrinsic motivation: the activity is performed because it is inherently interesting or enjoyable. For example: "I study psychology because I genuinely enjoy learning about human behaviour."

External regulation, introjected regulation, identified regulation, and integrated regulation are all forms of extrinsic motivation. However, they differ in how autonomous or controlled the motivation is: external and introjected regulation are relatively controlled, while identified and integrated regulation are increasingly autonomous. Integrated regulation is therefore highly autonomous, but it is still extrinsic motivation because the activity is performed for its personally valued outcome rather than solely for the inherent enjoyment of the activity itself. The quality of motivation matters more than a simple intrinsic-good/extrinsic-bad split.

How Does It Work?

When environments support autonomy, competence, and relatedness, people develop autonomous motivation (intrinsic or identified regulation). When these needs are thwarted, people rely on external regulation or become amotivated. The quality of motivation, not just quantity, determines outcomes.

Environment supports/undermines three needs → Quality of motivation (autonomous vs. controlled) → Well-being, persistence, performance outcomes

Real-Life Worked Example

Student A studies psychology because they genuinely enjoy learning about human behaviour. Their motivation is intrinsic. Their environment may also support autonomy (they experience studying as personally chosen and self-endorsed), competence (they receive useful feedback and experience progress when mastering challenging material), and relatedness (they feel connected to classmates and teachers with whom they discuss psychology).

Student B studies primarily because their parents threaten punishment if their grades fall. This is external regulation because the behaviour is being controlled by an external consequence. This particularly limits the student's experience of autonomy. However, it does not automatically tell us whether the student's competence or relatedness needs are satisfied: the student could still feel highly competent because they are good at psychology, and could also have strong, supportive relationships with classmates and teachers.

The type of motivation and the satisfaction of each psychological need are related concepts, but they should not be treated as identical. SDT predicts greater persistence and well-being under more autonomous motivation: Student A is more likely to learn deeply and persist than Student B, but this is a probabilistic prediction rather than a guaranteed outcome.

Key Supporting Research

Study 1

Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) — Meta-Analysis of Rewards

Aim: To examine whether extrinsic rewards undermine intrinsic motivation.

Procedure: Meta-analysis of 128 studies examining the effects of tangible rewards on intrinsic motivation.

Results: Expected, contingent rewards significantly undermined intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks. Non-contingent and unexpected rewards did not. The undermining effect was strongest for tasks people already found enjoyable.

Connection: Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) found that some expected tangible rewards, particularly when contingent on engaging in or completing an already interesting activity, were associated with reduced subsequent intrinsic motivation. However, the effects depended on the type of reward and circumstances: unexpected and some non-contingent rewards did not show the same undermining effect. This is consistent with SDT's proposal that controlling external rewards can sometimes reduce perceived autonomy and intrinsic motivation. It does not show that every reward is harmful or that extrinsic motivation is inherently ineffective.
Study 2

Reeve and Jang (2006) — Autonomy Support in Education

Aim: To investigate how teacher autonomy support affects student motivation and engagement.

Procedure: Observed teacher-student interactions and measured student motivation using SDT-based questionnaires.

Results: Teachers who offered choices, acknowledged feelings, and provided rationales had students with higher autonomous motivation and greater engagement.

Connection: Reeve and Jang observed teacher behaviours and student engagement and found that autonomy-supportive teaching behaviours — providing meaningful choices, acknowledging students' perspectives and feelings, and providing useful rationales — were associated with greater student autonomous motivation and engagement. The findings are consistent with SDT's prediction that autonomy-supportive environments are associated with higher-quality motivation and engagement. However, because the study was observational, it should not by itself be presented as proving that autonomy support directly caused the differences in motivation.

What Does the Evidence NOT Prove?

Most research is correlational, making it difficult to establish that need satisfaction causes better motivation. The three needs may not be equally important across all cultures — some evidence suggests relatedness may be more important in collectivist cultures. The continuum of motivation types is theoretically elegant but difficult to measure precisely.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths

  • Explains quality of motivation — Goes beyond the simple intrinsic/extrinsic distinction to explain why different types of motivation produce different outcomes.
  • Wide applicability — Applied in education, sport, healthcare, parenting, and organisational psychology across diverse cultures.

Limitations

  • Assumes universal needs — The claim that autonomy, competence, and relatedness are universal may not hold across all cultural contexts.
  • Difficult to measure need satisfaction — Self-report measures may not capture the full experience of autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

Another Way of Explaining This

Self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1977) focuses specifically on competence beliefs as the primary driver of motivation. While SDT treats competence as one of three essential needs, self-efficacy theory argues that belief in one's capability is the most important predictor of behaviour. SDT is more comprehensive but less specific about the mechanisms of competence.

SDT makes causal claims: need satisfaction causes autonomous motivation, which causes better outcomes. However, most evidence is correlational. The experimental evidence (e.g., Deci et al. 1999) shows reward effects on motivation but does not fully establish the causal chain from need satisfaction to well-being.

Need satisfaction and motivation types are measured through self-report scales (e.g., Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction Scale, Behavioural Regulation Questionnaire). These capture conscious experience but may miss unconscious need frustration.

Where Can I Use This?

  • Health & Well-being — Need satisfaction predicts psychological well-being; need frustration predicts depression and anxiety.
  • Human Development — Parenting styles that support autonomy, competence, and relatedness promote healthy development.
  • Human Relationships — Relatedness is central to relationship quality; autonomy support within relationships predicts satisfaction.

Scenario Practice

Apply the Theory

A student joins the football team because her friends are on it and she enjoys playing. Her coach gives regular feedback and lets players choose their position. After an injury, she stays motivated because she values being part of the team.

Using SDT, explain why this student remains motivated despite injury.

Show model answer

The student's motivation is autonomous: she plays for enjoyment (intrinsic) and values team membership (identified regulation). All three needs are satisfied: autonomy (chose to play, chooses position), competence (receives regular feedback), and relatedness (part of a team she values). Because her motivation is driven by internal factors rather than external rewards, the injury does not eliminate her motivation — she continues to value the activity and her connection to the team.

Don't Make These Mistakes

Common Misconceptions

  • Autonomy does not mean independence. It means volitional action, even when influenced by others.
  • Extrinsic motivation is not always bad. Identified regulation (recognising importance) is extrinsic but autonomous and effective.
  • SDT does not say rewards are always harmful. The undermining effect is specific to tasks people already find interesting.

What Do I Actually Need to Remember?

  1. Three basic needs: autonomy, competence, relatedness
  2. Motivation continuum: from intrinsic to amotivation
  3. Need satisfaction → autonomous motivation → better outcomes
  4. Key study: Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999) — rewards undermine intrinsic motivation
  5. Key study: Reeve & Jang (2006) — autonomy support in education
  6. Limitation: assumes universal needs, may not hold across all cultures
  7. Alternative: Self-efficacy theory (Bandura) focuses on competence alone
