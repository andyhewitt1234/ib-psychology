Multi-Store Model of Memory IB Required

The Multi-Store Model (MSM), proposed by Atkinson and Shiffrin (1968), describes memory as a linear flow of information through three distinct stores: sensory memory, short-term memory (STM), and long-term memory (LTM). Each store differs in capacity, duration, and coding.

Information flows through three stores — sensory memory, short-term memory, and long-term memory — with attention determining what passes from one store to the next.
Real-life example: You look up a phone number, repeat it in your head, and dial it — but moments later you've forgotten it entirely. The number was in your short-term memory (limited capacity and duration) but never transferred to long-term memory because you didn't rehearse it. This everyday slip demonstrates how information moves between memory stores.

What does the theory say?

The Multi-Store Model proposes that memory consists of three separate stores that process information in a linear sequence:

Sensory Memory: All incoming information first enters sensory memory, which briefly stores information from the senses. Iconic memory (visual) lasts about 0.5 seconds; echoic memory (auditory) lasts about 3 seconds. Only attended information passes to the next store.

Short-Term Memory (STM): Information that receives attention enters STM, which has a limited capacity of approximately 7±2 items (Miller, 1956) and a duration of about 18-30 seconds without rehearsal. Information is primarily encoded acoustically (by sound).

Long-Term Memory (LTM): Through rehearsal, information can be transferred to LTM, which has a potentially unlimited capacity and duration. Information in LTM is encoded semantically (by meaning).

The model emphasises that information must pass through each store in order — it cannot skip a stage. Attention is the key process that determines whether information moves from sensory memory to STM, and rehearsal determines whether it moves from STM to LTM.

How does the evidence support it?

The multi-store model has been supported by extensive experimental research demonstrating the distinct properties of sensory registers, short-term memory, and long-term memory. Sperling's (1960) partial report experiment provided evidence for the existence of a high-capacity but rapid-decay sensory register. Participants who were presented with a brief display of letters could report more letters when cued immediately after the display than when cued after a delay.

Peterson and Peterson (1959) provided evidence for the limited duration of short-term memory. Participants who were prevented from rehearsing trigrams could recall them after 3 seconds but not after 18 seconds, demonstrating that STM has a duration of approximately 18-30 seconds without rehearsal.

Bahrick and colleagues (1975) provided evidence for the potentially unlimited duration of long-term memory. Participants who had learned Spanish in high school could recall vocabulary words after 50 years, demonstrating that LTM can retain information for decades. Importantly, the degree of original learning predicted long-term retention.

The distinction between STM and LTM is further supported by evidence from brain-damaged patients. Patient HM, who had his hippocampus removed, could not form new long-term memories but retained his short-term memory, demonstrating a double dissociation between the two stores.

Strengths

The multi-store model provides a clear and parsimonious framework for understanding the structure of memory. By distinguishing between three stores with distinct properties, the model explains a wide range of memory phenomena, from the fleeting nature of sensory experience to the enduring nature of long-term recall.

The model's emphasis on the role of attention and rehearsal in memory provides practical guidance for improving memory performance. Understanding that attention is necessary for transferring information from sensory registers to STM, and that rehearsal is necessary for maintaining information in STM and transferring it to LTM, has practical implications for study skills and learning strategies.

The model has generated an enormous body of research that has advanced our understanding of memory processes. Research inspired by the model has identified the mechanisms through which information is encoded, stored, and retrieved, and has led to the development of more sophisticated models of memory.

Another strength is the model's high face validity. The distinction between short-term and long-term memory is intuitive and consistent with everyday experience. People readily recognise that they can hold a limited amount of information in mind at once and that some memories persist while others fade.

Limitations

A primary limitation of the multi-store model is that it treats long-term memory as a single, undifferentiated store. Research has shown that LTM comprises multiple distinct systems, including episodic memory, semantic memory, and procedural memory. These systems have different properties and are supported by different brain structures.

The model has been criticised for presenting memory as a passive process. Craik and Lockhart (1972) proposed the levels of processing framework, arguing that memory is better understood in terms of the depth of processing rather than the store in which information is held. According to this view, memory is an active, constructive process rather than a passive recording process.

The ecological validity of research supporting the model has been questioned. Many studies used artificial materials and procedures that may not reflect how memory operates in everyday life. Remembering trigrams in a laboratory setting is fundamentally different from remembering personal experiences in real-world contexts.

The model also fails to account for the constructive nature of memory. Bartlett's (1932) research demonstrated that memory is an active, reconstructive process in which people use their existing knowledge to fill in gaps and make inferences. The model's emphasis on passive storage does not capture this constructive quality.

Applications

The multi-store model has important educational implications, suggesting that effective learning requires attention, rehearsal, and meaningful processing. Understanding that information must be attended to in order to be remembered can help students develop more effective study habits. The model also suggests that maintenance rehearsal is less effective than elaborative rehearsal.

In clinical settings, the model provides a framework for understanding memory disorders. Damage to different memory stores produces different patterns of impairment, and understanding these patterns can help clinicians diagnose and treat memory problems. For example, patients with STM deficits may benefit from strategies that reduce the demands on STM.

The model has implications for the design of information systems and user interfaces. Understanding the limitations of STM (such as its capacity of approximately 7 items) can inform the design of menus, dashboards, and other information displays.

The model also has implications for understanding age-related changes in memory. Research has shown that different memory systems age at different rates, with STM declining more rapidly than LTM. Understanding these differential age effects can help develop interventions to support memory functioning in older adults.
