Diathesis-Stress Model IB Required

The diathesis-stress model explains that mental disorders result from the interaction between a pre-existing vulnerability (diathesis) and environmental stress. Neither factor alone causes the disorder — it is their combination that triggers psychological problems.

Mental illness develops when a person with a pre-existing vulnerability (genetic, biological, or psychological) encounters sufficient environmental stress to activate that vulnerability.
Real-life example: Identical twins share the same genes, but only one may develop depression after a stressful event like divorce or job loss. The twin had the genetic vulnerability (diathesis), but it was the environmental stress that triggered the disorder. The other twin, without the same stress exposure, may never develop depression despite having the same genetic risk.

What does the theory say?

The diathesis-stress model proposes that psychological disorders develop through the interaction of two factors:

Diathesis (vulnerability): A pre-existing predisposition that can be genetic (e.g., inherited genes for depression), biological (e.g., neurotransmitter imbalances), or psychological (e.g., negative thinking patterns or childhood trauma). The diathesis is a latent vulnerability that may never manifest without sufficient stress.

Stress: Environmental or life events that activate the vulnerability. Examples include bereavement, divorce, bullying, exam pressure, or prolonged work stress. The stress can be acute (a single traumatic event) or chronic (ongoing hardship).

The model predicts that individuals with a stronger diathesis may need less stress before the threshold for developing a disorder is reached, while those with a weaker diathesis may need greater or more prolonged stress. This threshold is a conceptual part of the model, not a precise measurable cutoff that psychologists can calculate for an individual: neither vulnerability nor stress guarantees that a disorder will develop, and the model cannot identify a precise numerical threshold for any one person. It is the interaction between vulnerability and stress that is central to the model.

How does the evidence support it?

The diathesis-stress model has been supported by extensive research demonstrating interactions between genetic vulnerability and environmental stress. Caspi and colleagues (2003) conducted an influential early study reporting a gene-environment interaction involving stressful life events, the 5-HTTLPR gene, and depression outcomes. However, later research and meta-analyses have produced mixed findings regarding this specific 5-HTTLPR interaction, so it is best understood as an important example of research investigating gene-environment interaction rather than definitive proof of the diathesis-stress model.

Twin studies have provided evidence for the heritability of psychological disorders while also demonstrating the importance of environmental factors. Research by Sullivan and colleagues has shown that genetic factors account for approximately 40-50% of the variance in depression risk, while environmental factors account for the remaining variance.

Research on life events and disorder onset has provided support for the stress component. Brown and Harris (1978) demonstrated that depressed women were significantly more likely to have experienced severe life events in the months preceding the onset of their depression than non-depressed controls. Importantly, the relationship was strongest for women with pre-existing vulnerabilities.

Neurobiological research has provided evidence for the mechanisms through which diathesis and stress interact. Research on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis has shown that individuals with genetic vulnerabilities may have hyperactive stress responses, leading to elevated cortisol levels that can damage brain structures involved in mood regulation.

Strengths

The diathesis-stress model provides a comprehensive framework for understanding why psychological disorders develop in some individuals but not others. By recognising that both vulnerability and stress are necessary for disorder onset, the model explains why the same stressor can produce different outcomes in different people.

The model has important clinical applications, as it suggests that treatment should address both vulnerability and stress factors. Pharmacotherapy may target biological vulnerabilities, while psychotherapy may help individuals develop coping strategies to manage stress. This dual approach recognises that effective treatment may need to address both components.

The model also has implications for prevention, suggesting that reducing exposure to stressors or strengthening resilience could prevent disorder onset in vulnerable individuals. Programmes that provide support to individuals at high genetic risk could buffer the effects of stress.

Another strength is the model's flexibility in incorporating different types of diathesis. The model can accommodate genetic vulnerabilities, psychological vulnerabilities (such as negative cognitive styles), and environmental vulnerabilities (such as childhood adversity). This flexibility allows the model to be applied across different disorders and different populations.

Limitations

A primary limitation of the diathesis-stress model is that it cannot predict exactly when a disorder will develop. While the model explains the general mechanism, it cannot specify the precise threshold at which the combination of diathesis and stress produces disorder. This limits its clinical utility for prognosis and treatment planning.

The model has been criticised for not explaining why different disorders develop from similar diathesis-stress combinations. For example, the same combination of genetic vulnerability and life stress might lead to depression in one person and anxiety in another. The model does not account for these differences in outcome.

The directionality problem presents a significant challenge. While the model proposes that stress leads to disorder, research has shown that the relationship is bidirectional: psychological disorders can also create stressful life events. For example, depression can lead to job loss and relationship difficulties, which are themselves stressors.

Finally, the model has been criticised for not adequately accounting for resilience. Research has shown that many individuals with strong diatheses never develop disorders, even when exposed to significant stress. The model does not explain why some individuals are resilient while others are vulnerable.

Applications

The diathesis-stress model has important implications for clinical assessment, suggesting that clinicians should assess both vulnerability and stress factors when evaluating patients. A comprehensive assessment that considers genetic history, psychological vulnerabilities, and recent stressors can provide a more complete picture of disorder risk.

The model informs treatment approaches by suggesting that interventions should target both vulnerability and stress. For individuals with biological vulnerabilities, pharmacotherapy may be appropriate, while psychological interventions may help individuals develop coping strategies. Combined approaches may be more effective than interventions that target only one component.

The model has implications for prevention programmes, suggesting that interventions should focus on either reducing stress or strengthening resilience in vulnerable populations. Programmes that provide social support, teach coping skills, or reduce exposure to adversity could prevent disorder onset.

The model also has applications in understanding comorbidity. The diathesis-stress framework can explain why multiple disorders often co-occur: shared diatheses and stressors may contribute to multiple disorders simultaneously. Understanding these shared mechanisms could lead to more integrated treatments.
