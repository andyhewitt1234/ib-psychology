01. Human Relationships: The Big Picture.

Group Behaviour examines how people's behaviour is influenced by group membership and other people, including social identity, social learning, conformity, compliance, culture and acculturation.

Interpersonal Relationships examines psychological processes affecting relationships between individuals, including how relationships are interpreted, how conflict develops, communication, biological influences and ways relationships can be improved.

A student joins a new friendship group and gradually begins using the group's slang, clothing style and opinions. This is group behaviour because membership of the group is influencing the student's behaviour. In contrast, two friends arguing because one interprets the other's short text message as rude is an interpersonal relationship issue because psychological processes are influencing the interaction between two individuals.

Relationships cannot usually be explained by one factor alone.

Cognitive factors — schemas, expectations, interpretations and attributions.

Sociocultural factors — group membership, norms, social learning and culture.

Biological factors — including chemical messengers associated with social behaviour.

Communication — language, tone, facial expressions and communication patterns.

Cognitive — a person who expects others to be untrustworthy interprets an unanswered message as deliberate rejection.

Sociocultural — a teenager changes how they behave around a friendship group because fitting in is important.

Biological — biological processes such as oxytocin may contribute to bonding and social behaviour, but do not determine relationships by themselves.

Communication — the message “Fine.” could seem neutral face-to-face when accompanied by a smile but angry in a text where tone and facial expression are missing.

Students will use psychological theories and research to understand group behaviour and interpersonal relationships, evaluate psychological evidence and explanations, apply relevant IB Psychology concepts, and complete the required survey/questionnaire class practical.

Question 1: A student starts using the same slang as their new friendship group. Which area of Human Relationships would investigate this?
Group behaviour, because the student's behaviour is being influenced by group membership and social influence.
Question 2: Two friends receive the same message — “Fine.” One thinks it is neutral while the other thinks the sender is angry. What does this demonstrate?
Cognitive processes and communication can interact. Existing expectations can influence interpretation, while text communication removes cues such as tone and facial expression.
Question 3: Why shouldn't psychologists explain relationships using only biology, cognition or culture?
Human relationships usually result from interacting influences. Biological processes, thoughts and expectations, social experiences, culture and communication can all contribute to behaviour.

02. Social Identity Theory: Core Process.

Social Identity Theory (SIT), developed by Tajfel and Turner (1979), proposes that part of our self-concept comes from the social groups to which we belong.

Social Categorisation — we mentally organise ourselves and other people into groups such as “us” and “them”.

Social Identification — we adopt a group as part of our identity, so its values, successes and failures can become personally meaningful.

Social Comparison — we compare our ingroup with relevant outgroups, often seeking a positive social identity, which can contribute to ingroup favouritism.

Social Categorisation → Social Identification → Social Comparison → possible ingroup favouritism

Two school houses are competing for a sports trophy. Students begin strongly identifying with their own house, cheering more loudly for house members and giving them more support. Some students also start describing the rival house as ‘annoying’ or ‘bad at sports,’ even though they barely know the students in it.

SIT suggests that identifying with an ingroup and comparing it with an outgroup can contribute to ingroup favouritism and more negative evaluations of the outgroup. However, group membership does not automatically cause discrimination; behaviour also depends on factors such as the situation and group norms.

Students are randomly placed into House Red and House Blue.

A student first thinks of themselves as a “Red” student (Social Categorisation), begins wearing the house colour proudly and celebrating its victories (Social Identification), and then starts saying “Red is better than Blue” and favouring Red students (Social Comparison and possible ingroup favouritism).

Someone begins thinking of themselves as a supporter of a particular team, feels personally proud when the team wins, and judges their team more favourably than a rival team.

Ingroup — a group a person identifies as belonging to.

Outgroup — a group they perceive themselves as not belonging to.

SIT does not mean group membership automatically causes discrimination; rather, categorisation and comparison can create conditions for ingroup favouritism, while behaviour is also affected by context and other factors.

Q1: A student says, “We are the Year 12s; they're only Year 11s.” Which SIT process is most clearly occurring?
Social categorisation — the student is organising people into an ingroup (“we”) and an outgroup (“they”).
Q2: After joining a school house, a student becomes genuinely proud when the house wins even though they did not compete. Which process does this illustrate?
Social identification — membership in the group has become part of the student's self-concept, so the group's success feels personally meaningful.
Q3: Why can social comparison lead to ingroup favouritism?
People may evaluate their own group positively relative to an outgroup because a positively valued group membership can contribute to positive social identity.
A student says, “We are the Year 12s; they're only Year 11s.” Which Social Identity Theory process is happening most clearly?
Social categorisation. The student is dividing people into an ingroup (‘we’) and an outgroup (‘they’). This is the first stage of Social Identity Theory, where people mentally organise themselves and others into social groups.

Social Identity Theory: Tajfel et al. (1971) — Minimal Group Paradigm

Tajfel wanted to strip group membership down to almost nothing. If people still favoured “their” group when the groups were meaningless, then simply being categorised as “us” and “them” might be enough to influence behaviour.

Real groups usually have a history. Football teams have rivalry. National groups have culture and history. Friendship groups have relationships. Political groups have beliefs and competition.

So if those groups favour their own members, it is hard to know why. Was it the rivalry? The history? The personal relationships? Or something else?

Tajfel therefore asked a more basic question: “What happens if we create groups that have almost no meaning at all?”

If favouritism still appears, then previous conflict, friendship, competition and personal relationships cannot easily explain it.

Aim: To investigate whether simply placing people into an in-group and an out-group could be enough to produce in-group favouritism.

The original minimal-group research involved adolescent boys.

They did not begin the experiment as members of meaningful rival groups. They were used to create temporary experimental groups that had no real history.

First, the boys completed a trivial task — for example, saying which paintings they preferred, or making simple perceptual judgements about things such as the number of dots they saw.

They were then told that, based on this task, they belonged to one of two groups. For example, the “Klee” group or the “Kandinsky” group (named after two painters).

The groups were deliberately trivial and had no meaningful history. Members did not know one another as group members, had no previous rivalry, shared no history, and could not personally benefit from the later allocation decisions.

Why does this matter? Tajfel was trying to create the minimum amount of “groupness” possible — a situation where the only thing two people had in common was sharing a label.

Because the groups were stripped down to almost nothing, this approach became known as the minimal group paradigm.

The boys were shown tables containing different combinations of points. Each choice distributed points between one anonymous member of their own group and one anonymous member of the other group.

The boys were not choosing money for themselves. They were deciding how rewards should be distributed to other anonymous participants. The points had monetary value and could be converted into rewards, so the choices had a real consequence.

Here is a simplified example of the kind of choice they faced:

OptionIn-group memberOut-group member
Option A10 points10 points
Option B12 points11 points
Option C7 points1 point

Option A = fairness. Both receive the same.

Option B = higher total reward. Both do fairly well, and together the two members would receive more overall.

Option C = the in-group member receives fewer points than in some other options (7 is less than 10 or 12), but gets a much bigger advantage over the out-group member (6 points ahead, rather than level or 1 point ahead).

To be clear: the real study used systematic point-allocation matrices with many different combinations — these three are simplified examples to show the kinds of choices available.

The boys were not trying to earn points for themselves. The task gave them a choice about how real rewards should be divided between members of “their” group and the “other” group.

The psychologically interesting question was this: would they simply maximise the total amount of reward? Would they divide it fairly? Or would being labelled as part of a group make them prefer outcomes that advantaged their own group?

Their decision therefore revealed how they treated anonymous people once those people had been labelled as “one of us” or “one of them.”

The finding was not simply “participants gave more money to their own group.” The more interesting result was this: participants often showed in-group favouritism, and they sometimes preferred an allocation that gave their own group a greater advantage over the out-group rather than an allocation that would have produced the largest possible total reward.

Consider two possible choices:

ChoiceIn-groupOut-groupMeaning
Choice 11925Total 44 — but the out-group gets more
Choice 271Total only 8 — but the in-group is ahead by 6

A participant choosing the second type of outcome is effectively saying: “I would rather my group receive less overall if it means we come out ahead of them.”

This does not mean every participant always chose the most discriminatory option. Participants showed a reliable tendency toward in-group favouritism and relative in-group advantage.

Nothing meaningful separated the groups. There was no long-standing rivalry, no competition over scarce resources before the task, no personal friendship with the anonymous recipients, and no personal financial gain for the allocator.

Yet simply knowing “I am in this group” and “that person is in the other group” was enough to influence allocation behaviour.

Social Categorisation (“us” vs “them”) → In-group / Out-group Distinction → Possible In-group Favouritism

This provides evidence for a key idea behind Social Identity Theory: social categorisation alone can influence how people treat members of their own group relative to another group.

The study demonstrates that very minimal social categorisation can be sufficient to produce in-group favouritism under controlled conditions.

It does not show that every group produces hostility, that people always discriminate, that categorisation alone explains complex prejudice, or that minimal laboratory groups are identical to ethnic, national, religious or political groups.

Minimal group — a group deliberately created with almost no meaningful history, relationship or competition.

In-group — the group a person believes they belong to.

Out-group — a group they do not belong to.

In-group favouritism — giving more favourable treatment or outcomes to members of one’s own group.

Social categorisation — mentally sorting people into groups such as “us” and “them.”

Point-allocation matrix — a table presenting different possible ways of distributing rewards between an anonymous in-group member and an anonymous out-group member.

Once the matrix task makes sense, researchers describe the strategies participants could use:

Maximum In-group Profit (MIP) — choose the option giving the in-group member the largest absolute amount.

Maximum Joint Profit (MJP) — choose the option producing the largest combined reward for both people.

Maximum Difference (MD) — choose the option creating the largest advantage for the in-group over the out-group.

The important finding is that participants sometimes chose Maximum Difference — sacrificing total reward in order to make the in-group come out ahead.

A participant sees two possible allocations: Option A gives the in-group member 15 points and the out-group member 15 points. Option B gives the in-group member 12 points and the out-group member 5 points. The participant chooses Option B. What does this choice suggest?
The participant sacrificed some possible reward for the in-group member — 15 could have been awarded — in order to create a much larger advantage over the out-group member (only 5, so the in-group is 7 points ahead). This is evidence of relative in-group favouritism.
Does choosing Option B prove the participant hates the out-group?
No. It shows preferential treatment of the in-group in this allocation task. In-group favouritism is not automatically the same as hostility toward the out-group.

Social Identity Theory: Application to Group Behaviour

Social Identity Theory helps explain how simply seeing ourselves as members of a group can influence how we think about and behave toward other people. Once people identify with an ingroup, they may compare it with an outgroup in ways that protect or strengthen a positive social identity. This can contribute to ingroup favouritism, negative stereotypes of outgroups, rivalry and sometimes discrimination.

Two groups of football supporters strongly identify with different teams. A fan may describe their own supporters as “loyal” and “passionate” while describing rival supporters as “aggressive” or “arrogant.” SIT suggests that identification with the ingroup and comparison with the rival outgroup can influence these different evaluations.

Students begin identifying strongly with different school houses. They support members of their own house more enthusiastically, celebrate their successes and may underestimate students from competing houses. The behaviour is not necessarily caused by personal dislike; group categorisation and identification can change how people evaluate others.

Employees from two departments begin thinking in terms of “our team” versus “their team.” They may give more credit to members of their own department and blame the other department when a shared project goes badly. Strong group identities can therefore interfere with cooperation.

SIT can also help psychologists think about ways to reduce destructive intergroup behaviour. One approach is to create a shared or superordinate goal that requires members of different groups to cooperate. For example, two competing school groups could be given a project that can only succeed if both groups contribute. This may encourage people to think of themselves as members of a larger shared group rather than only as separate ingroups and outgroups.

SIT does not predict that group membership will always produce prejudice or discrimination. Group norms, the social situation, previous relationships and other psychological processes also influence behaviour.

Two rival school groups initially compete and criticise each other, but hostility decreases when they must work together to organise a major school event. How could Social Identity Theory explain this change?
At first, students may categorise themselves as members of separate ingroups and compare their group with the rival outgroup, contributing to ingroup favouritism. A shared goal can encourage both groups to see themselves as part of a larger common group. This changes how the social categories are perceived and can reduce the importance of the original ingroup–outgroup distinction.

Social Identity Theory: Evaluation

Research using the minimal group paradigm, including Tajfel et al. (1971), found that people can show ingroup favouritism even when groups are created using trivial and meaningless distinctions. This supports a central prediction of SIT: social categorisation alone can be sufficient to influence how people allocate resources to ingroup and outgroup members under minimal-group conditions.

SIT can be applied to many forms of group behaviour, including school groups, sports supporters, national identity and workplace teams. It provides a useful psychological explanation for why people may favour members of groups with which they identify even when they have little personal knowledge of individual members.

SIT can place strong emphasis on social categorisation and group identity, but real-world intergroup behaviour can also be influenced by competition for resources, group norms, culture, previous experiences, personality and situational factors. Group membership therefore does not automatically produce prejudice or discrimination.

Much of the classic experimental evidence for SIT comes from minimal-group studies in which researchers deliberately create artificial groups. This provides strong experimental control, but these groups lack the history, emotional importance and real conflict found in many natural social groups. This can limit ecological validity when generalising the findings to complex real-world relationships.

Imagine two school houses with a long history of rivalry. SIT can help explain why students favour their own house and compare it positively with the rival house. However, SIT alone may not explain why the rivalry becomes particularly hostile. School norms, previous conflicts, competition for awards and influential group members could also affect behaviour. This demonstrates why SIT is useful but not a complete explanation of group behaviour.

SIT is valuable because it identifies a clear psychological mechanism linking social categorisation, group identification and social comparison to group behaviour, and this mechanism has experimental support. However, it should not be treated as a complete or deterministic explanation. Intergroup behaviour is usually produced by an interaction between social identity and other individual, cultural and situational influences.

A psychologist argues that Tajfel's minimal-group research proves that simply belonging to different groups will always cause prejudice and discrimination. What is wrong with this conclusion?
The conclusion is too deterministic. Tajfel's research shows that social categorisation can be sufficient to produce ingroup favouritism under minimal-group conditions, but it does not show that group membership always causes prejudice or discrimination. Real-world behaviour is also influenced by factors such as group norms, culture, competition, previous experiences and the social situation.

Social Learning Theory & Group Behaviour: Key Concepts

Bandura''s Social Learning Theory proposes that people learn behaviours by observing and imitating models. Four cognitive processes are required:

  1. Attention - Noticing the model's behaviour
  2. Retention - Remembering what was observed
  3. Reproduction - Having the physical ability to copy the behaviour
  4. Motivation - Having a reason to perform it (influenced by vicarious reinforcement)

Identification occurs when the observer perceives similarity to the model, increasing imitation likelihood.

Attention: A child watches an older sibling cook.

Retention: The child remembers the steps the next day.

Reproduction: The child is physically able to perform the steps.

Motivation: The child sees the sibling praised for helping and therefore wants to imitate the behaviour — vicarious reinforcement (observing someone else rewarded).

Identification: Occurs when the observer perceives similarity to the model, increasing likelihood of imitation.

A new student notices that several popular students make sarcastic jokes about teachers and receive laughs and approval from their friends. The student pays attention to this behaviour, remembers the style of the jokes, is capable of reproducing them, and sees the positive reactions as a reason to imitate the behaviour. Over time, the student begins making similar jokes.

SLT explains this behaviour through observational learning. The student attends to and remembers the models' behaviour, is able to reproduce it, and is motivated to imitate it after observing the models receive social approval. This is vicarious reinforcement: the observer sees someone else being rewarded and becomes more likely to imitate the behaviour. Identification may make imitation especially likely if the student sees the popular students as similar, desirable or high-status models.

A student watches a popular classmate receive praise for helping a new student. The next day, they begin helping new students too, even though nobody has directly rewarded them. How would Social Learning Theory explain this behaviour?
The student learned through observation rather than direct reinforcement. They attended to and remembered the classmate's behaviour, were able to reproduce it, and were motivated to imitate it after seeing the classmate receive praise. The praise acts as vicarious reinforcement because observing another person being rewarded increases the likelihood of imitation.

Social Learning Theory & Group Behaviour: SLT Applied to Group Behaviour

Social Learning Theory explains how behaviours can spread through groups. People observe how other group members behave and what happens to them afterwards. They may be especially likely to imitate people they identify with, such as people who are popular, respected, similar to them, or high in status.

Example: A new student notices that everyone listens to one popular student. Because the new student admires them, they may be more likely to copy the way that student talks or behaves.

People do not need to receive a reward themselves to learn from it. If they see someone else being rewarded for a behaviour, they may become more motivated to copy that behaviour. This is called vicarious reinforcement.

Example: A student sees their friend make a funny comment in class and everyone laughs. The student may copy this behaviour later because they saw their friend receive social approval.

  • Acquisition — learning a new behaviour through observation. Example: A new basketball player watches teammates perform a particular celebration after scoring and learns how to do it.
  • Maintenance — continuing a behaviour because it appears to produce positive consequences. Example: The player continues using the celebration because teammates cheer whenever someone does it.
  • Change — adopting a different behaviour after observing influential models. Example: A team captain begins congratulating opponents after games. Other players see that the coach praises this behaviour and gradually begin doing it too.

A student joins a new friendship group where several popular members regularly make fun of another student. At first, the new student does not participate. However, they repeatedly see the comments receive laughter and approval from the group. After several days, the new student begins making similar comments.

How does SLT explain this?

The student observes the behaviour of other group members and sees that it receives social approval. The laughter provides vicarious reinforcement, increasing the student's motivation to imitate the behaviour. Identification may make imitation even more likely because the student admires the popular group members and wants to be accepted by them.

Observe the behaviour → See what happens to the model → Remember the behaviour → Become motivated to imitate → Perform the behaviour

Social Learning Theory does not only explain negative behaviour. Positive behaviours can spread through groups in the same way.

Example: A respected football captain always encourages teammates after they make mistakes. The coach praises the captain for being supportive. Younger players observe this and begin encouraging their teammates too.

The younger players observe a valued model behaving supportively and see the behaviour receive positive consequences. This vicarious reinforcement can increase their motivation to imitate the captain. Over time, observational learning may help supportive behaviour become a group norm.

A new employee notices that respected members of their team always stay late to help colleagues and are praised by the manager. A few weeks later, the employee begins doing the same thing. How would Social Learning Theory explain this?
The employee observes respected team members helping others and sees them receive praise. The praise acts as vicarious reinforcement, making the employee more motivated to imitate the helping behaviour. Identification may also contribute because the employee sees the respected team members as valuable models. This shows how behaviour can spread through a group even when the observer was not originally rewarded directly.

Social Learning Theory & Group Behaviour: Bandura, Ross & Ross (1961) — Bobo Doll Study

Bandura and colleagues used the Bobo doll studies to investigate whether children could learn aggressive behaviour by observing a model, even without being directly rewarded for copying the behaviour.

To investigate whether children would imitate aggressive behaviour after observing an adult model behave aggressively.

Example: Imagine a child watching an older student repeatedly hit a toy while shouting aggressive phrases. SLT predicts that the child may learn some of these behaviours simply by watching, even if nobody directly teaches or rewards the child.

Young children were exposed to different adult models. Some children observed an adult behaving aggressively toward a Bobo doll, including hitting and striking it and using aggressive verbal expressions. Other children were exposed to a non-aggressive model or comparison conditions.

Social Learning Theory & Group Behaviour: Evaluation

Social Learning Theory is useful because it explains how people can learn behaviour by observing other people, without needing to experience reinforcement themselves. However, SLT does not explain every influence on human behaviour.

A major strength of SLT is that it explains how people can learn simply by observing models and the consequences of their behaviour.

Example: A student sees another student being praised for helping clean the classroom. The observer later begins helping too, even though they were never personally rewarded for doing so.

This is difficult to explain using direct reinforcement alone. SLT explains it through observational learning and vicarious reinforcement.

Bandura, Ross & Ross (1961) provides experimental support for observational learning. Children who observed an aggressive adult model were more likely to reproduce aggressive behaviours than children who did not observe the aggressive model.

Because researchers controlled important aspects of the situation, the study provides evidence supporting a relationship between observing a model and later imitation.

Much of the classic evidence for SLT comes from controlled laboratory research such as the Bobo doll study.

Example: Hitting an inflatable Bobo doll after watching an adult hit it is not necessarily the same as attacking another child at school. Children may have understood hitting the doll as part of a game.

This raises questions about ecological validity: behaviour observed in an artificial laboratory may not completely represent behaviour in everyday situations.

SLT can sometimes place strong emphasis on models and the social environment. Human behaviour can also be influenced by biological factors, cognitive processes, personality, previous experiences, cultural norms, and the situation.

Example: Two children might observe exactly the same aggressive model but respond differently. One may imitate the behaviour while the other does not. Differences in identification, motivation, previous experiences, consequences, and other individual factors may influence their responses.

Therefore, observing a behaviour does not automatically mean it will be performed.

Imagine several popular students regularly exclude another student from their friendship group. A new member observes that this behaviour receives approval and laughter.

SLT can explain why the new member may begin copying the exclusion through observational learning, identification and vicarious reinforcement.

However, SLT does not predict that imitation must occur. The student may have different values, may fear punishment, or may belong to another group where exclusion is strongly discouraged.

This shows why SLT is a useful but not deterministic explanation of group behaviour.

Social Learning Theory provides a strong explanation of how behaviour can be acquired through observation and how behaviours may spread through groups. Research such as Bandura, Ross & Ross (1961) provides experimental support for observational learning. However, behaviour is influenced by more than observation alone, and laboratory demonstrations of imitation may not fully represent complex behaviour in everyday life.

Conformity: What Is Conformity?

Conformity is a change in a person's behaviour, attitudes, or beliefs in response to real or perceived group pressure.

Normative social influence occurs when someone conforms because they want to fit in, be accepted, or avoid rejection.

The person may privately disagree with the group but still change their public behaviour.

Example: A student does not think a joke is funny, but everyone in their friendship group is laughing. The student laughs too because they do not want to feel left out.

In simple terms:

"I don't necessarily think they're right — I just want to fit in."

Informational social influence occurs when someone conforms because they believe other people may know more or have better information.

This is especially likely when the situation is uncertain or difficult.

Example: A student thinks the answer to a difficult psychology question is B. Four classmates confidently say the answer is C. The student changes their answer because they believe the others probably understand the question better.

In simple terms:

"I think they might know something I don't."

Normative influence → "I want to fit in."

Informational influence → "I think the group may be right."

Imagine a student joins a new friendship group. Everyone in the group says they love a particular musician.

The student may say they love the musician too because they want the group to accept them. This would be normative social influence.

However, imagine the group recommends a particular way of solving a difficult maths problem. The student follows their method because they believe the others understand the problem better. This would be informational social influence.

The outward behaviour can therefore look similar — following the group — while the psychological reason for conforming is different.

A student believes the answer to a question is A, but every other member of the group says B. The student changes to B because they are worried the others will think they are stupid if they disagree. Is this mainly normative or informational social influence, and why?
This is mainly normative social influence. The student changes their answer because they are concerned about how the group will judge them and want to avoid social rejection. If they changed because they genuinely believed the group knew the correct answer, that would instead demonstrate informational social influence.

Conformity: Conformity vs Compliance

Conformity and compliance both involve social influence, but they happen for different reasons.

Conformity occurs when a person changes their behaviour, attitudes, or beliefs in response to real or perceived group pressure.

There does not need to be a direct request.

Example: A student notices that everyone in their friendship group has started listening to the same artist. Nobody asks the student to listen to the artist, but the student starts listening too because they want to fit in.

This is conformity because the influence comes from the group and its norms, not from a direct request.

Compliance occurs when a person changes their behaviour by agreeing to a direct request from another person.

The person making the request does not necessarily have authority over them.

Example: A classmate asks, "Can I borrow your psychology notes tonight?" You agree and send them the notes.

This is compliance because someone made a direct request and you agreed to it.

Conformity → Group pressure or group norms → No direct request is necessary

Compliance → Someone makes a direct request → You agree to the request

Sometimes the behaviour looks identical, but the reason behind it is different.

Example 1 — Conformity: Everyone in your friendship group begins using a particular slang word. Nobody asks you to use it, but eventually you start using it too.

Example 2 — Compliance: A friend says, "Start using this word—it's funny." You agree and begin using it.

In both situations, your behaviour changes. However, the source of social influence is different.

Imagine a student begins sharing homework answers with members of their friendship group.

If the student starts doing this because everyone in the group shares answers and they want to fit in, this is conformity.

If another student directly says, "Can you send me your homework answers?" and the student agrees, this is compliance.

Psychologists therefore need to understand why the behaviour occurred, rather than classifying behaviour only by what it looks like.

A student starts wearing a school jumper in a particular way because everyone in their friendship group does it. Later, a friend directly asks the student to lend them £10 and the student agrees. Which behaviour demonstrates conformity and which demonstrates compliance?
Changing how the jumper is worn demonstrates conformity because the student's behaviour changes in response to a group norm without a direct request. Lending the £10 demonstrates compliance because the student agrees to a direct request from another person.

Conformity: Evaluation

Research such as Asch (1951) provides important evidence that group pressure can influence behaviour. However, psychologists must consider both the strengths and limitations of this evidence before applying it to everyday conformity.

Asch used a highly controlled procedure. Participants completed the same line-judgement task while the researcher controlled when the confederates gave incorrect answers.

This made it easier to investigate the effect of majority group pressure on conformity.

Example: If the researcher changes only whether the other group members give correct or incorrect answers while keeping the line task the same, differences in participants' responses can be more confidently linked to the group situation.

This gives the research strong internal validity compared with simply observing conformity in everyday life, where many factors may be changing at once.

Matching the lengths of lines in a laboratory is very different from many situations where people experience conformity in everyday life.

Example: Choosing the wrong line because several strangers chose it is very different from agreeing with close friends about whether to exclude someone from the friendship group. The real-life situation has relationships, emotions and possible social consequences.

This raises concerns about ecological validity. Behaviour in an artificial laboratory task may not completely represent conformity in more meaningful real-world situations.

Asch's original research used male college students in the United States.

This makes it difficult to assume that everyone would respond to group pressure in exactly the same way.

Example: A group of American male college students from the 1950s may respond differently from younger students, older adults, women, or people living in different cultural environments.

This limits the generalisability of the findings.

Conformity does not occur at the same level in every group or situation. Cultural norms, group relationships, the importance of the decision, and whether the group is unanimous can all influence conformity.

Example: A student may easily conform to friends about where to eat lunch but refuse to conform when the group suggests doing something the student believes is seriously wrong.

Therefore, Asch's findings should not be interpreted as showing that people will automatically follow a majority.

Imagine a student disagrees with their friendship group's opinion about a new classmate. Everyone else says the classmate is annoying, so the student publicly agrees even though they privately think the classmate seems friendly.

Asch's research can help explain how majority pressure may contribute to the student's conformity.

However, we cannot predict the student's behaviour from Asch's research alone. Their relationship with the group, confidence, cultural background, personal values and consequences of disagreeing may also influence whether they conform.

Conformity research is valuable because controlled experiments such as Asch (1951) demonstrate that majority group pressure can influence behaviour. However, artificial tasks and restricted samples limit how confidently the findings can be generalised to complex real-world groups. Conformity should therefore be understood as being influenced by both group pressure and the wider social and cultural situation.

The conclusion is too strong. Asch demonstrated that majority pressure can increase conformity, but participants did not conform on every trial and some resisted the majority. The study also used an artificial line-judgement task and a limited sample. Therefore, the research shows that group pressure can influence behaviour, not that people will always follow a group.

Compliance Techniques: Overview

Compliance occurs when a person changes their behaviour in response to a direct request from another person.

Compliance occurs when a person changes their behaviour in response to a direct request from another person.

Unlike conformity, where people may respond to group pressure without anyone directly asking them to do something, compliance involves an explicit request.

Example: A friend asks, "Can you help me move these boxes?" You agree and help them. This is compliance because your behaviour changed in response to a direct request.

Psychologists have identified techniques that can make people more likely to agree to a request.

In this unit, you will focus on two:

Foot-in-the-Door (FITD)

Start with a small request that the person is likely to accept. After they agree, make a larger related request.

Small request → agreement → larger request

Example:

"Can you answer two questions for my survey?"

Later: "Would you complete the full 15-minute survey?"

Door-in-the-Face (DITF)

Start with a very large request that the person is likely to refuse. After they refuse, make a smaller, more reasonable request.

Large request → refusal → smaller request

Example:

"Can you volunteer every Saturday for the next six months?"

After the person refuses: "Could you volunteer for just one Saturday?"

Foot-in-the-Door: SMALL first → LARGE second

Door-in-the-Face: LARGE first → SMALLER second

A useful way to remember them:

FITD = get your foot in with something small.

DITF = make such a large first request that the door is metaphorically slammed in your face.

The two techniques can increase compliance through different psychological processes.

With Foot-in-the-Door, agreeing to the first request may encourage the person to see themselves as someone who supports the cause or helps in that situation. Agreeing to the larger request can then appear consistent with their earlier behaviour.

Example: After signing a petition about protecting local parks, someone may be more willing to donate money to the same environmental campaign.

With Door-in-the-Face, the requester appears to make a concession by moving from a very large request to a smaller one. The other person may feel pressure to make a reciprocal concession by agreeing to the smaller request.

Example: After refusing to donate $100 to a charity, a request for $10 may suddenly seem much more reasonable.

Student B:

"Could you organise the entire event with me this month?"

After the classmate refuses, they ask:

This follows Door-in-the-Face because the first request is very large and the second request is smaller.

"FITD causes people to comply."

A more accurate psychological conclusion is:

"FITD can increase the likelihood of compliance under certain conditions."

Participants who had previously agreed to a small request were more likely to agree to the larger request than participants who received the large request immediately.

When the requester moves from a large request to a smaller one, it can appear that they have made a concession.

The other person may then feel social pressure to make a concession in return by agreeing to the smaller request.

"Ask for something huge and then ask for something smaller."

The psychological explanation matters.

The requester appears to move toward the participant:

"Okay, you don't have to make this enormous commitment. How about this much smaller one?"

The participant may then feel pressure to move toward the requester in return by accepting the smaller request.

Cultural Dimensions: Individualism vs Collectivism

Individualism and collectivism describe broad cultural tendencies in how people understand the relationship between the individual and the group.

Individualistic cultures tend to place greater emphasis on personal independence, individual goals, autonomy and self-expression.

People may be encouraged to make decisions based on their own preferences and personal goals.

Example: A student chooses a university course mainly because it matches their own interests and career goals, even though their family would prefer them to study something else.

This reflects greater emphasis on individual choice and personal goals.

Collectivistic cultures tend to place greater emphasis on group goals, social harmony, interdependence and responsibilities toward important groups, such as family.

People may be more likely to consider how their decisions affect other group members.

Example: A student considers their family's expectations when choosing a university because maintaining family harmony and fulfilling responsibilities toward the family are important to them.

This reflects greater emphasis on interdependence and group considerations.

Individualism → greater emphasis on "I"

Personal goals • independence • autonomy • individual choice

Collectivism → greater emphasis on "we"

Group goals • interdependence • harmony • responsibilities to the group

These cultural tendencies may influence how people respond to group expectations, relationships and social norms.

Example: Imagine a student disagrees with their friendship group.

A student who strongly values individual autonomy may feel comfortable openly expressing their disagreement.

A student who strongly values group harmony may decide not to openly challenge the group because maintaining the relationship is more important in that situation.

This does not mean that individualistic people never conform or that collectivistic people always conform. Cultural values are only one influence on behaviour.

Psychologists must be careful when using cultural dimensions.

Saying:

"Culture X is collectivistic, therefore everyone from Culture X will behave collectivistically"

is inaccurate.

Individuals within the same country can differ greatly because of personality, family, age, region, socioeconomic background, personal experiences and the particular situation.

Cultural dimensions are therefore best understood as group-level tendencies, not descriptions or predictions of every individual.

Student B mainly considers:

"How will this decision affect my family, and what would be best for us?"

Individualism and collectivism could help psychologists understand why the students may consider different factors when making the same decision.

However, we cannot determine their behaviour from culture alone. Both students may consider personal goals and family responsibilities.

Culture should usually be treated as an influence on behaviour rather than a deterministic cause.

A strong psychological explanation therefore says:

"Cultural values may influence the likelihood of particular behaviours."

rather than:

"Culture makes people behave this way."

How Can Culture Influence Group Behaviour?

Culture provides shared values, expectations and norms that can influence how people behave within groups.

Cultural values may influence how important it is to agree with or maintain harmony within a group.

Example: A student strongly disagrees with a decision made by their friendship group. If maintaining group harmony is highly valued, the student may be less willing to openly challenge the group.

In another social environment, openly expressing an individual opinion may be more strongly encouraged.

This means the same group situation could produce different behaviour depending partly on the cultural norms surrounding the individual.

Bond and Smith (1996) conducted a meta-analysis of conformity studies based on Asch's line-judgement procedure.

A meta-analysis statistically combines results from multiple studies to identify broader patterns across research.

They examined research conducted across different countries and found that levels of conformity varied across cultural contexts.

Overall, conformity tended to be higher in more collectivistic cultural contexts than in more individualistic cultural contexts.

This supports the idea that cultural values can contribute to differences in group behaviour.

In cultural contexts where interdependence and group harmony are strongly valued, responding to the expectations of an important group may sometimes be considered appropriate social behaviour.

Example: Choosing not to openly contradict members of an important family group could help maintain harmony. From this perspective, agreement with the group does not necessarily mean that someone is weak or unable to think independently.

The meaning of conformity itself can therefore depend on cultural context.

Bond and Smith's findings do not mean:

"Collectivistic people conform and individualistic people do not."

That would be an inaccurate stereotype.

Conformity can also depend on group size, unanimity, the person's relationship with the group, the importance of the decision, individual differences and the particular situation.

Cultures also contain considerable variation between individuals.

A stronger conclusion is:

"Cultural values such as individualism and collectivism may contribute to differences in the likelihood of conformity across cultural contexts."

Acculturation is the process of psychological and cultural change that can occur when individuals or groups from different cultural backgrounds come into continuous contact.

Acculturation: Why Strategies Differ

People do not simply choose an acculturation strategy in isolation. The strategy that develops can be influenced by the receiving culture, social support, individual circumstances and cultural distance. For example, two international students might both want to maintain their original culture while becoming involved in their new school. One attends a welcoming school that encourages cultural diversity and quickly develops supportive friendships, while the other experiences exclusion and struggles to communicate. Even though both students initially want to integrate, their different social environments may contribute to very different acculturation experiences.

The receptiveness of the receiving culture refers to how accepting the wider society is of people maintaining different cultural identities while participating in society. For example, a school that encourages students to speak their heritage languages, celebrates different cultural traditions and actively includes international students may make integration easier than a school where students feel pressure to abandon their original cultural identity.

The surrounding culture can therefore affect which acculturation strategies are realistically available.

Social support from family, friends, schools and communities can make cultural adaptation easier. For example, a newly arrived student who has supportive family members, helpful teachers and friends from both their original and new cultural environments may find it easier to maintain connections with both cultures.

In contrast, someone who is socially isolated may find cultural adaptation more difficult.

Cultural distance refers to the degree of difference between someone's original cultural environment and the new one. The greater the differences in areas such as language, social norms, values and everyday expectations, the more adjustment may be required.

Example: Moving between two countries where the student already speaks the language and recognises many of the school and social expectations may require less adjustment than moving somewhere with a completely unfamiliar language and very different expectations about classroom behaviour, family roles and social interaction.

Greater cultural distance does not automatically cause unsuccessful adaptation, but it may increase the number of changes a person has to manage.

Individual characteristics and circumstances can also influence acculturation. Factors such as age, language ability, previous intercultural experience, coping strategies and personal goals may affect how someone responds.

Example: A student who already speaks the local language may find it easier to develop friendships in the wider culture than someone who cannot yet communicate confidently.

However, psychologists should avoid assuming that acculturation outcomes simply reflect an individual's personality. Individual and environmental factors interact.

These factors do not operate separately.

Acculturation: Consequences

Acculturation can be associated with both positive experiences and psychological challenges, and outcomes differ considerably between individuals. For example, a student who moves to another country may develop new friendships, learn another language and successfully maintain connections with their original culture. Another student may struggle with discrimination, conflicting family expectations and uncertainty about where they belong. Both are experiencing acculturation, but the consequences are very different.

Research on acculturation has often found that integration tends, on average, to be associated with more positive psychological and sociocultural adjustment because people can maintain connections with their original culture while also participating in the wider cultural environment.

Example: An international student might continue speaking their family's language and maintaining heritage friendships while also developing friendships at their new school. Having access to support and belonging in both cultural environments may contribute to better adjustment.

However, integration does not guarantee positive outcomes. The surrounding society must also provide genuine opportunities for participation and acceptance.

Marginalisation involves relatively little connection with either the original cultural group or the wider cultural environment. This pattern has often been associated with poorer psychological adjustment.

Example: A teenager might feel disconnected from their family's cultural traditions while also feeling rejected by peers in the wider society. A lack of belonging and social support could contribute to loneliness, identity difficulties or stress.

This is an association, not a rule. Psychologists should not assume that every person classified as marginalised will experience the same psychological outcomes.

Acculturative stress is psychological stress associated with difficulties that can arise while adapting to contact between different cultural environments. For example, a student may be trying to learn in a new language, make friends, understand unfamiliar social expectations and satisfy their family's expectations about maintaining their original culture. If managing these competing demands becomes stressful, the student may experience acculturative stress.

Acculturative stress can be influenced by:

  • language difficulties
  • discrimination and prejudice
  • conflicting cultural expectations
  • loss of familiar social support
  • uncertainty about identity or belonging
  • difficulties participating in the wider society

Acculturation does not automatically cause acculturative stress. The level of stress depends partly on the challenges and resources surrounding the individual.

Social support can help people manage the challenges associated with cultural adaptation. For example, a newly arrived student who has supportive family members, helpful teachers and friends may have people to turn to when experiencing language difficulties or uncertainty about unfamiliar social expectations.

A person experiencing similar cultural changes without those supportive relationships may find the process more difficult.

Social support may therefore act as a protective factor, although it does not guarantee that someone will experience no difficulties.

Acculturation can sometimes create conflict between expectations from different cultural environments. For example, a teenager's parents may expect important decisions to prioritise family responsibilities, while their friendship group encourages greater independence and individual choice.

The teenager may value both sets of relationships, making the situation more complicated than simply choosing one culture over another.

A student moves to a new country and initially participates actively in school. Over time, they experience repeated jokes about their accent and feel pressure at home to avoid becoming "too different" from their family.

The student gradually becomes quieter at school and begins avoiding some social situations.

Acculturative stress could help explain this behavioural change. The student is managing discrimination, belonging and conflicting cultural expectations.

However, psychologists should not conclude:

"Acculturation caused the student to withdraw."

Their behaviour could also be influenced by personality, previous experiences, relationships, school environment and other psychological factors.

Acculturation outcomes are probabilistic rather than deterministic.

Integration tends to be associated with better adjustment, but this does not mean integration automatically causes good psychological outcomes.

Marginalisation tends to be associated with poorer adjustment, but this does not mean every marginalised individual will experience psychological problems.

Factors such as social support, discrimination, cultural distance and the receptiveness of the receiving culture can help explain why outcomes differ.

Acculturation: Evaluation

Berry's acculturation model is useful because it gives psychologists a clear way to understand how people may relate to both their original culture and the wider cultural environment, but real cultural adaptation is often more complicated than four categories suggest. For example, an international student might appear to use integration at school by maintaining their original identity while developing local friendships, yet behave more like separation at home where family traditions dominate. This shows both the usefulness and the limitation of trying to place a complex cultural experience into a single category.

Berry's model distinguishes between integration, assimilation, separation and marginalisation, rather than assuming that everyone responds to cultural contact in the same way. For example, two students could move to the same country, yet one maintains strong connections with their original culture while participating in the wider culture, whereas another gradually adopts the wider culture and becomes less connected to their original cultural identity.

This makes the model useful for describing different patterns of acculturation.

The model does not assume that adapting to a new cultural environment requires abandoning the original culture. Integration allows someone to maintain meaningful connections with both.

For example, someone might speak Mandarin with their family, celebrate Chinese traditions and identify strongly with Chinese culture while also speaking English, developing local friendships and participating fully in Canadian society.

Cultural identity therefore does not necessarily require choosing one culture or the other.

Real cultural identities can be complex and situation-dependent, making four categories potentially too rigid. For example, a teenager might maintain their family's culture strongly at home, participate extensively in the wider culture at school, and sometimes feel disconnected from both when interacting with another friendship group.

Calling that person simply “integrated” or “separated” may hide important differences between different areas of their life.

Acculturation is a dynamic process, meaning someone's relationship with different cultural environments can change. For example, a newly arrived student might initially spend most of their time with people from their original cultural community because they have limited ability in the local language. Several years later, they may develop strong local friendships while continuing to maintain their original cultural identity.

Their acculturation pattern has changed over time rather than remaining fixed.

Berry's four strategies can sound like personal choices, but individuals do not always have equal opportunities to choose how they participate in society. For example, a migrant may want to integrate by maintaining their original culture while participating fully in the wider society, but discrimination or exclusion may make participation difficult.

Wider issues such as prejudice, discrimination, social inequality and the receptiveness of the receiving culture can therefore influence the acculturation process.

This is important because psychologists should not automatically interpret someone's acculturation strategy as simply a matter of personal preference.

A psychologist observes that an international student mainly socialises with people from their original cultural community and concludes:

“The student has chosen separation because they do not want to participate in the wider culture.”

This conclusion may be too simplistic.

The student might actually want greater involvement but face language barriers, discrimination, limited opportunities for interaction or a lack of social support.

Berry's model can describe the pattern of behaviour, but understanding why that pattern developed requires examining the person's wider social environment.

Berry's model provides a useful framework for understanding different patterns of cultural adaptation, but the four strategies should not be treated as fixed personality types.

Real acculturation can:

  • differ across situations
  • change over time
  • be influenced by social support
  • be constrained by discrimination and exclusion
  • involve complex and overlapping cultural identities

The four strategies are therefore best treated as a framework for understanding patterns of acculturation rather than fixed labels that determine individual behaviour.

Group Behaviour Application Challenge: Scenario 1 — The Office Rivalry

Can Several Psychological Explanations Work Together?

Real group behaviour is rarely explained completely by one psychological theory. Imagine two departments in a company competing for the same budget. Employees begin describing the other department as lazy and incompetent, copying hostile jokes made by respected colleagues and agreeing with negative opinions during meetings even when they privately think those opinions are exaggerated. Social Identity Theory, Social Learning Theory and conformity can each help explain different parts of the same behaviour.

This challenge brings together the major ideas from the Group Behaviour section.

The Scenario

Two departments — Marketing and Product Development — work for the same company.

Management announces that only one department will receive additional funding next year.

Over the following weeks:

  • employees increasingly talk about “us” and “them”
  • Marketing employees describe Product Development as arrogant and difficult
  • Product Development employees describe Marketing as lazy and superficial
  • respected senior employees begin making jokes about the rival department
  • newer employees start repeating those jokes
  • some employees publicly agree with negative comments even though privately they think the rivalry has become ridiculous
  • employees become less willing to share information with the other department

Both departments actually have similar performance records.

Social Identity Theory (SIT) can explain how categorising employees into Marketing and Product Development may contribute to an ingroup–outgroup distinction. Once employees identify strongly with their own department, they may compare it favourably with the rival department in order to maintain a positive social identity.

For example, a Marketing employee might begin saying “our department actually understands customers — they don't”, despite having little evidence that Product Development performs worse.

This demonstrates social categorisation, social identification and social comparison, which may contribute to ingroup favouritism and negative views of the outgroup.

Social Learning Theory (SLT) can help explain how particular behaviours spread through each department. Newer employees observe respected senior colleagues making hostile jokes about the rival group. If those jokes receive laughter and approval, the newer employees may be motivated to imitate them through vicarious reinforcement.

For example, a new employee notices that a popular manager receives laughter whenever they mock Product Development. The employee later begins making similar jokes.

The employee has learned a group behaviour through observation, identification and the consequences experienced by the model.

Conformity can explain why employees may publicly agree with the group even when they privately disagree. For example, an employee might think the criticism of Marketing is unfair but remain silent during a meeting because everyone else appears to agree and they do not want to be rejected by their team.

This would be an example of normative social influence: the employee conforms primarily to gain acceptance or avoid social rejection.

If an inexperienced employee instead assumes that experienced colleagues must know something they do not, informational social influence could contribute.

One possible approach would be to create a superordinate goal that neither department can achieve alone.

For example, management could create a major project requiring Marketing and Product Development to combine their expertise to launch a new product successfully. Instead of competing for separate departmental success, employees would have a meaningful shared goal requiring cooperation.

This connects with ideas behind intergroup contact and cooperation: simply placing rival groups together is not necessarily enough. The conditions surrounding their interaction matter.

Imagine a new employee joins Marketing.

During their first week, they hear respected colleagues repeatedly criticising Product Development. Everyone laughs at hostile jokes, and employees who defend Product Development are treated as though they are disloyal to Marketing.

After several weeks, the new employee begins repeating the same jokes.

Several processes could operate together:

  • SIT: Identifying with Marketing creates an ingroup–outgroup distinction.
  • SLT: The employee observes respected models being socially rewarded for hostile jokes.
  • Conformity: The employee experiences pressure to behave consistently with the group's norms.

The theories are therefore complementary rather than necessarily competing explanations.

Complex behaviour can have multiple interacting explanations.

A strong psychological explanation should therefore avoid saying:

“SIT caused the office rivalry.”

Instead:

“SIT may help explain the development of ingroup–outgroup distinctions, while SLT and conformity may help explain how hostile behaviours and group norms spread and are maintained.”

This demonstrates more sophisticated psychological reasoning because it recognises that different mechanisms can contribute to the same observable behaviour.

A new employee begins repeating hostile jokes about another department after watching respected colleagues receive laughter and approval for making the same jokes. Which theory best explains how this specific behaviour was learned, and what is the mechanism?
Social Learning Theory most directly explains how the specific behaviour was learned. The employee observed respected models making the jokes and saw them receive social approval. This vicarious reinforcement, combined with identification with the models, could increase the employee's motivation to imitate the behaviour. SIT may also explain why the rival department is viewed as an outgroup, but SLT most directly explains the acquisition of the specific joking behaviour.

  • he begins calling members of the community “us”

  • he adopts slang commonly used by the group
  • he copies jokes made by popular members
  • he begins expressing negative opinions about rival groups
  • he agrees publicly with group opinions that he privately doubts
  • he spends increasing amounts of time interacting with the community

Social Identity Theory can explain why membership in the gaming community becomes part of Leo's social identity. By categorising people as members of “our community” and “their community,” Leo creates an ingroup–outgroup distinction.

For example, when Leo begins saying “our players are smarter than theirs,” he is making a favourable comparison between his ingroup and an outgroup.

The process can be traced as:

Social categorisation → Social identification → Social comparison → Possible ingroup favouritism

SIT therefore helps explain why group membership changes how Leo sees his own group and rival groups.

Social Learning Theory can explain how Leo acquires specific behaviours used by members of the community. For example, Leo watches a popular player use a particular insult during a livestream. Other members respond with laughter and approval, and Leo later begins using the same insult himself.

Attention → Retention → Reproduction → Motivation

Leo may also identify with the popular player, making imitation more likely.

The approval received by the model provides vicarious reinforcement, giving Leo information about the social consequences of the behaviour without Leo having to experience those consequences first.

Conformity can explain why Leo sometimes publicly agrees with the group even when he privately disagrees. For example, Leo may think a rival player actually performed well but post that the player was terrible because everyone else in the community is criticising them.

If Leo does this because he wants acceptance and fears being rejected, this demonstrates normative social influence.

If Leo is inexperienced and believes the other members probably understand the game better than he does, informational social influence may instead contribute.

SIT → Why does group membership become psychologically important?

SLT → How are specific behaviours learned by observing other members?

Conformity → Why might someone follow group norms or opinions?

They are therefore not necessarily competing explanations.

We are now shifting to interpersonal relationships — relationships and interactions between specific individuals. For example, two close friends might begin arguing because one interprets the other's short text messages as signs that they are angry. Here, the important psychological question is not primarily how a group influenced them, but how psychological processes are shaping the relationship between two people.

Social relationships involve connections between individuals and the psychological processes that can influence how those relationships develop and function. Understanding Interpersonal Relationships: Four Required Explanatory Areas

There is no single explanation for why relationships develop, succeed or experience conflict. Psychologists can examine the same relationship from different levels of explanation. For example, if two friends repeatedly misunderstand each other, a psychologist might examine how they interpret each other's behaviour, how they communicate, whether biological processes contribute to bonding and trust, and what psychological strategies might help improve their interactions.

In this section, we will examine four major explanatory areas.

Cognitive explanations examine how mental processes such as schemas, expectations, interpretations and attributions influence relationships.

For example, if someone has developed the expectation that “people cannot be trusted,” they may interpret a friend's unanswered message as deliberate rejection rather than considering that the friend may simply be busy.

The person's existing beliefs have influenced their interpretation, which may then influence their behaviour toward the friend.

Relationships are influenced not only by what people say, but also by how messages are communicated and interpreted.

For example, the word “Fine.” might sound genuinely positive when spoken with a smile and warm tone but appear angry or dismissive when sent as a text message without facial expression or tone of voice.

We will examine how language, communication patterns and non-verbal information can influence relationships and conflict.

Biological processes may also contribute to social behaviour. Chemical messengers, including hormones and neurotransmitters, can be associated with processes involved in bonding, trust, mood and social interaction.

For example, oxytocin has been studied in relation to bonding and trust. However, this does not mean that oxytocin simply “creates love” or determines whether a relationship will succeed. Its effects depend on the person, situation and wider social context.

Biological explanations therefore describe one influence among several.

Psychology can also investigate whether particular strategies can improve relationships or reduce conflict.

For example, someone who immediately thinks “They ignored me because they don't respect me” might learn to reframe the situation by considering another possible explanation: “Maybe they were distracted and didn't notice me.”

Other approaches may involve improving communication, increasing empathy or creating conditions that encourage positive interaction between people or groups.

Imagine two friends have a serious argument after one fails to reply to a message.

A psychologist could investigate:

Cognition → How was the unanswered message interpreted?

Communication → Was meaning lost because tone and non-verbal information were absent?

Chemical messengers → Could biological processes associated with emotion or social behaviour contribute?

Improvement strategies → Could reframing or better communication reduce the conflict?

These explanations do not necessarily compete. Different psychological processes can interact to influence the same relationship.

Cognitive Explanations of Relationships: The Core Mechanism

How Can Thinking Shape a Relationship?

Cognitive explanations of relationships propose that the way we think about other people influences how we respond to them. Imagine Maya sends her friend a message and receives no reply for several hours. Maya already believes that people often lose interest in her, so she interprets the silence as “She's ignoring me because she doesn't care.” Maya becomes distant the next day. Her friend notices the cold behaviour and becomes distant too.

The important point is that the unanswered message did not directly produce Maya's behaviour. Maya's interpretation of the message influenced her response.

A useful way to understand this is:

Existing schema → Interpretation → Expectation/Judgement → Behavioural response → Relationship consequence

Each stage can influence the next.

A schema is a mental framework developed through experience that helps us organise and interpret information.

Relationship schemas can contain expectations about ourselves and other people. For example, someone whose previous friendships involved betrayal might develop the expectation that “people cannot be trusted.”

When something ambiguous happens in a new friendship, that schema may influence what the person notices and how they interpret it.

Because social behaviour is often ambiguous, the same event can be interpreted differently.

Example: Two people receive the message:

“Fine.”

One interprets it as:

“They're angry with me.”

Another interprets it as:

“They're probably busy.”

The external information is identical, but their interpretations differ.

Interpretations can then create expectations about what another person is likely to do.

For example, if Alex interprets his friend's unanswered message as rejection, he may begin expecting:

“They're going to stop being my friend.”

That expectation can influence how Alex behaves during the next interaction.

Thoughts and expectations can influence behaviour.

If Alex expects rejection, he might:

  • become defensive
  • stop initiating conversations
  • respond coldly
  • pay greater attention to signs of rejection

His cognition has therefore begun to affect the relationship itself.

Imagine:

Schema: “People are unreliable.”

↓

Ambiguous event: Friend replies several hours late.

↓

Interpretation: “They don't care about me.”

↓

Expectation: “They will let me down again.”

↓

Behaviour: Acts coldly and stops initiating conversation.

↓

Relationship consequence: Friend becomes more distant.

This demonstrates how cognition can contribute to a feedback process within a relationship.

Schemas may influence:

  • Attention → What information we notice.
  • Interpretation → What we think another person's behaviour means.
  • Memory → Which interactions we remember most easily.
  • Expectations → What we think another person will do next.
  • Behaviour → How we respond to that person.

This helps explain why two people can experience similar events but respond very differently.

Liam has previously experienced several friendships in which people broke his trust.

“He's making excuses. I knew I couldn't trust him.”

However, we should not conclude that schemas are the only cause of Liam's behaviour. The actual behaviour of his friend, previous experiences, emotions and wider social context may also contribute.

Cognitive Explanations of Relationships: Darley & Gross (1983) — Supporting Evidence

Can Expectations Change How We Judge Another Person?

Darley and Gross (1983) investigated whether expectations about another person's background could influence how people interpreted that person's behaviour. Participants were given information suggesting that a young girl came from either a high socioeconomic status (SES) or low socioeconomic status (SES) background. When participants later observed information about the girl's academic performance, their prior expectations influenced their judgements of her ability.

The study provides evidence for an important cognitive idea: what we already believe or expect can influence how we interpret information about another person.

Darley and Gross wanted to investigate whether expectations created by socioeconomic information would influence judgements about a child's academic ability.

In other words:

Can a schema influence how people interpret later information about someone?

Participants saw information about a young girl named Hannah.

Some participants were shown information suggesting Hannah came from a high-SES background, while others were shown information suggesting she came from a low-SES background.

Participants then received information about Hannah's academic performance.

Importantly, participants could be exposed to the same behavioural information, but they approached that information with different expectations about Hannah.

When participants had information about Hannah's performance, their earlier expectations about her socioeconomic background influenced their judgements.

Participants who expected Hannah to have higher academic ability tended to interpret the performance more favourably, while participants with lower expectations tended to judge it less favourably.

The important finding is therefore not simply:

“People stereotyped Hannah.”

It is that prior expectations influenced the interpretation of subsequent information.

The process can be understood as:

Information about SES

↓

Expectation/schema about Hannah

↓

Observation of her performance

↓

Interpretation influenced by prior expectation

↓

Different judgement of Hannah's ability

This provides evidence that cognition can operate top-down: existing expectations influence how incoming information is interpreted.

Similar processes can occur in everyday relationships.

Example: A student is told before meeting a new classmate:

“He's really unfriendly.”

Later, the classmate gives several short answers during a conversation.

The student may interpret those answers as evidence that the classmate is rude.

Someone without the original expectation might instead think:

“Maybe he's shy.”

Prior expectations can therefore influence how ambiguous social behaviour is interpreted.

The controlled procedure allowed researchers to manipulate information about Hannah's background while examining how this influenced later judgements.

This provides evidence that expectations can influence social perception rather than simply showing that different people naturally form different opinions.

Judging information about a child in a research setting is much simpler than interpreting someone within a genuine friendship or romantic relationship.

Real relationships involve:

  • repeated interactions
  • emotions
  • communication
  • shared history
  • feedback from both people

The findings therefore support a cognitive mechanism, but we should be cautious about assuming that the exact same effect operates identically in every real relationship.

Before meeting Daniel, Sofia is told:

“Daniel is arrogant.”

During their first conversation, Daniel checks his phone twice.

Sofia thinks:

“See? He doesn't care what I'm saying.”

Another person might interpret the same behaviour as:

“Maybe he's waiting for an important message.”

Darley and Gross's research helps explain how Sofia's prior expectation may influence her interpretation of ambiguous behaviour.

It does not prove that the expectation is the only explanation for her judgement.

Two participants observe the same ambiguous behaviour from another person but judge it differently after receiving different information about that person's background. What psychological mechanism does this demonstrate?
It demonstrates how schemas and expectations can influence interpretation. Prior information creates different expectations, which can then shape how the same incoming behaviour is understood. Darley and Gross (1983) therefore provide evidence for the role of top-down cognitive processing in social perception.

Cognitive Explanations of Relationships: Application to Relationships

How Can a Schema Actually Change a Relationship?

A relationship schema does more than influence what someone thinks — it can influence how they behave toward another person, which can then change how that person responds. Imagine Daniel has developed the belief “people eventually abandon me.” His friend cancels plans because she is sick, but Daniel interprets the cancellation as evidence that she is losing interest in the friendship. He becomes distant, she notices his cold behaviour and begins contacting him less often. Daniel then thinks, “I knew she would abandon me.”

His original belief has contributed to behaviour that may help produce the very outcome he expected.

This is called a self-fulfilling prophecy.

A self-fulfilling prophecy occurs when an expectation influences behaviour in ways that contribute to making the expected outcome more likely.

The process can be:

Existing belief → Interpretation → Expectation → Behaviour → Other person's response → Original belief appears confirmed

Importantly, this does not mean thoughts magically make events happen.

The mechanism works because expectations can change behaviour, and that behaviour can influence other people.

Imagine Sofia has developed the schema:

“People are untrustworthy.”

Her partner arrives home later than expected.

Sofia:

  • Pays attention to: the late arrival.
  • Interprets it as: “They're hiding something.”
  • Expects: dishonesty.
  • Behaves by: becoming suspicious and repeatedly questioning them.
  • Partner responds by: becoming frustrated and less communicative.

Sofia concludes:

“See? I knew I couldn't trust them.”

Her schema has influenced how she selected, interpreted and responded to information, potentially changing the interaction itself.

Confirmation bias is the tendency to give greater attention or weight to information that supports an existing belief while overlooking information that challenges it.

For example, if Sofia believes her partner is unreliable, she may strongly remember the two occasions they arrived late while paying much less attention to the many occasions they arrived on time.

Her existing belief can therefore become increasingly difficult to challenge.

Cognitive processes do not only create relationship problems.

Imagine Alex generally expects his close friend to be supportive. When the friend sends an unusually short message, Alex thinks:

“They're probably having a difficult day.”

Instead of responding defensively, Alex asks:

“Everything okay?”

His interpretation encourages supportive behaviour rather than conflict.

Schemas and expectations can therefore influence relationships in both helpful and unhelpful ways.

Mia believes:

“My friends eventually get bored of me.”

A close friend does not invite her to one social event.

Mia interprets this as rejection and begins ignoring the friend at school.

The friend becomes confused and eventually stops approaching Mia.

Mia concludes:

“I was right. She doesn't want to be my friend anymore.”

A cognitive explanation can trace:

Schema → Interpretation of exclusion → Expectation of rejection → Withdrawal → Friend withdraws → Schema appears confirmed

This illustrates a possible self-fulfilling prophecy.

However, psychologists should not assume Mia's cognition is the only influence. The friend's actual behaviour, communication between them and wider social circumstances may also contribute.

A strong answer should explain the mechanism, not merely state:

“Schemas affect relationships.”

Instead:

“Existing schemas may influence how ambiguous social information is interpreted. These interpretations can create expectations that influence behaviour toward another person. Because that behaviour can change how the other person responds, the original expectation may sometimes become self-fulfilling.”

A student believes that people cannot be trusted. When a friend cancels plans, the student assumes the friend is lying, becomes distant and stops inviting them anywhere. The friend eventually stops contacting the student. How could this become a self-fulfilling prophecy?
The student's existing schema influences the interpretation of the cancelled plans. This creates an expectation that the friend is untrustworthy, which leads the student to withdraw. The friend's later withdrawal may partly be a response to this behaviour, making the student's original belief appear correct. The expectation therefore influenced behaviour in a way that contributed to the predicted outcome.

Cognitive Explanations of Relationships: Evaluation

How Strong Is the Cognitive Explanation?

Cognitive explanations are useful because they help explain why the same social behaviour can produce very different reactions in different people. For example, two friends both receive the message “OK.” One interprets it as angry because they expect rejection, while the other assumes the sender is simply busy. The external information is identical, but different expectations produce different interpretations.

However, explaining relationships only through cognition would be too simplistic because relationships are also influenced by communication, social experience, culture, biological processes and the actual behaviour of other people.

Cognitive explanations can explain why people respond differently to the same social information.

Example: Two students are left out of a group chat.

Student A thinks:

“They probably forgot to add me.”

Student B thinks:

“They deliberately excluded me because they don't like me.”

Their different schemas and expectations may contribute to different emotional and behavioural responses.

This helps explain individual differences in interpersonal behaviour.

Darley and Gross (1983) provide evidence that prior expectations can influence how people interpret information about another person.

Participants' expectations about Hannah's socioeconomic background influenced their later judgements of her academic ability when they observed performance information.

This supports the cognitive mechanism:

Prior expectation → Interpretation of information → Judgement

However, this supports the general cognitive mechanism rather than proving that schemas determine the outcome of real relationships.

A schema is a mental representation, so psychologists cannot simply see a schema operating inside someone's mind.

For example, if someone interprets an unanswered message as rejection, a researcher may infer that an existing rejection-related schema influenced the interpretation.

But other explanations may also be possible.

Researchers therefore often infer schemas from:

  • behavioural responses
  • questionnaires
  • memory
  • judgements
  • experimental tasks

This creates a measurement challenge.

Cognitive explanations can overemphasise internal mental processes if they ignore other influences.

Imagine a person believes:

“My partner is becoming distant.”

A purely cognitive explanation might focus on schemas and interpretation.

But the partner might actually be behaving differently.

Relationship behaviour may also be influenced by:

  • communication patterns
  • cultural expectations
  • stress
  • social circumstances
  • biological processes
  • previous interactions between the two people

Cognition is therefore one level of explanation rather than a complete explanation.

Studies such as Darley and Gross allow psychologists to investigate cognitive processes under controlled conditions.

This is valuable because variables can be manipulated carefully.

However, judging information about someone in an experiment is much simpler than a friendship or romantic relationship developing across months or years of repeated interaction.

Real relationships involve feedback between people, emotions, communication and shared experiences.

This can limit the ecological validity of some experimental evidence.

Noah thinks his friend is deliberately ignoring him after receiving no reply for six hours.

A cognitive psychologist might investigate whether Noah's existing schemas influenced this interpretation.

But a strong psychological explanation would also ask:

  • Did the friend usually reply quickly?
  • Was there an actual conflict beforehand?
  • Was the message ambiguous?
  • How did Noah communicate afterward?
  • What other situational factors were operating?

The cognitive explanation is useful, but it should not automatically replace other possible explanations.

Cognitive explanations are valuable because they identify mechanisms through which schemas, expectations and interpretations can influence interpersonal behaviour.

They are particularly useful for explaining why different people may interpret the same social information differently.

However:

  • Schemas are inferred rather than directly observed.
  • Some supporting research uses artificial tasks.
  • Cognition is only one influence on relationships.

The strongest conclusion is therefore:

Cognitive processes can contribute significantly to interpersonal relationships, but they interact with biological, social, cultural and situational influences.

Darley and Gross (1983) showed that prior expectations can influence judgements about another person. Why would it be too strong to conclude that this proves schemas determine how real relationships develop?
The study supports the cognitive mechanism that prior expectations can influence interpretation and judgement, but a controlled experimental judgement is much simpler than a real relationship. Relationships develop through repeated interactions and are also influenced by communication, culture, social circumstances, biological processes and the actual behaviour of both people. The evidence therefore supports a cognitive influence rather than cognitive determinism.

Cognitive Explanations of Conflict: Attributional Errors

How We Explain Behaviour Can Create Conflict

An attribution is an explanation we give for why someone behaved in a particular way. Imagine your friend arrives 30 minutes late. You could think, “The bus was probably delayed” — a situational explanation — or “They are inconsiderate” — an explanation based on the person's character.

These explanations matter because the attribution we make can influence how we respond. If we repeatedly explain another person's negative behaviour as evidence of their personality while explaining our own behaviour through circumstances, conflict can escalate.

Dispositional attribution = explaining behaviour through characteristics of the person.

Example: “He shouted because he's an aggressive person.”

Situational attribution = explaining behaviour through circumstances surrounding the person.

Example: “He shouted because he had just received extremely stressful news.”

Both types of explanation can sometimes be correct. The problem occurs when our judgement becomes systematically biased.

The fundamental attribution error is the tendency to overemphasise dispositional explanations for another person's behaviour while underestimating situational influences.

Example: A coworker gives a very short answer during a meeting.

You conclude:

“She's rude.”

But you do not know that she has just received an urgent family message.

You have focused on her disposition while overlooking a possible situational explanation.

In relationships, this can turn isolated behaviour into a judgement about someone's entire personality.

Self-serving bias involves explaining outcomes in ways that protect or enhance our view of ourselves — commonly taking more personal credit for positive outcomes while attributing negative outcomes more to external circumstances.

This can create an important asymmetry during conflict.

Example:

When I am rude:

“I was stressed. I've had a terrible day.”

When you are rude:

“You're selfish and disrespectful.”

Each person may see their own behaviour as understandable while interpreting the other person's behaviour as evidence of a character flaw.

Imagine two roommates leave dishes in the kitchen.

When Maya leaves dishes, she thinks:

“I had an exhausting day and didn't have time.”

When her roommate leaves dishes, Maya thinks:

“She's lazy.”

Her roommate may make exactly the same kind of judgement in reverse.

Both people therefore believe:

“My behaviour has a reasonable explanation. Your behaviour shows what kind of person you are.”

This attributional asymmetry can contribute to blame and resentment.

Do not confuse these concepts:

  • Hostile attribution bias → interpreting ambiguous behaviour as intentionally hostile.
  • Fundamental attribution error → overemphasising personality and underestimating situational explanations for another person's behaviour.
  • Self-serving bias → interpreting outcomes in ways that protect or enhance one's view of oneself.

They can sometimes occur in the same conflict, but they describe different cognitive processes.

Jamie forgets to reply to Taylor's message.

Taylor thinks:

“Jamie doesn't care about anyone except himself.”

The next week Taylor forgets to reply to Jamie and thinks:

“I've just been incredibly busy.”

Taylor explains Jamie's behaviour through personality but explains similar personal behaviour through circumstances.

If Taylor then becomes angry with Jamie, these attributional differences may contribute to interpersonal conflict.

Attributional explanations are useful because they show that conflict can depend partly on how people explain behaviour, not simply on the behaviour itself.

However, psychologists should not assume that every negative judgement is an attributional error.

Sometimes another person's behaviour genuinely does reflect their intentions or characteristics.

The important question is whether people are systematically overlooking relevant situational information.

Chris arrives late and says, 'Traffic was terrible.' The next day his friend arrives late and Chris thinks, 'She's so inconsiderate.' What cognitive problem is illustrated by the difference between these explanations?
Chris explains his own behaviour using a situational factor but interprets his friend's similar behaviour as evidence of her disposition. This illustrates an attributional asymmetry and is consistent with processes such as the fundamental attribution error and self-protective attribution. Such differences in explanation can contribute to blame and interpersonal conflict.

Cognitive Explanations of Conflict: Evaluation

How Useful Are Cognitive Explanations of Conflict?

Cognitive explanations are valuable because they show how a disagreement can escalate even when neither person originally intended a major conflict. For example, two friends may both want to resolve a disagreement, but if each interprets the other's short responses as hostile, both may become defensive and unintentionally make the conflict worse.

However, not every conflict is caused by biased thinking. Sometimes people face genuine incompatible goals, unfair behaviour, discrimination, betrayal or other real external problems.

A good psychological explanation must consider both cognition and the actual situation.

Cognitive explanations are particularly useful for explaining why relatively small events sometimes develop into larger conflicts.

The mechanism can be:

Ambiguous event → Negative interpretation → Defensive behaviour → Negative response → Escalation

Example: One roommate forgets to buy milk.

The other interprets this as:

“They never care about anyone else.”

The discussion shifts from one forgotten task to an attack on personality, increasing the likelihood of conflict.

Cognitive mechanisms therefore help explain the process of escalation.

The same behaviour can produce very different reactions depending on how it is interpreted.

Example: Two people receive:

“We need to talk.”

One thinks:

“Something terrible has happened.”

Another thinks:

“They probably just want to discuss our plans.”

Different schemas, expectations and previous experiences may contribute to these different interpretations.

Cognitive explanations can therefore help explain why different individuals react differently to similar interpersonal events.

Researchers cannot directly observe a hostile attribution, schema or expectation in the same way they can directly observe someone's behaviour.

They often infer cognitive processes through:

  • questionnaires
  • experimental responses
  • behavioural choices
  • verbal reports

This creates a measurement problem because the psychological mechanism itself is not directly observable.

Cognitive explanations can become problematic if they imply that conflict exists mainly because someone interpreted the situation incorrectly.

Example: An employee believes a colleague is deliberately excluding them from important meetings.

A cognitive explanation might investigate hostile attribution or confirmation bias.

But perhaps the colleague really is deliberately excluding them.

Psychologists therefore need evidence about both:

the person's cognition

and

the external situation.

Otherwise, genuine causes of conflict may be underestimated.

Interpersonal conflict can also be influenced by:

  • communication patterns
  • social norms
  • cultural expectations
  • power differences
  • stress
  • previous relationship history
  • biological processes

Cognitive mechanisms should therefore be viewed as one part of a larger explanation.

Two students disagree during a group project.

Student A thinks:

“Student B rejected my idea because she doesn't respect me.”

Student A becomes defensive.

Student B interprets the defensiveness as aggression and responds angrily.

A cognitive explanation can show how interpretation and attribution contribute to escalation.

But a complete explanation should also investigate:

  • What did Student B actually say?
  • Was there already tension between them?
  • Were their goals genuinely incompatible?
  • How did each person communicate?

The cognitive explanation is useful precisely when it is treated as an influence rather than the entire explanation.

Cognitive explanations of conflict are particularly strong at explaining:

  • how ambiguous behaviour is interpreted
  • why expectations influence responses
  • how conflict can become self-reinforcing
  • why people may blame each other differently

Their major limitation is that focusing too heavily on cognition may cause psychologists to underestimate real situational and interpersonal causes of conflict.

A balanced conclusion is:

Cognitive processes can contribute to the development and escalation of interpersonal conflict, but they operate alongside communication, social, cultural, biological and situational influences.

A psychologist explains an argument entirely by saying that one person has a hostile attribution bias. What important limitation should we consider before accepting this explanation?
The psychologist must consider whether the other person's behaviour was actually hostile and whether other situational factors contributed to the conflict. Cognitive biases can influence interpretation, but not every negative interpretation is incorrect. A strong explanation considers both the person's cognition and evidence about the external situation.

Communication & Language: Communication Is More Than Words

How can the same words communicate completely different messages?

Imagine your friend says “Fine.” while smiling warmly at you. Now imagine they send “Fine.” in a text message immediately after an argument. The word is identical, but its meaning may feel completely different because communication involves more than the literal words being used.

Communication in relationships involves both verbal information and non-verbal information, together with the context in which the message occurs and the way the receiver interprets it.

Verbal communication refers to the words and language people use.

For example, during an argument:

“I felt hurt when you didn't reply to my message.”

focuses on a specific behaviour and feeling.

Compare this with:

“You always ignore me.”

The second statement makes a much broader accusation and may be more likely to produce a defensive response.

Language therefore does more than transmit information. The way something is phrased can influence how another person interprets and responds to the message.

Non-verbal communication includes information communicated through:

  • facial expressions
  • tone of voice
  • eye contact
  • posture
  • gestures
  • physical distance

For example, someone saying “I'm happy to help” while smiling and speaking warmly may communicate something very different from someone saying exactly the same words while sighing, avoiding eye contact and rolling their eyes.

The words have not changed. The additional social information has.

Digital communication can remove many of these non-verbal cues.

Imagine receiving:

“Sure.”

It could mean:

“Yes, absolutely.”

or:

“Fine, if I have to.”

Without facial expression or tone of voice, the message may be more ambiguous. The receiver may therefore rely more heavily on the context of the conversation and their existing expectations about the other person.

This connects communication with cognitive explanations of relationships: communication provides the information, but cognitive processes help determine how that information is interpreted.

Communication also involves how people respond to one another.

Active listening involves paying attention to what another person is saying and checking that their meaning has been understood.

For example:

“So you felt ignored when I didn't reply?”

attempts to check the other person's interpretation.

Compare this with:

“That's ridiculous. I was busy.”

The first response does not necessarily mean agreeing with the other person. It means attempting to understand what they are communicating before responding.

Communication between two people can create a continuing cycle:

Message → Interpretation → Response → Other person's interpretation → New response

For example, Alex sends Maya a very short message because he is busy.

Maya interprets the short message as rude and responds angrily.

Alex interprets Maya's angry response as unreasonable and becomes defensive.

A message that originally had little emotional meaning can therefore contribute to conflict through the interaction between communication and interpretation.

Sam asks Jordan:

“Are you coming tonight?”

Jordan replies:

“I guess.”

If Jordan says this face-to-face while smiling, Sam may interpret it positively.

If Jordan sends the same words by text after the two have been arguing, Sam may interpret the message as annoyed or reluctant.

The behaviour cannot be understood from the words alone. Non-verbal cues, context and interpretation all matter.

Avoid claims such as “body language communicates most of what people mean.”

A more psychologically useful explanation is:

Verbal content + non-verbal cues + context + the receiver's interpretation can all contribute to how a message is understood.

Why might the message “Fine.” be interpreted differently in a text after an argument than when somebody says it while smiling face-to-face?
Face-to-face communication provides non-verbal information such as facial expression and tone of voice. A text message removes many of these cues, increasing ambiguity. The previous argument may also influence the receiver's expectations and therefore how the message is interpreted.
Think about a time when you misunderstood a text message or online comment. What information might have been available if the conversation had happened face-to-face?
Face-to-face communication could provide additional information such as tone of voice, facial expression, gestures and immediate feedback. These cues may help clarify the intended meaning. However, misunderstanding can still occur because people also interpret communication through their expectations and previous experiences.
Two friends begin arguing in a group chat because one interprets a short reply as disrespectful. What could they do to reduce the chance that the misunderstanding continues?
They could ask what the other person meant rather than immediately assuming hostile intent, provide more context, or move the conversation to a form of communication that provides more cues, such as speaking face-to-face or by voice. This could interrupt the feedback loop between interpretation and response.

Communication & Language: Gottman's Four Horsemen

Can recurring communication patterns help psychologists understand relationship conflict?

Imagine an argument between two partners.

One says:

“You're so selfish. You never think about anyone else.”

The other immediately makes excuses.

One rolls their eyes and mocks the other.

Eventually, one person stops responding completely.

Relationship researcher John Gottman studied patterns of interaction between couples and identified several recurring negative communication behaviours associated with relationship distress and breakdown. Four became known as the Four Horsemen:

Criticism, Contempt, Defensiveness and Stonewalling.

Criticism attacks the other person's character rather than focusing on a specific behaviour or problem.

For example:

“You didn't wash the dishes.”

identifies a behaviour.

But:

“You never help because you're lazy and selfish.”

turns the disagreement into an attack on the person.

This distinction matters because criticism can shift an argument from solving a problem to defending personal identity.

Contempt communicates disgust, mockery or superiority toward another person.

It can include:

  • sarcasm
  • ridicule
  • sneering
  • eye-rolling
  • insulting humour

For example:

“Wow, brilliant idea. You're such a genius.”

said sarcastically communicates superiority rather than genuine praise.

Contempt can therefore communicate that one partner sees the other as inferior rather than simply disagreeing with their behaviour.

Defensiveness occurs when someone responds to criticism by rejecting responsibility, making excuses or counterattacking.

For example:

“You forgot to call me again.”

“Well, maybe I would remember if you didn't complain about everything.”

Instead of addressing the original problem, the response redirects blame and may keep the conflict going.

Stonewalling involves withdrawing from meaningful interaction.

A person may stop responding, avoid eye contact, leave the conversation or emotionally disengage.

For example, during an argument one partner repeatedly says:

“Whatever.”

and then refuses to continue communicating.

Stonewalling should not automatically be confused with taking a constructive break from an argument. Temporarily stepping away and agreeing to return when both people are calmer may help communication. Stonewalling involves disengagement that prevents meaningful interaction.

The Four Horsemen can form an escalating communication cycle:

Criticism → Defensiveness → Contempt → Stonewalling → Greater frustration or hostility

For example, criticism may produce defensiveness. Defensiveness may frustrate the other partner, leading to contempt. The other person may then withdraw, which creates even greater frustration.

The important psychological idea is therefore not simply identifying four behaviours. It is understanding how patterns of communication can influence subsequent interaction.

Gottman's longitudinal research observed couples' interactions and examined whether communication patterns were associated with later relationship outcomes.

Recurring negative interaction patterns, including behaviours represented by the Four Horsemen, were strongly associated with relationship distress and could help predict later relationship outcomes within the samples studied.

However:

  • Prediction does not automatically demonstrate causation.
  • Relationship breakdown may involve many interacting influences, including stress, personality, cognition, previous experiences, cultural expectations and the behaviour of both partners.

Maya says to Alex:

“You're completely selfish.”

Alex responds:

“Well, you're impossible to please.”

Maya rolls her eyes and laughs sarcastically.

Alex eventually stops responding and looks at his phone.

The interaction contains:

Criticism → Defensiveness → Contempt → Stonewalling

Rather than treating each behaviour separately, psychologists can examine how one response may contribute to the next response in the interaction.

Do not write:

“Gottman proved that the Four Horsemen cause divorce.”

A more accurate conclusion is:

Negative communication patterns such as the Four Horsemen have been associated with relationship distress and can help predict relationship outcomes, but this does not demonstrate that they inevitably cause relationship breakdown.

Someone tells their partner, “You're a selfish person who never thinks about anyone else.” Which of the Four Horsemen is this most clearly demonstrating?
Criticism. The statement attacks the person's character rather than focusing on a specific behaviour that could be discussed or changed.
Think about disagreements you see between friends, family members, online communities or even public figures. Why might contempt make resolving a disagreement particularly difficult?
Contempt communicates mockery, disgust or superiority rather than simply disagreement. This may make the other person feel attacked or disrespected and can encourage defensiveness or withdrawal, making constructive communication more difficult.
Suppose researchers find that couples who frequently show contempt are more likely to separate several years later. Why can't we simply conclude that contempt caused their relationships to end?
The relationship shows prediction and association, but other variables may contribute to both contempt and relationship breakdown. Conflict, stress, personality, previous relationship problems or other factors could influence both. The relationship may also be bidirectional, with existing relationship problems increasing contempt.

Communication & Language: Evaluation

How useful are communication explanations for understanding relationships?

Communication explanations are useful because relationships involve continuous interaction between people. Rather than examining only what individuals think or feel internally, psychologists can examine observable patterns of behaviour between people.

However, communication cannot explain every aspect of a relationship by itself.

An important strength is that researchers can observe and code actual communication behaviour.

For example, researchers studying couples can record an interaction and identify behaviours such as criticism, contempt, defensiveness or withdrawal.

This provides behavioural evidence rather than relying entirely on participants remembering and reporting how they normally communicate.

Longitudinal research can examine whether communication patterns observed at one point are associated with later relationship outcomes.

For example, if contempt is observed during an interaction and researchers follow the couple over several years, they can investigate whether contempt predicts later relationship distress or breakdown.

This can make communication research particularly useful for understanding patterns and prediction.

Suppose contempt predicts later relationship breakdown.

This does not automatically mean:

Contempt → relationship breakdown

Several possibilities remain.

  • Existing relationship problems might increase contempt.
  • Contempt might contribute to later problems.
  • Both could influence each other.
  • A third variable, such as chronic stress, could influence both.

Therefore, longitudinal prediction can provide valuable evidence without necessarily establishing a simple causal relationship.

Communication behaviours can have different meanings depending on cultural and social context.

Cultures may differ in expectations concerning:

  • directness
  • emotional expression
  • eye contact
  • disagreement
  • politeness

For example, avoiding direct disagreement may be interpreted as poor communication in one cultural context but as respectful behaviour in another.

Researchers should therefore be cautious about assuming that one communication style represents healthy or unhealthy interaction in every culture.

People may behave differently when they know they are being observed.

A couple discussing a disagreement in a research setting may communicate differently from how they communicate during an argument at home.

This creates a possible reactivity problem and can reduce ecological validity.

Relationships are influenced by more than communication.

Communication may interact with:

  • cognitive interpretations
  • previous relationship experiences
  • personality
  • stress
  • cultural expectations
  • biological processes
  • the actual behaviour of the other person

For example, repeatedly telling someone to “communicate better” may not solve a conflict caused by genuine betrayal, discrimination, incompatible goals or serious external stress.

Two friends repeatedly argue through text messages.

One uses very short responses.

The other interprets them as rude and responds with:

“You always talk to me like I'm stupid.”

The first friend becomes defensive.

A communication explanation can identify missing non-verbal cues, criticism and defensiveness.

But a psychologist should also ask whether previous conflict, expectations, stress or cultural differences are influencing the interaction.

Communication is therefore one level of explanation rather than the complete explanation.

A strong evaluation should avoid arguing that communication explanations are either completely correct or completely useless.

A better conclusion is:

Communication provides an important observable level of explanation for relationship behaviour, but communication patterns interact with cognitive, sociocultural, biological and situational influences.

A longitudinal study finds that contempt strongly predicts later relationship breakdown. What can psychologists reasonably conclude?
Contempt may be a useful predictor of later relationship outcomes and may contribute to relationship difficulties. However, prediction alone does not establish that contempt directly caused the breakdown because reverse causation, bidirectional effects and third variables remain possible.
Imagine two people from different cultural backgrounds disagree about whether avoiding eye contact during an argument is respectful or rude. What does this tell us about evaluating communication explanations?
Communication behaviours do not necessarily have identical meanings across cultures. Researchers need to consider cultural context rather than assuming that one behaviour always indicates the same psychological state or relationship quality.
Someone says, “Every relationship problem could be solved if people just communicated better.” What is psychologically wrong with this claim?
It reduces a complex relationship to a single explanation. Communication can contribute to relationship quality and conflict, but relationships are also influenced by cognition, culture, stress, personality, biological processes, previous experiences, external circumstances and the actual behaviour of the people involved. Some conflicts involve genuine problems rather than simply poor communication.

Chemical Messengers: Oxytocin

Can a chemical messenger influence how we interact with other people?

Imagine a parent holding and comforting their baby. The interaction involves touch, facial expressions, previous experiences, emotions and learning. It also involves biological processes.

One chemical messenger psychologists have investigated in social relationships is oxytocin.

Oxytocin is a hormone and neuropeptide produced in the hypothalamus. It is released into the bloodstream through the posterior pituitary and also acts within the brain.

Oxytocin has been studied in behaviours involving:

  • bonding
  • affiliation
  • social recognition
  • trust
  • caregiving

It is particularly associated with childbirth and breastfeeding and has also been investigated in parent-infant bonding and other social interactions.

Oxytocin is sometimes called the “bonding hormone” or “love hormone.”

These labels are useful for remembering it, but they are psychologically oversimplified.

Oxytocin does not simply make people love or trust one another.

A better model is:

Oxytocin may influence social processing → the social context influences how this is expressed → behaviour

Kosfeld and colleagues investigated whether oxytocin could influence trust.

Participants received either intranasal oxytocin or a placebo before completing an economic trust game involving decisions about how much money to entrust to another person.

Participants who received oxytocin showed higher levels of trusting investment than the placebo group.

Because researchers experimentally manipulated whether participants received oxytocin or placebo, the study provides stronger evidence for a causal effect of oxytocin on behaviour within this particular experimental task.

However, giving money to an anonymous person in an economic game is not the same as trusting a friend, partner or family member in everyday life.

This creates an important question about ecological validity.

Research by Feldman and colleagues has also found associations between oxytocin and aspects of parental bonding and caregiving behaviour.

This provides evidence that oxytocin is related to naturally occurring social relationships.

However, much of this evidence is correlational.

If higher oxytocin levels are associated with more affectionate caregiving, this does not by itself tell us whether:

oxytocin influences caregiving, caregiving influences oxytocin, or both influence each other.

Oxytocin does not necessarily produce universally positive social behaviour.

Research such as De Dreu et al. (2010) suggests that oxytocin can sometimes strengthen in-group favouritism.

This creates an important connection with Social Identity Theory.

A biological process that increases affiliation with members of one's own group does not necessarily increase positive behaviour toward everybody.

The effects of oxytocin therefore appear to be context-dependent.

A parent holds, talks to and comforts their baby every evening.

Oxytocin may contribute to biological processes involved in bonding and affiliation.

But the relationship cannot be explained by oxytocin alone.

It is also influenced by:

  • repeated interaction
  • learning
  • cognitive interpretations
  • stress
  • social support
  • culture
  • individual differences

The biological explanation therefore provides one level of explanation for the relationship.

Avoid:

“Oxytocin causes trust and bonding.”

Prefer:

“Oxytocin may contribute to social processes such as affiliation and trust, but its effects are context-dependent and interact with other influences on behaviour.”

Why is calling oxytocin the “love hormone” potentially misleading?
The label suggests a simple deterministic relationship between oxytocin and positive social behaviour. Oxytocin may influence social processing and affiliation, but its effects depend on the individual and social context and do not always produce trust or positive behaviour.
Kosfeld et al. found that participants given oxytocin invested more money in a trust game. Why shouldn't we immediately conclude that oxytocin makes people more trusting in all real relationships?
The study demonstrated an effect within a particular controlled experimental task. Trusting someone with money in a laboratory game may differ from trust in friendships, families or romantic relationships. This creates an ecological-validity limitation and means the findings should not be generalized too broadly.
Think about a close relationship in everyday life. Why would explaining that relationship entirely through oxytocin be inadequate?
Real relationships develop through interactions among biological, cognitive and sociocultural influences. Chemical messengers may contribute to affiliation and bonding, but learning, shared experiences, expectations, communication, culture, stress and the behaviour of both people also matter.

Chemical Messengers: Serotonin

Could a neurotransmitter influence how someone responds during social conflict?

Imagine someone becomes irritated during an argument but manages to stop, think and control an aggressive response.

That behaviour involves cognition and learning, but biological processes involved in emotional and behavioural regulation may also contribute.

One chemical messenger studied in this area is serotonin.

Serotonin is a neurotransmitter involved in several psychological and biological processes, including:

  • mood regulation
  • emotional processing
  • behavioural regulation
  • aspects of social behaviour

Psychologists have investigated serotonergic functioning in relation to behaviours such as impulsivity and aggression, which can influence interpersonal relationships and conflict.

Some research has found associations between lower serotonergic functioning and greater levels of impulsive aggression.

This does not mean:

Low serotonin → aggression

Human aggression is much more complicated.

A better explanation is:

Serotonergic functioning + brain systems + cognition + learning + social context + immediate situation → behaviour

For example, two people experiencing the same provocation may respond differently because their behaviour is influenced by multiple interacting factors.

Serotonin has been investigated in processes including:

  • emotional regulation
  • impulsivity
  • responses to social threat
  • aggression
  • social decision-making

These processes may influence interpersonal relationships.

For example, difficulty regulating an angry response could contribute to an argument escalating.

But serotonin would be one contributing biological influence, not a complete explanation of the conflict.

Researchers cannot simply look at someone's behaviour and determine exactly how much serotonin is operating in their brain.

Serotonergic functioning may be investigated using:

  • pharmacological manipulation
  • neuroimaging
  • biological markers
  • behavioural tasks

Each method provides only part of the picture.

This creates an important measurement issue when evaluating biological explanations.

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) alter serotonergic signalling in the brain.

Their psychological effects provide evidence that manipulating serotonergic systems can influence behaviour and emotional processes.

However, it would be incorrect to reason:

SSRIs influence mood → therefore low serotonin alone causes negative mood or relationship problems.

Neurotransmitter systems are complex, and psychological behaviour cannot usually be reduced to the amount of a single chemical messenger.

Jordan becomes angry during an argument but pauses before responding.

Their behaviour could involve:

  • Biological influences: serotonergic functioning and brain systems involved in regulation.
  • Cognitive influences: how Jordan interprets the other person's behaviour.
  • Learning: strategies Jordan has learned for controlling anger.
  • Communication: how the other person expresses the disagreement.
  • Situation: stress, fatigue and what actually happened.

Understanding the behaviour therefore requires multiple levels of explanation.

Avoid:

“Low serotonin causes aggressive relationships.”

Prefer:

“Serotonergic functioning has been associated with processes such as emotional regulation and impulsive aggression and may contribute to interpersonal behaviour alongside other influences.”

Why is “low serotonin causes aggression” an inadequate psychological explanation?
Research may identify associations between serotonergic functioning and impulsive aggression, but aggression is influenced by many interacting biological, cognitive, sociocultural and situational factors. An association does not establish a simple deterministic causal pathway.
Two people are insulted in exactly the same way. One becomes aggressive while the other calmly walks away. Why does this make a single-neurotransmitter explanation difficult to defend?
The same situation can produce different behaviour because individuals differ in biological functioning, interpretation, previous learning, emotional regulation, social expectations and other factors. Serotonin may contribute to behavioural regulation, but it cannot explain the response by itself.
Think about arguments you have seen in real life, online or in films. What factors other than biology might determine whether an argument escalates into aggression?
Possible influences include how each person interprets the situation, previous conflict, learned responses, social norms, peer influence, stress, communication style and the behaviour of the other person. These influences can interact with biological processes rather than operating independently.

Chemical Messengers: Evaluation

How useful are chemical messengers for explaining interpersonal relationships?

Chemical messengers provide an important biological level of explanation for interpersonal behaviour.

Research on oxytocin and serotonin suggests that biological processes can contribute to social affiliation, trust, emotional regulation and responses during social conflict.

However, explaining relationships entirely through chemical messengers would be reductionist.

Some biological research uses experiments in which researchers manipulate a chemical messenger or the system in which it operates.

For example, Kosfeld et al. (2005) compared participants receiving intranasal oxytocin with participants receiving a placebo before a trust game.

Experimental manipulation provides stronger evidence for causality than simply measuring naturally occurring associations.

However, the causal conclusion remains specific to the conditions and behaviour studied.

Laboratory tasks can simplify complex social relationships.

For example, deciding how much money to invest in an economic trust game is measurable and controlled.

But real interpersonal trust may involve:

  • years of shared experience
  • emotional attachment
  • previous betrayal
  • communication
  • cultural expectations
  • power differences

Therefore, laboratory findings may not perfectly represent real relationships.

Chemical messengers are difficult to measure directly in the living human brain.

Researchers often rely on indirect measures, pharmacological manipulation, biological markers, neuroimaging or behavioural responses.

Different methods may capture different aspects of the biological system.

This creates an important measurement problem.

A reductionist explanation attempts to explain complex behaviour using a smaller or simpler set of processes.

For example:

“They trust each other because of oxytocin.”

reduces a complex relationship to one biological process.

Trust may instead involve:

biology + cognition + learning + communication + culture + shared experience + situation

Similarly:

“He became aggressive because his serotonin was low.”

ignores the person's interpretation of the situation, previous learning, social environment and what the other person actually did.

The same chemical messenger does not necessarily produce the same behaviour in every situation.

Oxytocin, for example, has been associated with affiliation and trust but can also be related to in-group favouritism under some conditions.

The psychological effect of a biological process therefore depends partly on context.

Some biological evidence is correlational.

For example:

Higher oxytocin is associated with more affectionate caregiving.

This does not establish:

Higher oxytocin causes affectionate caregiving.

Possible explanations include:

Oxytocin → caregiving

Caregiving → oxytocin

Oxytocin ↔ caregiving

or another factor influencing both.

Students should therefore always examine the research method before making causal conclusions.

Imagine two close friends who trust one another deeply.

A biological explanation might investigate oxytocin and processes involved in affiliation.

A cognitive explanation might examine expectations and schemas.

A sociocultural explanation might examine shared group membership and cultural expectations.

A communication explanation might examine how the friends respond to and support each other.

These explanations do not necessarily compete.

They may describe different interacting levels of the same relationship.

Chemical messengers are valuable because they provide evidence that biological processes contribute to interpersonal behaviour.

But a strong IB answer should also consider:

causality, measurement, ecological validity, context and reductionism.

The strongest conclusion is:

Chemical messengers such as oxytocin and serotonin may contribute to interpersonal behaviour, but their effects are context-dependent and interact with cognitive, sociocultural and situational influences.

Why does an experiment such as Kosfeld et al. provide stronger causal evidence than finding a correlation between naturally occurring oxytocin and trust?
Researchers manipulated whether participants received oxytocin or placebo, allowing greater control over the proposed causal variable. A correlation only shows that two variables are associated and cannot establish which variable caused the other or whether a third variable influenced both.
A headline says: “Scientists discover the chemical that makes people fall in love.” What questions should a psychology student immediately ask?
Students should ask how the chemical was measured or manipulated, what behaviour was actually measured, whether the study was experimental or correlational, whether the sample and task represent real relationships, and whether alternative biological, cognitive and sociocultural explanations were considered.
If biology, cognition and culture can all help explain the same relationship, does that mean one of those explanations must be wrong?
No. Different approaches can explain behaviour at different levels. Biological processes may interact with cognitive interpretations, learning, culture and social situations. A more complete psychological explanation may therefore integrate several approaches rather than treating them as mutually exclusive.

Strategies for Improving Relationships: Allport's Contact Hypothesis

Can bringing different groups together actually reduce prejudice and improve relationships?

Imagine two groups of students who rarely interact and hold negative stereotypes about each other.

Simply putting them in the same room may not improve their relationship. If they compete, argue or feel unequal, contact could even reinforce existing prejudice.

Psychologist Gordon Allport (1954) proposed the Contact Hypothesis: contact between members of different groups can reduce prejudice, but it is particularly effective when important conditions are present.

Allport proposed four important conditions.

1. Equal status. The groups should interact on relatively equal terms.

Example: Students from two schools work together as equal members of the same project rather than one group being placed in charge of the other.

2. Common goals. The groups should share an outcome that both want to achieve.

Example: Two groups of students who normally compete must design one presentation together and receive a shared result.

3. Intergroup cooperation. The groups should cooperate rather than compete against each other.

Example: Each group has different information needed to solve the same problem, so success requires them to work together.

4. Support from authorities, laws or social norms. People in positions of authority should clearly support positive intergroup contact.

Example: Teachers establish expectations of respect and cooperation and actively challenge discriminatory behaviour.

Positive contact can provide information that challenges existing stereotypes.

A student might initially believe:

“People from that group are unfriendly.”

After repeatedly cooperating with members of that group, the student encounters behaviour that does not fit the stereotype.

This may encourage them to reconsider their existing beliefs.

Positive interaction can also reduce anxiety about interacting with members of another group and create opportunities for friendship and cooperation.

A simplified mechanism is:

Positive intergroup contact → stereotype-challenging experiences + reduced intergroup anxiety → more positive attitudes → improved intergroup relationships

This is not automatic. The quality and context of the contact matter.

Pettigrew and Tropp (2006) conducted a large meta-analysis of research examining intergroup contact and prejudice.

Across hundreds of studies, greater intergroup contact was generally associated with lower levels of prejudice.

The findings provide broad support for the Contact Hypothesis across different groups and settings.

Importantly, Allport's favourable conditions generally strengthen the effects of contact, although research suggests that beneficial contact can sometimes occur even when every condition is not perfectly satisfied.

Because much of the evidence included in a meta-analysis comes from different research designs, however, researchers still need to consider causality.

For example, people who already hold less prejudiced attitudes may be more willing to seek contact with other groups.

A school notices tension between local students and newly arrived international students.

Instead of simply telling the groups to “spend more time together,” the school creates mixed teams that:

  • have equal roles
  • work toward a shared goal
  • depend on one another to succeed
  • receive clear support from teachers

The Contact Hypothesis predicts that this type of structured interaction is more likely to improve intergroup relationships than contact alone.

Do not reduce the Contact Hypothesis to:

“More contact reduces prejudice.”

A stronger explanation is:

Positive intergroup contact can contribute to reduced prejudice, particularly when contact involves favourable conditions such as equal status, common goals, cooperation and institutional support.

Why might simply putting two prejudiced groups in the same room fail to improve their relationship?
Contact alone does not guarantee positive interaction. Competition, unequal status, hostility or negative experiences could reinforce existing stereotypes. Allport argued that conditions such as equal status, common goals, cooperation and institutional support make positive outcomes more likely.
Think about your school, workplace or community. What is one situation in which Allport's conditions could realistically be used to improve relationships between groups?
One possibility would be creating mixed teams in which members of different groups have equal roles and must cooperate toward a meaningful shared goal. The specific example can vary, but the important point is that the contact should involve more than simply placing different groups near each other.
A school introduces mixed-group projects and prejudice decreases afterward. Why should we still be cautious about saying the projects definitely caused the change?
Other influences may have changed at the same time, such as school policies, friendships, wider social attitudes or previous levels of prejudice. Strong causal conclusions depend on the research design and the extent to which alternative explanations can be ruled out.

Strategies for Improving Relationships: Cognitive Approaches

If interpretations can contribute to relationship conflict, can changing those interpretations improve relationships?

Imagine your friend walks past you without saying hello.

Your immediate thought is:

“They're deliberately ignoring me.”

You become annoyed and avoid them later.

But then you discover they had just received upsetting news and genuinely had not noticed you.

The event did not change.

Your interpretation of the event changed.

Cognitive approaches to improving relationships focus on identifying and modifying patterns of thinking that may contribute to misunderstanding or conflict.

People often interpret social behaviour quickly.

For example:

Event: A friend does not reply for several hours.

Automatic interpretation: “They're ignoring me because they don't care.”

Possible response: Anger or withdrawal.

A cognitive strategy involves stopping before reacting and asking:

“What evidence supports my interpretation, and what other explanations are possible?”

Alternative explanations might include:

  • they are working
  • their phone is unavailable
  • they forgot
  • they are stressed
  • they have not seen the message

The goal is not to assume everything is harmless.

The goal is to avoid treating the first interpretation as automatically correct.

Cognitive strategies can also encourage people to consider situational explanations for another person's behaviour.

For example:

“My partner is late because they don't respect me.”

could be reconsidered alongside:

“Could traffic, work or another situational factor explain why they are late?”

This can help reduce the tendency to overemphasize dispositional explanations when situational information is available.

However, sometimes someone's behaviour really is inconsiderate. Cognitive approaches should improve the accuracy and flexibility of interpretation, not encourage people to excuse genuinely harmful behaviour.

Recall the self-fulfilling prophecy:

Expectation → Behaviour → Other person's response → Expected outcome becomes more likely

Imagine Noah believes:

“My friends don't really want me around.”

Because of this expectation, he stops initiating conversations and gives short answers.

His friends eventually contact him less often.

Noah interprets this as evidence that his original belief was correct.

A cognitive strategy could interrupt the cycle by helping Noah recognize the expectation before it shapes his behaviour.

Perspective-taking involves deliberately considering a situation from another person's point of view.

During a disagreement, someone might ask:

“How might this situation look from their perspective?”

For example, Maya believes her roommate deliberately ignored a message.

Before confronting her angrily, Maya considers that her roommate may have been working, sleeping or dealing with another problem.

Perspective-taking does not require Maya to agree with the roommate.

It gives her additional information before deciding how to respond.

Changing an interpretation may change communication.

Instead of:

“You ignored me because you don't care.”

someone might say:

“I wasn't sure why you didn't reply. What happened?”

The second response allows the other person to provide information before a conclusion is reached.

A possible improvement cycle is therefore:

Ambiguous behaviour → Consider alternative interpretations → Ask for information → Receive additional evidence → Respond more accurately

Jordan sends Alex:

“We need to talk tomorrow.”

Alex immediately thinks:

“Jordan is angry with me.”

Alex could react defensively before the conversation even begins.

A cognitive approach would encourage Alex to identify this as an interpretation rather than a known fact, consider alternative explanations and gather more information before responding.

This could reduce unnecessary conflict.

Cognitive approaches do not assume:

“Relationship problems are all in your head.”

Instead:

Cognitive processes can contribute to relationship behaviour because interpretations and expectations influence how people respond to one another. Changing inaccurate or inflexible interpretations may therefore help improve some interactions.

Your friend cancels plans and you immediately think, “They obviously don't value our friendship.” What cognitive strategy could you use before responding?
Separate the observable event from your interpretation. The cancellation is known; the reason is not. Consider alternative explanations and gather additional information before concluding that the behaviour reflects how your friend feels about the relationship.
Think about a disagreement from everyday life. How could asking “What else could explain this behaviour?” change what happens next?
Considering alternative explanations may reduce an immediate hostile or dispositional interpretation. This can change the person's emotional and behavioural response and may prevent a misunderstanding from escalating. It does not mean ignoring evidence of genuinely harmful behaviour.
Why could changing someone's interpretation sometimes improve their communication as well?
Interpretations influence responses. If someone interprets ambiguous behaviour as deliberately hostile, they may communicate defensively or aggressively. A more flexible interpretation may encourage them to ask questions, seek clarification or respond less defensively, changing the subsequent interaction.

Strategies for Improving Relationships: Evaluation

Can psychological research really tell us how to improve relationships?

Strategies such as structured intergroup contact and cognitive approaches show how psychological theories can be applied to real social problems.

This is an important strength of relationship research.

However, whether a strategy works depends on the people, problem and context involved.

A major strength is practical application.

The Contact Hypothesis can inform:

  • schools
  • workplaces
  • community programmes
  • interventions aimed at reducing prejudice

Cognitive approaches can help people recognize interpretations and expectations that may contribute to interpersonal conflict.

Psychological explanations therefore do more than describe relationships. They can generate strategies that attempt to change behaviour.

Effective interventions should be linked to a proposed mechanism.

Contact is not simply:

“Put different groups together.”

It attempts to create experiences that may challenge stereotypes, reduce anxiety and encourage cooperation.

Similarly, cognitive approaches do not simply tell someone:

“Think positively.”

They attempt to identify interpretations, expectations or attributional patterns that may influence subsequent behaviour.

Connecting an intervention to a mechanism allows psychologists to explain why the strategy might work.

Different relationship problems may have different causes.

Consider three situations:

Situation A: Two groups avoid each other because of stereotypes.

Structured intergroup contact may help.

Situation B: Two friends repeatedly misinterpret ambiguous messages.

A cognitive or communication strategy may help.

Situation C: Someone is being deliberately bullied or abused.

Simply changing the victim's interpretation would be inappropriate because the problem is not necessarily an inaccurate cognitive interpretation.

Psychologists therefore need to identify the problem before choosing an intervention.

The success of an intervention can depend on:

  • culture
  • power relationships
  • social norms
  • previous experiences
  • motivation
  • institutional support
  • the behaviour of other people

For example, encouraging cooperation between groups may be less successful if the wider environment continues to reward discrimination or hostility.

Strategies cannot always be separated from the social context in which they operate.

Researchers also need to decide what “improved relationships” actually means.

Possible measures include:

  • reduced prejudice
  • increased cooperation
  • fewer conflicts
  • greater relationship satisfaction
  • changes in observed behaviour

Different measures may produce different conclusions.

Someone might report more positive attitudes on a questionnaire while their actual behaviour changes very little.

This makes measurement an important issue when evaluating relationship interventions.

Relationship interventions can also raise ethical questions.

For example:

Should people be encouraged to maintain a relationship that is genuinely harmful?

Could an intervention place responsibility on one person when the other person's behaviour is the real problem?

Could forced intergroup contact create additional conflict?

Strategies should therefore aim to improve relationships without ignoring power, genuine harm or individual autonomy.

A school has conflict between two groups of students.

One teacher suggests:

“Just teach them to think more positively about each other.”

Another says:

“Just put them together more often.”

A psychologist would first ask:

What psychological and social processes are maintaining the conflict?

If stereotypes and lack of contact are central, structured contact may help.

If hostile interpretations are maintaining misunderstandings, cognitive strategies may help.

If there is genuine discrimination or unequal power, broader changes may also be necessary.

The intervention should match the mechanism maintaining the problem.

When evaluating a strategy for improving relationships, ask:

  • What mechanism is being targeted?
  • What evidence suggests it works?
  • How is improvement being measured?
  • In what contexts might it work differently?
  • Are there ethical or power-related concerns?

A strong conclusion is:

Psychological strategies can improve some relationships when they target relevant mechanisms, but their effectiveness depends on the nature of the problem, the evidence supporting the intervention and the social context in which it is used.

Why is “just think more positively” not an adequate description of a cognitive strategy for improving relationships?
Cognitive approaches involve identifying specific interpretations, expectations or attributional patterns and examining whether they accurately represent the available evidence. The aim is more flexible and accurate interpretation, not simply replacing negative thoughts with positive ones.
A school forces two hostile groups to spend lunch together every day, but their relationship becomes worse. Does this automatically disprove the Contact Hypothesis?
No. Researchers would need to examine the conditions of the contact. If the groups remained unequal, competed, experienced hostility or lacked institutional support, the intervention may not reflect the favourable conditions proposed by the Contact Hypothesis.
Someone is genuinely being treated badly in a relationship. Why could telling them to change their interpretation be psychologically and ethically problematic?
The negative interpretation may accurately reflect real behaviour. Treating every relationship problem as a cognitive distortion could ignore genuine harm and incorrectly place responsibility on the person experiencing it. Psychologists need to distinguish inaccurate interpretations from real external problems.

Interpersonal Relationships Application Challenge: Scenario 1: The Misunderstanding

How can a tiny interaction turn into a much bigger relationship problem?

Maya and Sofia have been close friends for several years.

One afternoon Maya sends Sofia a message:

“Are we still meeting after school?”

Several hours pass without a reply.

Maya notices that Sofia has been active online and thinks:

“She's deliberately ignoring me.”

Maya becomes annoyed and sends:

“Fine. Forget it.”

Sofia eventually replies:

“What is your problem? I was helping my sister.”

Maya interprets this response as aggressive.

She replies:

“You always have an excuse.”

Sofia becomes defensive and stops replying.

By the next day, both believe the other person caused the argument.

There is no single explanation for the conflict.

Several psychological processes may be interacting.

Maya observes a fact:

Sofia has not replied.

But she adds an interpretation:

“She's deliberately ignoring me.”

Maya does not yet know Sofia's reason.

This illustrates how schemas, expectations and attributional processes can influence the interpretation of ambiguous social behaviour.

If Maya already expects people to ignore her, that expectation may make the hostile interpretation particularly accessible.

Digital communication removes many non-verbal cues.

When Maya sends:

“Fine. Forget it.”

Sofia cannot hear her tone of voice or see her facial expression.

Similarly:

“What is your problem?”

may be interpreted differently depending on the relationship history and context.

The interaction can develop into a feedback loop:

Message → Interpretation → Response → New interpretation → New response

Suppose Maya expects:

“Sofia doesn't really care about our friendship.”

That expectation contributes to an angry message.

Sofia becomes defensive and withdraws.

Maya then sees Sofia's withdrawal as evidence that her original belief was correct.

The expectation has influenced Maya's behaviour in a way that contributed to the outcome she expected.

A cognitive strategy could interrupt the process before Maya responds.

Instead of assuming:

“She's deliberately ignoring me.”

Maya could distinguish between:

What I know: Sofia has not replied.

and:

What I am assuming: Sofia is deliberately ignoring me.

Maya could then seek more information.

For example:

“Hey, just checking whether you saw my message. Are we still meeting?”

This does not guarantee that conflict will disappear, but it reduces the chance that an unsupported interpretation immediately drives the next behaviour.

Situations like this occur frequently in friendships, families and online communication.

People often have to interpret behaviour without knowing the other person's intentions.

Psychology helps explain why the interpretation between an event and the response can sometimes be as important as the original event.

A strong psychological explanation does not simply say:

“They had poor communication.”

It identifies the mechanism:

Ambiguous behaviour → interpretation → communication response → other person's interpretation → escalating interaction

What is the difference between what Maya knows and what Maya assumes when Sofia does not reply?
Maya knows that Sofia has not replied. She assumes that Sofia is deliberately ignoring her. The first is an observable event; the second is an interpretation of Sofia's intention.
Where could Maya interrupt the self-fulfilling prophecy before the conflict escalates?
She could interrupt it between her initial interpretation and her behavioural response. Instead of immediately sending an angry message, she could consider alternative explanations or ask Sofia what happened.
Think about text messages and social media. Why might these environments make misunderstandings like Maya and Sofia's particularly easy to create?
Digital communication often removes non-verbal cues such as tone of voice, facial expression and immediate feedback. Ambiguous messages therefore require more interpretation, which can allow expectations, schemas and previous experiences to influence the meaning assigned to them.

Interpersonal Relationships Application Challenge: Scenario 2: The Couple

Can the same relationship conflict be explained at several different levels?

Alex and Jordan have been together for three years.

Recently both have been under considerable pressure at work.

One evening Alex says:

“You never listen to me anymore.”

Jordan replies:

“That's ridiculous. You're the one who complains about everything.”

Alex rolls their eyes and says sarcastically:

“Right, because you're obviously perfect.”

Jordan stops responding and looks at their phone.

Alex becomes even angrier:

“See? You don't care.”

Several of Gottman's Four Horsemen appear in the interaction.

Criticism: “You never listen to me anymore.”

Defensiveness: “You're the one who complains about everything.”

Contempt: “Right, because you're obviously perfect.”

Stonewalling: Jordan stops responding and disengages.

These behaviours can form an escalating interaction pattern in which each response influences what happens next.

Alex interprets Jordan's withdrawal as:

“You don't care.”

But withdrawal could have several possible explanations.

  • Jordan may be angry.
  • Jordan may feel overwhelmed.
  • Jordan may be trying to avoid saying something hurtful.
  • Jordan may genuinely be disengaging from the relationship.

Psychology should not automatically assume which interpretation is correct.

The important cognitive question is:

How does Alex's interpretation influence Alex's next behaviour?

Both people have been experiencing considerable stress.

Biological processes involved in emotional and behavioural regulation may influence how easily each person controls an angry or impulsive response.

Chemical messengers such as serotonin may contribute to regulatory processes.

However, it would be reductionist to conclude:

“They are arguing because of serotonin.”

Biological processes interact with cognition, communication and the situation.

The conflict is occurring while both people are under significant work pressure.

This matters.

A psychologist should therefore consider:

individual processes + relationship processes + wider situation

rather than assuming the argument reveals a permanent characteristic of either person.

Different strategies could target different mechanisms.

A communication strategy could encourage Alex to describe a specific behaviour rather than attack Jordan's character.

A cognitive strategy could encourage both people to check interpretations before assuming intentions.

A temporary constructive break could allow both people to regulate their emotions before returning to the discussion.

The best strategy depends on what is actually maintaining the conflict.

Real relationship conflicts rarely fit neatly into a single psychological approach.

The same argument can involve:

  • communication patterns
  • cognitive interpretations
  • biological regulation
  • stress
  • previous experiences
  • the actual behaviour of both people

This is why psychological explanations can be complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

Avoid searching for one cause of a complex interpersonal interaction.

A stronger explanation asks:

What biological, cognitive, sociocultural and situational processes may be interacting to influence this behaviour?

Identify one example each of criticism, defensiveness, contempt and stonewalling in Alex and Jordan's argument.
Criticism: “You never listen to me anymore.” Defensiveness: “You're the one who complains about everything.” Contempt: the sarcastic statement “Right, because you're obviously perfect.” Stonewalling: Jordan stops responding and looks at their phone.
Alex interprets Jordan's withdrawal as proof that Jordan does not care. What should a psychologist ask before accepting that explanation?
A psychologist should consider alternative explanations for the withdrawal and gather more information about the context. Jordan could be angry, overwhelmed, attempting to regulate emotion or genuinely disengaging. The observable behaviour does not by itself reveal the person's intention.
If you were trying to help Alex and Jordan, why might combining psychological approaches be more useful than choosing only one?
Different approaches identify different processes. Communication strategies could address their interaction pattern, cognitive strategies could address interpretations, and understanding stress and emotional regulation could address situational and biological influences. These processes may interact within the same conflict.

Interpersonal Relationships Application Challenge: Scenario 3: Workplace Tension

How can individual thinking and group processes combine to create conflict?

A company combines two departments into one project team.

The employees quickly begin referring to themselves as:

“the design team”

and:

“the marketing team.”

Members of each original department mostly sit together and support their own group's ideas.

Priya, from marketing, suggests a change to the project.

Daniel, from design, responds:

“That won't work.”

Priya thinks:

“Design never respects our ideas.”

Later, Daniel makes another suggestion.

Priya dismisses it immediately without asking for more information.

Daniel tells his colleagues:

“See? Marketing is impossible to work with.”

Tension between the two groups increases.

The employees have categorized themselves according to their original departments.

Design = in-group

Marketing = out-group

Group identification can influence how members interpret and respond to each other.

The important point is not that group membership automatically causes hostility.

Instead, categorisation can create conditions in which in-group favouritism and intergroup comparison become more likely.

Priya interprets Daniel's comment:

“That won't work.”

through an existing expectation:

“Design never respects our ideas.”

She may then pay greater attention to behaviour that confirms this expectation while overlooking examples of cooperation.

This resembles confirmation bias.

Her interpretation also influences her later behaviour when she dismisses Daniel's suggestion.

The situation can become cyclical:

Group categorisation → expectations about the out-group → interpretation of behaviour → hostile or defensive response → out-group responds → original expectations appear confirmed

Daniel then concludes:

“Marketing is impossible to work with.”

Both sides can therefore accumulate experiences that appear to confirm their negative expectations.

Allport's Contact Hypothesis suggests that simply placing the two departments together may not be enough.

The company could create conditions involving:

  • equal status between members
  • shared goals
  • cooperation
  • clear organizational support

For example, instead of rewarding the departments separately, the company could create mixed teams in which members depend on one another to achieve a shared outcome.

This could provide experiences that challenge existing group stereotypes.

Employees could also be encouraged to separate behaviour from interpretation.

Daniel said:

“That won't work.”

That statement may be dismissive.

But Priya's broader conclusion:

“Design never respects our ideas.”

goes beyond the single observable event.

Asking for clarification could provide more information before the event is generalized to the entire out-group.

Similar processes can occur between:

  • departments in a workplace
  • groups of students
  • sports teams
  • political groups
  • cultural groups
  • online communities

Psychology can therefore connect group behaviour with interpersonal relationships.

Individual interactions can be influenced by group identities, while individual interactions can also strengthen beliefs about groups.

This scenario demonstrates an important principle:

Different levels of explanation can interact.

Group identity can influence cognition → cognition can influence interpersonal behaviour → interpersonal behaviour can reinforce group identity.

Strong psychological explanations examine these interactions rather than assuming behaviour has only one cause.

How does Social Identity Theory help explain why a disagreement between Priya and Daniel could become a conflict between “marketing” and “design”?
Both employees identify themselves and each other through group membership. This categorisation can influence expectations and interpretations, so one person's behaviour may be treated as evidence about the entire out-group rather than simply the behaviour of one individual.
Priya already believes that “design never respects our ideas.” How could confirmation bias strengthen that belief over time?
Priya may pay greater attention to dismissive behaviour from design employees while overlooking cooperative or respectful interactions. This selective processing can make the original belief appear increasingly supported.
Think about a real school, workplace, sports or online rivalry. What could be changed so that members begin thinking more in terms of “we” rather than two competing groups?
Creating meaningful shared goals, mixed cooperative teams, equal status and a sense of common identity may reduce the importance of the original group boundary. The specific strategy depends on the context, and simply forcing contact does not guarantee improvement.

IB Concepts in Human Relationships: Perspective

How can two psychologists explain exactly the same relationship behaviour differently?

Imagine someone becomes extremely angry after receiving a short message from their partner.

A psychologist using a cognitive perspective might investigate how the message was interpreted.

A psychologist focusing on biological influences might investigate processes involved in emotional regulation.

A psychologist using a sociocultural perspective might examine social norms, group membership or cultural expectations.

They are observing the same behaviour but asking different questions.

This is the IB concept of perspective.

Perspective refers to the way psychological behaviour can be understood from different viewpoints or levels of explanation.

Throughout Human Relationships, you have already encountered several perspectives.

A cognitive explanation focuses on processes such as:

  • schemas
  • expectations
  • interpretations
  • attribution
  • cognitive biases

Example: Alex receives the message:

“We need to talk.”

Alex immediately interprets it as evidence that the relationship is in trouble.

A cognitive explanation asks how Alex's existing expectations influenced that interpretation and subsequent behaviour.

A biological explanation examines processes involving the brain, nervous system, hormones or neurotransmitters.

Example: During an intense argument, biological processes involved in emotional and behavioural regulation may contribute to whether someone responds impulsively or controls their response.

This does not mean biology determines the behaviour.

A sociocultural explanation examines influences such as:

  • group membership
  • cultural norms
  • social expectations
  • modelling
  • social identity

Example: A teenager begins using the same expressions and clothing style as a new friendship group.

Social Identity Theory might focus on identification with the group, while Social Learning Theory might examine modelling and reinforcement.

Different perspectives do not necessarily compete.

Imagine Jordan becomes defensive during an argument.

A cognitive explanation could examine Jordan's interpretation of criticism.

A biological explanation could examine emotional regulation.

A sociocultural explanation could examine learned expectations about conflict.

A communication explanation could examine how the criticism was expressed.

These explanations may describe different interacting parts of the same behaviour.

Perspective is not only about psychological theories.

People within relationships can also interpret the same event differently.

Imagine two coworkers leave the same meeting.

One thinks:

“That discussion was direct and productive.”

The other thinks:

“That discussion was aggressive and disrespectful.”

Differences in expectations, culture, previous experiences and roles can contribute to these different interpretations.

A student joins a new friendship group and begins changing the way they speak.

There are several possible perspectives:

Social Identity Theory: identifying with the group may make group membership psychologically important.

Social Learning Theory: the student may observe and imitate behaviour that receives social approval.

Cognitive processes: expectations about fitting in may influence decisions.

Cultural context: norms influence which behaviours communicate group membership.

A strong psychological explanation can consider how these perspectives complement one another.

Do not automatically ask:

“Which perspective is correct?”

A stronger psychological question is:

“What does each perspective explain, what evidence supports it, and how might the different perspectives interact?”

A student copies the slang used by a new friendship group. How could Social Identity Theory and Social Learning Theory provide different explanations of the same behaviour?
Social Identity Theory could explain the behaviour through identification with the group and the psychological importance of group membership. Social Learning Theory could explain how the student observes, imitates and is reinforced for using the group's language. The explanations focus on different processes and can both contribute.
Think of a disagreement where two people genuinely seemed to experience the same event differently. What psychological factors might have produced those different perspectives?
Possible influences include different schemas, expectations, previous experiences, cultural norms, group identities, emotional states and information available to each person. Different perspectives do not necessarily mean that one person is deliberately being dishonest.
If biological and cognitive explanations both help explain an argument, does one of them have to be wrong?
No. Biological processes may influence emotional regulation while cognitive processes influence how the situation is interpreted. Different levels of explanation can interact within the same behaviour.

IB Concepts in Human Relationships: Evidence

How do psychologists know whether an explanation of relationships is actually supported?

Psychological claims should be based on evidence, not simply on whether an explanation sounds believable.

Throughout Human Relationships, you have encountered very different kinds of evidence.

Understanding those differences is essential when deciding what conclusions psychologists can reasonably make.

Experiments manipulate an independent variable while attempting to control alternative explanations.

Example: Tajfel et al. (1971)

Participants were assigned to minimal groups and made allocation decisions involving in-group and out-group members.

The controlled design helped researchers investigate whether social categorisation could be sufficient to produce in-group favouritism under minimal-group conditions.

The strength is control.

The limitation is that minimal-group tasks are highly artificial compared with many real social groups.

Kosfeld et al. (2005) experimentally manipulated whether participants received intranasal oxytocin or placebo before an economic trust task.

Because the proposed biological influence was manipulated, the design provides stronger evidence for a causal effect within that experimental context.

But students should still ask:

“Does trusting someone with money in a laboratory game represent trust in a real relationship?”

This is an ecological-validity question.

Relationship researchers can observe and code interaction.

For example, researchers studying communication may record couples and identify recurring patterns such as criticism, defensiveness, contempt and withdrawal.

Observation can provide evidence about actual behaviour rather than relying entirely on memory or self-report.

However, participants may alter their behaviour because they know they are being observed.

This is reactivity.

Some relationship research identifies associations between naturally occurring variables.

Example:

Greater intergroup contact is associated with lower prejudice.

This is useful evidence.

But correlation alone cannot establish:

Intergroup contact caused the reduction in prejudice.

People with lower prejudice may be more willing to seek intergroup contact, or other variables may influence both.

A meta-analysis statistically combines findings from multiple studies addressing a related research question.

Pettigrew and Tropp (2006) analysed evidence from hundreds of studies of intergroup contact and prejudice.

Finding a pattern across many studies can provide stronger evidence that an effect is not limited to one particular sample.

However, the quality of a meta-analysis still depends partly on the quality and comparability of the studies included.

Questionnaires and interviews can provide access to people's:

  • attitudes
  • beliefs
  • experiences
  • perceptions of relationships

But self-report data can be affected by:

  • social desirability
  • inaccurate memory
  • misunderstanding questions
  • response biases

Someone may also report being accepting of another group while behaving differently in real situations.

Compare these claims:

“Oxytocin causes people to trust.”

“Oxytocin increased trusting investment in a controlled experimental task.”

The second conclusion is more precise because it matches the evidence.

Strong psychology does not make the largest possible claim.

It makes the strongest claim justified by the research design and results.

Imagine a headline says:

“Scientists prove that social media destroys relationships.”

An IB Psychology student should immediately ask:

  • What was actually measured?
  • What research method was used?
  • Was social-media use manipulated or simply measured?
  • How was relationship quality measured?
  • Who participated?
  • Could another variable explain the association?

The quality of a psychological conclusion depends on the evidence behind it.

Always connect the claim to the evidence.

Ask:

What was measured? → How was it measured? → What research design was used? → What conclusion does that design actually justify?

A study finds that people reporting more intergroup contact also report less prejudice. Can researchers conclude that contact caused the lower prejudice?
Not from the correlation alone. Contact could reduce prejudice, people with lower prejudice could seek more contact, or another variable could influence both. The research design determines whether a causal conclusion is justified.
Why might a highly controlled laboratory study provide strong causal evidence but still tell us less about everyday relationships?
Experimental control can help isolate the effect of an independent variable, strengthening causal inference. However, artificial tasks and settings may not reproduce the complexity of everyday relationships, creating a possible ecological-validity limitation.
You see a viral headline claiming that scientists have “proved” why relationships fail. What should you check before believing it?
Check what was actually measured, the research method, the sample, whether the evidence was experimental or correlational, how the variables were operationalized, whether alternative explanations were controlled and whether the headline accurately reflects the researchers' conclusion.

IB Concepts in Human Relationships: Application

Can you use IB concepts to think like a psychologist rather than simply memorize theories?

IB Psychology is not just about remembering names such as Tajfel, Bandura, Asch, Berry, Gottman or Allport.

The goal is to use psychological knowledge to analyse behaviour.

Consider the following situation.

A school combines students from two previously separate programmes.

Within several weeks, students begin referring to themselves as:

“our programme”

and:

“their programme.”

Students mostly socialize with members of their original programme.

Teachers notice that disagreements between individual students are increasingly described as conflicts between the two groups.

The school responds by creating mixed project teams.

After several months, surveys show that attitudes between the groups have become more positive.

What should a psychologist conclude?

Different psychological perspectives highlight different processes.

Social Identity Theory could examine categorisation into in-groups and out-groups.

Social Learning Theory could examine whether students model attitudes and behaviour demonstrated by influential peers.

Cognitive explanations could examine stereotypes and expectations about the other group.

The Contact Hypothesis could explain why structured cooperation might improve intergroup relationships.

These perspectives may be complementary.

The school introduced mixed project teams and attitudes later improved.

Can we conclude:

“The project caused the improvement”?

Not automatically.

Other changes may have occurred during the same period.

  • Students may have developed friendships naturally.
  • Teachers may have changed their behaviour.
  • Wider school norms may have changed.

A stronger causal conclusion would require evidence capable of ruling out plausible alternative explanations.

The school measured improvement using a questionnaire.

But what does “better relationships” mean?

Researchers could measure:

  • attitudes toward the other group
  • number of cross-group friendships
  • observed cooperation
  • frequency of conflict
  • willingness to work together

Different operationalizations may produce different conclusions.

Measurement therefore influences what psychologists believe they have discovered.

Students may answer questionnaires in socially desirable ways.

For example, after teachers repeatedly emphasize inclusion, students may know that:

“I respect the other group”

is the socially acceptable response.

This does not necessarily mean their private attitudes or actual behaviour have changed to the same degree.

Researchers therefore need to consider response bias.

Human relationships are not fixed.

The students' group identities, attitudes and friendships may change through repeated interaction.

A relationship between two groups that begins with distrust can develop differently as members gain new experiences.

Psychologists can therefore investigate how and why behaviour changes over time.

Psychological knowledge can be used to influence real people.

If a school uses the Contact Hypothesis to design an intervention, it has a responsibility to consider:

  • whether participation is appropriate
  • whether groups have equal status
  • whether anyone could experience harm
  • whether discrimination or unequal power is being ignored
  • whether the intervention respects the people involved

Applying psychology creates ethical responsibilities.

Imagine someone tells you:

“People from those two groups just don't get along.”

An IB Psychology response should go much further.

You could ask:

Perspective: What psychological processes could explain the conflict?

Causality: What evidence shows what produced it?

Measurement: How was the relationship between the groups assessed?

Bias: Could the evidence be distorted?

Change: Has the relationship changed over time?

Responsibility: What should psychologists consider before trying to change it?

These concepts turn a simple opinion into a psychological investigation.

The six IB concepts are not simply another list to memorize.

Use them as questions for analysing psychological claims:

  • Bias — Could the evidence or interpretation be distorted?
  • Causality — What can we actually say caused the behaviour?
  • Change — How and why might behaviour develop over time?
  • Measurement — How was the psychological behaviour turned into something researchers could study?
  • Perspective — What different explanations or viewpoints are possible?
  • Responsibility — What are the consequences and ethical responsibilities of psychological research and its application?
The mixed project teams were introduced and prejudice later decreased. Which IB concept should immediately make you cautious about saying the intervention worked?
Causality. The timing is consistent with the intervention contributing to the change, but other variables may also have changed. The strength of the causal conclusion depends on the research design.
Students report more positive attitudes on a questionnaire, but they still avoid members of the other group during lunch. Which IB concept becomes especially important?
Measurement. The questionnaire measures reported attitudes, while lunchtime behaviour provides a different operationalization of the relationship. The two measures may produce different conclusions about whether relationships actually improved.
Think of a claim about relationships you have encountered in everyday life or social media. How could the IB concepts help you evaluate it rather than simply accepting or rejecting it?
You could consider the perspective behind the explanation, examine the evidence and possible bias, question causal claims, investigate how the behaviour was measured, consider whether relationships change over time and think about the responsibility involved in applying the claim to real people.

Survey & Questionnaire Practical: Why Surveys & Questionnaires?

How can psychologists investigate people's attitudes, beliefs and experiences when those things cannot always be directly observed?

Imagine you want to investigate this question:

“Do students feel a stronger sense of belonging when they participate in school clubs?”

You cannot simply look at a student and know how strongly they feel they belong.

One solution is to ASK them.

This is why surveys and questionnaires are useful psychological research tools.

A survey is a research method used to collect information from a sample of people about variables such as:

  • attitudes
  • beliefs
  • opinions
  • experiences
  • behaviours
  • perceptions

A questionnaire is one common tool researchers can use to collect survey data.

For example, researchers interested in interpersonal relationships could ask students about:

  • sense of belonging
  • friendship quality
  • experiences of group membership
  • attitudes toward other groups
  • communication
  • perceived social support

A questionnaire presents participants with a standardized set of questions.

This means participants generally receive the same questions in the same format.

This can make responses easier to compare.

However, the quality of the data depends heavily on the quality of the questions.

A badly designed questionnaire can produce misleading data even if hundreds of people complete it.

Closed questions provide predetermined response options.

Example:

“How strongly do you agree with the statement:

‘I feel accepted by the people in my class.’”

1 — Strongly disagree

2 — Disagree

3 — Neither agree nor disagree

4 — Agree

5 — Strongly agree

This is an example of a rating-scale item.

Closed questions can:

  • produce quantitative data
  • be relatively quick to answer
  • make responses easier to compare
  • allow researchers to identify patterns across participants

Predetermined options may fail to capture the complexity of what participants actually think or experience.

Open questions allow participants to respond in their own words.

Example:

“Describe one situation that has made you feel particularly included or excluded at school.”

Open questions can:

  • produce richer responses
  • reveal experiences researchers did not anticipate
  • allow participants to explain their views

Responses can:

  • take longer to analyse
  • be difficult to compare
  • require interpretation by the researcher

This can introduce additional researcher judgement into the analysis.

Observation is useful when psychologists want to investigate visible behaviour.

But some psychological variables cannot be directly observed.

For example:

You might observe that a student spends lunchtime alone.

But you cannot automatically conclude:

“The student feels lonely.”

They might enjoy spending time alone.

A questionnaire could provide information about the participant's own experience.

This demonstrates an important principle:

Different research methods provide access to different kinds of evidence.

Questionnaires usually produce self-report data because participants provide information about themselves.

This gives researchers access to thoughts, attitudes and experiences that may otherwise be difficult to measure.

But self-report creates potential problems.

Participants may:

  • forget information
  • misunderstand questions
  • answer inaccurately
  • present themselves positively
  • give the answer they believe is socially acceptable

Therefore:

What people SAY and what people actually DO are not always identical.

Suppose researchers want to investigate prejudice between two groups of students.

They could ask:

“How positively do you feel toward students from the other group?”

This provides useful information about reported attitudes.

But imagine students know their teachers strongly support inclusion.

Some students may provide socially acceptable answers even if their private attitudes are different.

This is an example of SOCIAL DESIRABILITY BIAS.

Researchers therefore need to think carefully about what their questionnaire actually measures.

Imagine a questionnaire finds that students who report having more close friendships also report greater happiness.

What can we conclude?

We can say the variables are ASSOCIATED in this sample.

We cannot automatically conclude:

“Having more friends causes greater happiness.”

Perhaps happier people form more friendships.

Perhaps another variable influences both.

Survey research can identify important patterns, but the research design determines the conclusions psychologists can make.

A questionnaire does not simply “measure what people think.”

Ask:

What psychological variable are we trying to measure? → How has it been operationalized? → What type of data are we collecting? → What biases could affect the responses? → What conclusions does the research design allow?

Why might a questionnaire be more useful than observation if a psychologist wants to investigate students' sense of belonging?
Sense of belonging is an internal experience that cannot be directly observed. A questionnaire can ask participants about their own perceptions and experiences. Observation could provide additional behavioural evidence, but observing someone alone or with others does not by itself reveal how strongly that person feels they belong.
You want to investigate friendship quality. Would you choose an open question or a closed question? Why?
Either could be appropriate depending on the research aim. A closed rating-scale question could make friendship-quality scores easier to compare across many participants. An open question could provide richer information about what participants believe makes a friendship strong. Researchers can also combine both types.
A survey finds that students who spend more time with friends report lower stress. Why can you not immediately conclude that spending time with friends reduces stress?
The variables are associated, but the direction of the relationship is uncertain and other variables could be involved. Lower-stress students might socialize more, social interaction might influence stress, or another factor could influence both. A correlational survey does not by itself establish causation.

Survey & Questionnaire Practical: Designing Good Questions

A questionnaire is only as good as the questions it contains.

Consider this question:

“Don't you agree that spending too much time on social media damages friendships?”

What is wrong with it?

The wording encourages participants toward a particular answer.

This is a LEADING QUESTION.

Poor question design can influence participants' responses and reduce the quality of the evidence collected.

Questions should be understandable and as unambiguous as possible.

Poor question:

“Do you regularly interact positively with your peers?”

What does “regularly” mean?

What counts as “positively”?

What does “peers” mean to the participant?

A clearer question might be:

“During the past 7 days, on how many days did you spend at least 30 minutes socializing with friends outside lessons?”

The second question gives the participant clearer criteria.

A leading question encourages a particular response.

Poor question:

“Do you agree that supportive friendships improve students' lives?”

The wording already suggests that friendships are supportive and beneficial.

A more neutral version could be:

“How satisfied are you with the support you receive from your close friends?”

1 — Very dissatisfied

2 — Dissatisfied

3 — Neither satisfied nor dissatisfied

4 — Satisfied

5 — Very satisfied

A double-barrelled question asks about two things at once.

Example:

“How satisfied are you with your friendships and family relationships?”

Imagine a participant is very satisfied with their friendships but very dissatisfied with their family relationships.

How should they answer?

The researcher would not know which part of the question produced the response.

Instead, separate the variables:

“How satisfied are you with your friendships?”

and:

“How satisfied are you with your family relationships?”

ONE question should measure ONE thing.

Consider:

“How often do you argue with your friends?”

Should the participant think about:

  • this week?
  • this month?
  • this year?
  • their entire friendship?

A clearer question would specify a period:

“During the past 30 days, approximately how many disagreements have you had with a close friend?”

Specific time frames can make participants' interpretations more consistent.

Imagine asking:

“How often do you communicate with your closest friend?”

Never

Sometimes

Often

Very often

What exactly does “often” mean?

Different participants may interpret the categories differently.

Depending on the research question, more concrete response options may improve consistency.

For example:

“How many days during the past 7 days did you communicate with your closest friend?”

0

1

2

3

4

5

6

7

Researchers must make sure response options match what they are trying to measure.

Rating scales should generally provide a reasonable range of responses rather than pushing participants toward one side.

For example:

How strongly do you agree?

1 — Strongly disagree

2 — Disagree

3 — Neither agree nor disagree

4 — Agree

5 — Strongly agree

The response options allow both disagreement and agreement.

Good questionnaire design requires researchers to turn an abstract psychological concept into something measurable.

Suppose the concept is:

SENSE OF BELONGING

That cannot be directly measured like height.

Researchers need an operational definition.

For example, they might measure belonging using participants' responses to several statements such as:

“I feel accepted by other students at my school.”

“I feel like I am part of my school community.”

“I feel that other students include me.”

Participants could rate each statement from:

1 — Strongly disagree

to:

5 — Strongly agree

The researchers have now turned an abstract concept into measurable responses.

But they should still ask:

Does this questionnaire genuinely capture “sense of belonging”?

That is a MEASUREMENT question.

Before collecting the final data, researchers can PILOT the questionnaire with a small number of people similar to the intended participants.

A pilot can help identify:

  • confusing wording
  • unclear instructions
  • missing response options
  • questions everyone interprets differently
  • questions participants are uncomfortable answering
  • questionnaires that take too long

Example:

A researcher asks:

“How much time do you spend with your social group?”

During the pilot, participants repeatedly ask:

“Does online contact count?”

The researcher has discovered an ambiguity BEFORE the main study.

They can now clarify the question.

Suppose you want to investigate:

“Is perceived social support associated with school belonging?”

Before collecting any data, you need to decide:

  • How will SOCIAL SUPPORT be operationalized?
  • How will SCHOOL BELONGING be operationalized?
  • What questions will participants answer?
  • What response scales will you use?
  • Could the wording influence their responses?
  • Will participants interpret the questions consistently?

Good research begins before the first participant answers anything.

When designing a questionnaire, repeatedly ask:

“Will every participant understand this question in approximately the same way?”

Then check:

  • Is it clear?
  • Is it neutral?
  • Does it ask only one thing?
  • Is the time frame clear?
  • Are the response options appropriate?
  • Does it actually measure the intended psychological construct?
What is wrong with this question?
It is leading because the wording encourages participants to accept the idea that frequent phone checking damages relationships. It also uses potentially vague wording such as “constantly.” A more neutral and clearly operationalized question would reduce these problems.
What is wrong with this question?
It is double-barrelled because it asks about friendship satisfaction and family communication in the same question. A participant could feel very differently about the two. They should be measured using separate questions.
Imagine you are creating a questionnaire about loneliness. Why is writing “How lonely are you?” by itself probably not enough for a strong psychological investigation?
Loneliness is a complex psychological construct. Researchers need to decide how it will be operationalized, provide clear response options or use multiple items, consider a relevant time frame and examine whether the questions actually capture the construct they intend to measure.

Survey & Questionnaire Practical: Ethical Considerations

A questionnaire may look harmless.

But asking people about relationships, prejudice, loneliness, conflict or belonging can involve personal and sensitive information.

Psychological researchers therefore have ethical responsibilities before, during and after collecting data.

Participants should receive enough appropriate information to make an informed decision about participation.

They should understand relevant information such as:

  • that participation is voluntary
  • what they will generally be asked to do
  • how their data will be used
  • whether there are foreseeable risks or sensitive topics
  • their right to withdraw

Consent is not simply getting someone to tick a box.

The participant needs meaningful information on which to base the decision.

Participants should not be pressured into participating.

This is particularly important when there is a POWER DIFFERENCE.

Imagine a teacher says:

“Everyone in my class needs to complete my psychology questionnaire.”

Students may feel they cannot refuse even if the teacher says participation is voluntary.

Researchers should consider whether participants genuinely feel free to say no.

Participants should be able to stop participating without being punished or pressured.

Where appropriate and practically possible, researchers should also explain whether participants can request withdrawal of their data and how this can be done.

Relationship questionnaires can involve sensitive information.

Imagine asking:

“Have you experienced serious conflict with someone in your family?”

Participants may not want teachers, classmates or other participants to know their response.

Researchers therefore need appropriate procedures for protecting participants' information.

CONFIDENTIALITY means that identifiable participant information is protected and not improperly disclosed.

ANONYMITY means that responses are not linked to information that identifies the participant.

These terms are related but are NOT identical.

A study can be confidential without being fully anonymous.

Example:

A researcher collects participants' email addresses so they can be contacted later.

The researcher protects those identities and does not disclose individual responses.

The data may be confidential, but it is not fully anonymous to the researcher.

Researchers should consider whether questions could cause:

  • distress
  • embarrassment
  • anxiety
  • discomfort
  • unwanted disclosure of sensitive information

For example:

A questionnaire investigating relationship conflict should not include highly personal questions simply because the researcher finds them interesting.

Researchers should ask:

“Is this information genuinely necessary for the research question?”

Sensitive questions may require additional safeguards.

Questionnaire research should avoid unnecessary deception.

Sometimes revealing the exact research aim beforehand could influence responses.

If some information must be withheld for a justified research reason, researchers need to consider whether this is ethically acceptable and whether participants should receive an appropriate debrief afterward.

After participation, researchers can provide information explaining the purpose of the research and relevant details participants need to know.

Debriefing is particularly important if any information was withheld or if participants may have misunderstood the purpose of the study.

A debrief should not simply say:

“Thanks for participating.”

It should provide meaningful information appropriate to the study.

Researchers should also think about:

  • where data will be stored
  • who can access it
  • whether identifying information is necessary
  • how responses will be reported
  • how long information needs to be retained

Collecting unnecessary identifying information creates unnecessary risk.

Imagine your class investigates:

“Is school belonging associated with friendship quality?”

It would be inappropriate to ask students to write their names on the questionnaire unless identification is genuinely necessary.

Students should understand that participation is voluntary.

Questions should avoid unnecessary intrusion.

Responses should be handled responsibly.

The class should report GROUP PATTERNS rather than exposing individual students' answers.

The practical is an opportunity to learn research methodology.

It is not an excuse to investigate classmates' private lives.

Suppose you want to ask:

“Have you ever been rejected by a friendship group?”

Before including it, ask:

  • Why do I need this question?
  • Could it cause distress or embarrassment?
  • Can the research question be answered using a less intrusive question?
  • Who will see the response?
  • Could the participant be identified?
  • What will happen if someone becomes uncomfortable?

Ethical thinking should happen BEFORE data collection, not after something goes wrong.

Ethics is not a checklist added after designing a study.

Ethical decisions affect the entire research process:

Research question → participant recruitment → informed consent → question design → data collection → confidentiality and data protection → right to withdraw → debriefing → reporting the results

A well-designed psychological investigation should be BOTH methodologically useful and ethically responsible.

A teacher says participation is voluntary but distributes the questionnaire during class and tells every student to complete it. What ethical issue should you notice?
The power relationship may undermine genuinely voluntary participation. Students could feel pressured to participate because the request comes from their teacher. Researchers need to consider whether participants realistically feel able to refuse without negative consequences.
A questionnaire does not ask for names, but it asks for exact age, nationality, class, gender and several other details. Can the researcher automatically promise complete anonymity?
No. A combination of demographic details may make some participants identifiable even without names. Researchers should consider whether each piece of identifying or potentially identifying information is necessary.
You are designing a real class questionnaire. Before adding a personal question, what should you ask yourself?
Ask whether the question is genuinely necessary for the research aim, whether it could cause distress or unwanted disclosure, how the response will be protected and whether the same research question could be investigated using a less intrusive question.

63. Identify Methodological Components

Can you look at a psychological investigation and identify how it was actually designed?

Consider this proposed class practical:

RESEARCH QUESTION:

“Is perceived social support associated with students' sense of school belonging?”

Researchers give students a questionnaire containing:

  • 5 rating-scale items measuring perceived social support
  • 5 rating-scale items measuring school belonging

Participants respond to each statement from:

1 — Strongly disagree

to:

5 — Strongly agree

Researchers calculate a social-support score and a school-belonging score for each participant and examine whether the two variables are associated.

Let's break the investigation apart.

The researchers are using a SURVEY.

A QUESTIONNAIRE is the tool used to collect the data.

This distinction matters:

Survey = the research method

Questionnaire = a data-collection tool that can be used within survey research

Perceived social support

But “social support” is an abstract psychological construct.

Researchers therefore need an OPERATIONAL DEFINITION.

In this investigation:

Perceived social support is operationalized as the participant's score on the 5 social-support questionnaire items.

School belonging

This is also an abstract construct.

In this investigation:

School belonging is operationalized as the participant's score on the 5 school-belonging questionnaire items.

The rating-scale responses produce numerical data that researchers can analyse quantitatively.

However, researchers should still ask:

Do these numbers genuinely represent the psychological constructs we are interested in?

Assigning a number to something does not automatically make the measurement valid.

Researchers also need to decide:

WHO will participate?

Suppose the researchers ask students who happen to be available in their psychology class.

This would be a form of CONVENIENCE SAMPLING.

It is easy and practical.

But the sample may not represent the wider population of students.

If the psychology class differs systematically from other students, GENERALIZABILITY may be limited.

Participants should receive the same:

  • instructions
  • questions
  • response scales
  • basic procedure

Standardization helps reduce unwanted variation in how the questionnaire is administered.

For example, imagine one group is told:

“This questionnaire investigates whether students with poor friendships struggle to fit in.”

Another group is simply told:

“Please complete the following questionnaire.”

The different instructions could influence responses.

Several forms of bias could affect the results.

SOCIAL DESIRABILITY:

Participants may answer in ways they believe make them look socially successful or well adjusted.

RESPONSE BIAS:

Participants may develop patterns such as repeatedly selecting similar response categories without carefully considering each item.

SAMPLING BIAS:

The people selected may differ systematically from the wider population the researchers hope to understand.

RESEARCHER BIAS:

Researchers' expectations could influence decisions about question wording, interpretation or analysis.

Researchers should consider:

  • informed consent
  • voluntary participation
  • right to withdraw
  • privacy
  • confidentiality
  • unnecessary collection of identifying information
  • possible discomfort caused by personal questions
  • appropriate debriefing

These are not separate from methodology.

Ethical decisions influence how the investigation can be designed and conducted.

Suppose researchers find:

Students reporting greater social support also tend to report greater school belonging.

A justified conclusion is:

“Perceived social support and school belonging were positively associated in this sample.”

An unjustified conclusion would be:

“Social support causes students to feel that they belong at school.”

Why?

Because neither variable was manipulated.

The direction of the relationship is uncertain, and third variables may influence both.

For example:

School belonging might help students form supportive relationships.

Supportive relationships might strengthen school belonging.

A positive school environment might influence both.

The method determines the conclusion.

When reading any psychological study, train yourself to identify:

RESEARCH QUESTION ↓ METHOD ↓ SAMPLE ↓ VARIABLES ↓ OPERATIONALIZATION ↓ PROCEDURE ↓ DATA ↓ ETHICS ↓ POSSIBLE BIAS ↓ JUSTIFIED CONCLUSION

This turns a description of a study into a methodological analysis.

Do not evaluate a study by memorizing:

“Strength = control.”

“Limitation = small sample.”

Instead ask:

  • How was THIS study designed?
  • How was THIS variable measured?
  • Who provided the evidence?
  • What bias could affect THIS procedure?
  • What conclusion does THIS method justify?

Methodological evaluation should be specific to the investigation.

A researcher says, “I measured friendship quality.” What should an IB Psychology student immediately ask?
“How was friendship quality operationalized?” Friendship quality is an abstract psychological construct, so the researcher must specify how it was turned into something measurable, such as a score on a questionnaire with clearly defined items.
A class finds a strong positive association between social support and school belonging. What is the strongest conclusion they can make from a correlational survey?
They can conclude that greater reported social support was associated with greater reported school belonging in their sample. They cannot conclude from the association alone that one variable caused the other.
Why should you identify the sampling method before deciding how widely the results of a psychological investigation can be applied?
The sample determines who actually provided the evidence. If the sampling procedure produces a narrow or systematically different group, the results may not generalize well to the wider population. A large sample is not automatically representative.

Review & Exam Practice: Exam-Style Question 2

A strong psychology answer does more than describe a theory and a study.

It also shows that you understand what the evidence CAN and CANNOT tell us.

Consider this exam-style question:

“Evaluate one explanation of human relationships.”

Let's use SOCIAL IDENTITY THEORY as an example.

Evaluation means making a reasoned judgement about an explanation using relevant evidence and psychological reasoning.

You could consider:

  • supporting evidence
  • conflicting or limiting evidence
  • research methodology
  • causality
  • ecological validity
  • cultural or contextual influences
  • alternative explanations
  • the extent to which the explanation accounts for behaviour

The goal is NOT to create a memorized list of:

“one strength and one limitation.”

Your evaluation should help answer:

HOW WELL DOES THIS EXPLANATION ACCOUNT FOR THE BEHAVIOUR?

You cannot evaluate something you have not clearly explained.

Social Identity Theory proposes that part of an individual's self-concept can derive from membership in social groups.

Important processes include:

Social categorisation → Social identification → Social comparison

Once an in-group becomes psychologically meaningful, people may under some conditions show preferential attitudes or behaviour toward members of that group.

Tajfel et al. (1971) provide relevant experimental evidence.

Participants were assigned to minimal groups and then made allocation decisions involving anonymous in-group and out-group members.

Patterns of in-group favouritism emerged despite the groups having little meaningful history.

WHY IS THIS IMPORTANT?

The study demonstrates that even relatively minimal social categorisation can influence behaviour toward in-group and out-group members.

This supports an important prediction of Social Identity Theory.

Do not simply write:

“A strength is that it was a laboratory experiment.”

Explain WHY that matters.

For example:

Because group membership was created under controlled conditions, researchers could investigate the effect of categorisation while limiting many influences found in existing real-world groups.

This strengthens causal inference within the experimental setting.

BUT:

The minimal groups were highly artificial.

Real social identities may involve:

  • history
  • culture
  • status
  • competition
  • discrimination
  • emotional attachment
  • long-term interaction

Therefore, behaviour in a minimal-group task may not fully represent the complexity of real intergroup relationships.

That is a meaningful evaluation because it affects how widely we can apply the evidence.

Social Identity Theory can help explain why group membership may influence social behaviour.

But it does not mean:

“Group membership automatically causes prejudice.”

People belong to many groups without becoming hostile toward outsiders.

Other influences may include:

  • social norms
  • competition
  • previous experiences
  • cultural context
  • personality
  • learning
  • situational factors

This does not make Social Identity Theory useless.

It establishes the BOUNDARIES of the explanation.

Imagine a teenager begins making negative comments about a rival school after joining a new friendship group.

Social Identity Theory could explain the importance of:

IN-GROUP versus OUT-GROUP membership.

Social Learning Theory could explain how the teenager observes and imitates comments that receive approval from friends.

Conformity could explain pressure to publicly agree with group norms.

These explanations can operate together.

A good evaluation therefore does not always ask:

“Which theory wins?”

It can ask:

“What part of the behaviour does each explanation account for?”

A weak conclusion:

“Social Identity Theory is good because research supports it, but it also has limitations.”

A stronger conclusion:

“Social Identity Theory provides a useful explanation of how social categorisation and identification can influence behaviour toward in-group and out-group members, and controlled research such as Tajfel et al. provides evidence that even minimal categorisation can produce in-group favouritism. However, real group relationships are influenced by additional historical, cultural, cognitive and situational factors. The theory is therefore valuable for explaining one important process in human relationships, but should not be treated as a complete explanation of intergroup behaviour.”

Imagine students from two school houses become increasingly hostile during an inter-house competition.

A weak answer says:

“This is Social Identity Theory because there are two groups.”

A developed answer asks:

  • How did categorisation create an in-group and out-group?
  • How strongly do students identify with the groups?
  • What social comparisons are occurring?
  • What behaviour is actually being influenced?
  • What other factors, such as competition or group norms, are involved?
  • What evidence would allow us to test the explanation?

That is psychological evaluation.

For an EVALUATE question, think:

EXPLAIN THE THEORY ↓ USE RELEVANT EVIDENCE ↓ ASK WHAT THE EVIDENCE ACTUALLY SHOWS ↓ IDENTIFY LIMITATIONS OR BOUNDARIES ↓ CONSIDER ALTERNATIVE OR COMPLEMENTARY EXPLANATIONS ↓ MAKE A REASONED JUDGEMENT

Evaluation is not:

“Strength + limitation.”

Evaluation is:

“How convincing is this explanation, given the evidence and what we know about the complexity of behaviour?”

Why is “Tajfel was a laboratory experiment, so it had high control” not enough as evaluation?
The student needs to explain why the control matters. In Tajfel's study, controlled minimal-group conditions helped isolate the influence of social categorisation from many features of existing real-world groups. This strengthens causal inference within the study, but the artificial setting also raises questions about how well the findings represent complex real social identities.

No. A psychological theory can explain an important process without explaining every influence on behaviour. Social Identity Theory may explain how categorisation and identification influence behaviour, while learning, norms, competition, culture and individual differences may also contribute.
What is the difference between an evaluation and a memorized “strength and limitation” list?
Evaluation connects evidence and reasoning to a judgement about the explanation. Instead of merely naming strengths and limitations, the student explains how methodological issues, supporting evidence, alternative explanations and context affect what the theory can reasonably explain.

HL Extension: Motivation: Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Motivation

Would you behave differently if you helped a friend because you genuinely wanted to rather than because someone paid you?

Both behaviours might look identical from the outside.

But the MOTIVATION behind them is different.

INTRINSIC MOTIVATION occurs when behaviour is performed because the activity itself is experienced as interesting, enjoyable or personally satisfying.

Example:

Maya spends an hour helping her friend prepare for an interview because she genuinely enjoys helping her and values spending time together.

There is no external reward required for the behaviour to feel worthwhile.

EXTRINSIC MOTIVATION occurs when behaviour is performed because it is connected to an outcome that is separable from the activity itself.

Possible external outcomes include:

  • money
  • grades
  • prizes
  • recognition
  • approval
  • avoiding punishment

Example:

A student participates in a school volunteering programme primarily because completing it will earn an award.

The behaviour may still be useful and positive.

The distinction concerns WHY the person is doing it.

Imagine two students stay after school to help organize an event.

STUDENT A:

“I enjoy helping with these events and spending time with everyone.”

STUDENT B:

“I need this for my service certificate.”

Both students perform the same observable behaviour.

But their motivations differ.

This creates an important methodological problem:

Psychologists cannot always determine motivation simply by observing behaviour.

The theory proposes three basic psychological needs:

AUTONOMY

Feeling that one's behaviour involves meaningful choice and self-direction.

COMPETENCE

Feeling capable and effective.

RELATEDNESS

Feeling connected to and cared for by others.

People are more likely to experience relatedness when they feel:

  • accepted
  • cared for
  • connected
  • significant to other people

Example:

A student joins a drama group.

The activity can satisfy relatedness while also providing opportunities for competence and autonomy.

“I'd love to see you this weekend if you're free.”

Both involve a desire for connection.

Students reporting greater belonging also report greater well-being.

What caused what?

Belonging may contribute to well-being.

Greater well-being may make social interaction easier.

A supportive environment may increase both.

Without an appropriate research design, correlation cannot determine the causal direction.

Imagine a school wants to increase students' sense of belonging.

It creates mandatory social events.

Attendance increases.

Has belonging improved?

Not necessarily.

ATTENDANCE is observable behaviour.

BELONGING is a psychological construct.

Students may attend because attendance is compulsory while still feeling disconnected.

Researchers need evidence that actually measures the psychological outcome they claim has changed.

When evaluating motivational explanations, ask:

  • How was the motivation measured?
  • Can the same behaviour have different motives?
  • Could several motives operate simultaneously?
  • Could culture change the meaning of the behaviour?
  • What causal conclusion does the evidence justify?

Motivation is psychologically useful precisely because it attempts to explain WHY behaviour occurs.

But because motives are not directly observable, psychologists must be careful about how they measure and infer them.

A student attends every school social event. Why can you not conclude that they have a strong need to belong?
The behaviour could have several motivations. The student might enjoy the events, attend because friends are there, seek recognition, feel pressured to participate or simply enjoy the activities. Observable behaviour does not directly reveal the psychological motive behind it.
A questionnaire finds that students with stronger feelings of belonging report greater happiness. What causality problem should you identify?
The direction of causality is uncertain. Belonging could contribute to happiness, happier students might find it easier to form social connections, or another variable such as a supportive school environment could influence both.
Why should psychologists be cautious about assuming that family obligation is always an example of external pressure?
The meaning of family obligation can vary across individuals and cultural contexts. A person may personally value responsibility toward family and experience the behaviour as meaningful and self-endorsed. Researchers should investigate how the individual understands the motivation rather than imposing an interpretation from outside.

HL Extension: Culture: Cultural Variation in Relationships

Do friendships, families and romantic relationships mean exactly the same thing everywhere?

Humans form relationships across all known cultures.

But culture can influence:

  • what people expect from relationships
  • how relationships are expressed
  • how people communicate
  • how responsibilities are understood
  • how conflict is managed
  • how relationships are formed and maintained

This does NOT mean:

“Culture determines behaviour.”

It means cultural norms and values can provide a social context that influences relationship behaviour.

Culture can be understood as a system of shared meanings, values, practices and norms that develops within groups and is transmitted socially.

Culture influences what people learn about:

“What is normal?”

“What is appropriate?”

“What should a good friend do?”

“What responsibilities do family members have?”

“How should disagreement be expressed?”

These expectations can influence behaviour within relationships.

Imagine two university students are offered an opportunity to study abroad.

STUDENT A thinks:

“This is my decision. I need to choose what is best for my future.”

STUDENT B thinks:

“I need to discuss this with my family because my decision affects all of us.”

Neither response is inherently more psychologically healthy.

The students may be drawing on different expectations about:

  • independence
  • interdependence
  • family responsibility
  • decision-making

Culture can influence what people believe relationships REQUIRE from them.

One commonly studied cultural dimension is:

INDIVIDUALISM ↔ COLLECTIVISM

In more individualistic cultural contexts, greater emphasis may be placed on:

  • independence
  • personal goals
  • individual choice
  • personal autonomy

In more collectivistic cultural contexts, greater emphasis may be placed on:

  • interdependence
  • group goals
  • relational obligations
  • maintaining group harmony

These are broad cultural tendencies.

They are NOT personality labels for every person in a country.

Imagine someone receives an important job offer in another city.

One person might prioritize:

“What opportunity is best for me?”

Another might prioritize:

“How will this decision affect my parents, partner or wider family?”

Different cultural expectations about autonomy and obligation may influence how the decision is approached.

The behaviour makes sense within the person's SOCIAL AND CULTURAL CONTEXT.

Culture can also influence communication norms.

For example, cultural contexts can differ in expectations surrounding:

  • directness
  • disagreement
  • emotional expression
  • politeness
  • eye contact
  • silence

Imagine someone disagrees with their manager.

In one context, openly stating:

“I disagree with that decision”

may be interpreted as confident and appropriately direct.

In another context, the same statement may be interpreted as disrespectful.

The words have not changed.

The SOCIAL MEANING of the communication has.

Cultural norms may also influence how people manage disagreement.

Some contexts may place greater value on:

  • direct discussion
  • explicit expression of individual preferences

Others may place greater value on:

  • avoiding open confrontation
  • preserving harmony
  • indirect communication

This does not mean every member of a culture behaves in the same way.

Individual differences and specific relationship contexts remain important.

Expectations about friendship can also vary.

For example, people may differ in expectations surrounding:

  • emotional disclosure
  • loyalty
  • frequency of contact
  • practical support
  • obligations to friends
  • boundaries between friendship and family

A behaviour interpreted as:

“very close friendship”

in one cultural setting may not have exactly the same meaning in another.

A major mistake is to write:

“People from individualistic cultures behave like this.”

“People from collectivistic cultures behave like that.”

Cultures contain enormous variation.

Two people living in the same country may differ because of:

  • family background
  • age
  • gender
  • socioeconomic context
  • region
  • religion
  • personal experiences
  • personality
  • exposure to multiple cultures

National culture is not a psychological instruction manual.

Culture can also CHANGE.

Migration, technology, globalization, education and contact between cultural groups can influence norms over time.

Individuals can also participate in multiple cultural groups simultaneously.

For example, someone may identify with:

  • a national culture
  • a family culture
  • a school culture
  • a religious community
  • an online community
  • an international peer group

Relationship behaviour may therefore reflect several cultural influences rather than one.

Imagine Mei rarely disagrees openly with her parents.

Her friend concludes:

“She must be afraid to express herself.”

That interpretation may be correct.

But it may also be wrong.

Mei might value a communication style in which disagreement is expressed more indirectly or in which preserving family harmony is important.

A psychologist should investigate the MEANING of the behaviour within its cultural and relational context rather than automatically interpreting it through one cultural perspective.

Avoid:

CULTURE → BEHAVIOUR

A stronger psychological model is:

Cultural norms and values → influence expectations and interpretations → interact with individual experiences and the relationship context → contribute to behaviour

Culture influences relationships.

It does not program everyone within a culture to behave identically.

Why is saying “collectivistic people care about family more than individualistic people” a poor psychological conclusion?
It turns a broad cultural tendency into a stereotype about individuals. Individualism and collectivism describe patterns in values and social organization, but people within any culture vary substantially. Family commitment can also be strong in both individualistic and collectivistic contexts while being expressed differently.
Think of a behaviour that could be considered respectful in one cultural or family context but disrespectful in another. Why does this matter for relationship psychology?
The meaning of social behaviour depends partly on learned norms and expectations. Behaviours such as eye contact, direct disagreement, silence, emotional expression or forms of address can communicate different meanings in different contexts. Psychologists therefore need to interpret behaviour within its cultural setting.
Why might describing someone only according to their nationality provide a poor explanation of their relationship behaviour?
People participate in multiple cultural and social groups and differ in family background, experiences, personality, region, age and other influences. National culture may provide useful contextual information, but it should not be treated as determining the behaviour of every individual.

HL Extension: Culture: Emic and Etic Approaches

How can psychologists study relationships across cultures without assuming that one culture's way of understanding behaviour applies to everyone?

Imagine a psychologist develops a questionnaire in one country to measure:

“close friendship.”

The questionnaire asks participants how often they:

  • discuss personal feelings
  • share private problems
  • verbally express affection

The psychologist then takes the same questionnaire to another culture.

Participants score lower.

Can the researcher conclude:

“People in this culture have weaker friendships”?

NO.

The questionnaire may be measuring one CULTURAL EXPRESSION of closeness rather than closeness itself.

This is why psychologists need to understand EMIC and ETIC approaches.

An ETIC approach studies behaviour from outside a particular cultural system and attempts to identify patterns or concepts that can be compared across cultures.

An etic approach may ask:

“Does this psychological process occur across different cultures?”

or:

“How do cultures compare on the same measure?”

Example:

Researchers use the same relationship-satisfaction scale in several countries and compare average scores.

Using common measures can allow researchers to:

  • compare groups
  • identify similarities
  • investigate possible cross-cultural patterns
  • test whether a psychological explanation generalizes beyond one setting

This can help psychology move beyond findings from only one population.

An IMPOSED ETIC occurs when researchers assume that a concept or measurement developed in one culture has the same meaning in another culture without sufficient justification.

Return to the friendship questionnaire.

Suppose the measure assumes:

“Close friends discuss personal emotions frequently.”

That may reflect one culturally influenced understanding of intimacy.

In another context, closeness might be expressed more strongly through:

  • practical help
  • loyalty
  • shared responsibilities
  • regular presence
  • support for family

Using emotional disclosure as the main measure could underestimate relationship closeness in that context.

The problem may be the MEASUREMENT rather than the relationship.

An EMIC approach attempts to understand behaviour from within a particular cultural context.

Researchers try to understand concepts, meanings and behaviours as they are understood by members of that culture.

Instead of beginning with:

“Here is our definition of a close relationship.”

an emic researcher might ask:

“What does a close relationship mean within this cultural context?”

This can reveal culturally specific meanings that an externally developed measure might miss.

Imagine researchers want to investigate:

“What makes someone a good adult child?”

An etic approach might use the same questionnaire across several cultures.

An emic approach might first investigate how people within a particular culture understand:

  • family responsibility
  • independence
  • respect
  • caregiving
  • financial support
  • emotional closeness

The emic approach may reveal that the concept itself is understood differently across settings.

Emic research can provide:

  • greater cultural context
  • locally meaningful concepts
  • richer understanding of behaviour
  • reduced risk of imposing outside assumptions

This is particularly useful when studying relationships because behaviours can have different meanings across cultural contexts.

If researchers develop completely different concepts and measures within every culture, direct comparison can become difficult.

For example:

Culture A measures friendship through emotional disclosure.

Culture B measures friendship through practical support.

Which group has “stronger friendships”?

The measures may not be directly comparable.

Emic depth can therefore come at the cost of easier cross-cultural comparison.

The approaches do not have to compete.

Researchers might:

1. Begin with EMIC research to understand how a concept is understood locally.

2. Identify features that appear meaningful across several cultural settings.

3. Develop an ETIC measure that allows comparison while remaining sensitive to cultural differences.

This can produce stronger cross-cultural research than simply assuming a measure developed in one setting works everywhere.

Emic and etic approaches are closely connected to the IB concept of MEASUREMENT.

Suppose researchers claim:

“Culture A has lower relationship satisfaction than Culture B.”

Ask:

  • How was relationship satisfaction operationalized?
  • Was the measure developed in one culture?
  • Do the questions have the same meaning in both settings?
  • Do response scales function similarly?
  • Does the construct itself have comparable meaning?

Before comparing numbers, psychologists need to know whether they are actually measuring the same thing.

Emic and etic approaches also connect to PERSPECTIVE.

An outsider may interpret a behaviour differently from someone inside the cultural context.

Example:

An adult lives with their parents into their thirties.

An outsider might interpret this as:

“lack of independence.”

Within another cultural context, the same behaviour might represent:

“family commitment and mutual support.”

Neither label should automatically be imposed without understanding the social meaning of the behaviour.

Imagine an international school wants to measure:

“How supported do students feel by their families?”

The school downloads a questionnaire developed in another country.

Before using it, a psychologist should ask:

  • What does “support” mean to students here?
  • Does support mainly involve emotional discussion?
  • Practical help?
  • Academic guidance?
  • Financial support?
  • Time together?
  • Family obligation?
  • Do the questionnaire items capture the ways support is actually experienced by this population?

That is culturally informed psychological measurement.

ETIC asks:

“What can we compare across cultures?”

EMIC asks:

“What does this behaviour mean within this culture?”

Neither approach is automatically superior.

Strong cross-cultural psychology asks:

  • Are we measuring the same psychological construct?
  • Does the behaviour have the same meaning?
  • Are we discovering a cultural difference — or creating one through the way we measured it?
Researchers use a friendship questionnaire developed in the United States with students in five different countries. Is this automatically a good etic study?
No. Cross-cultural comparison is useful, but researchers first need evidence that the questionnaire measures the same construct meaningfully across the different cultural settings. Otherwise, the study risks imposing assumptions from the culture in which the measure was developed.
Why could an emic approach reveal something about relationships that a standardized international questionnaire misses?
An emic approach investigates how relationships and behaviours are understood within the local cultural context. It may identify culturally meaningful expressions of closeness, obligation, support or communication that were not included in a measure developed elsewhere.
You discover that people in one country score lower on a questionnaire measuring emotional disclosure between friends. Why should you NOT immediately conclude that their friendships are less close?
The questionnaire may assume that emotional disclosure is the primary indicator of closeness. Friendship closeness could be expressed through different behaviours, such as loyalty, practical support, shared responsibilities or regular presence. Researchers need to establish that the measure has comparable meaning before interpreting the cultural difference.

HL Extension: Culture: Evaluation

Culture clearly matters in human relationships.

But how should psychologists study cultural influences without turning broad patterns into stereotypes?

This is the central challenge of evaluating cultural explanations.

Cultural explanations remind psychologists that behaviour does not occur in a social vacuum.

Consider this behaviour:

A university student consults their parents before accepting a job.

Without cultural context, someone might interpret this as:

“The student lacks independence.”

But within another cultural or family context, consulting parents may represent:

  • respect
  • interdependence
  • family responsibility
  • shared decision-making

Cultural psychology can therefore prevent psychologists from assuming that one cultural interpretation of behaviour is universal.

Suppose a relationship theory was developed entirely using participants from one country.

Can psychologists assume it works identically everywhere?

No.

Cross-cultural research allows psychologists to investigate whether:

  • similar processes appear across cultures
  • the strength of an effect varies
  • the meaning of behaviour differs
  • a theory needs modification

This improves psychology by testing the boundaries of explanations.

Broad cultural dimensions can become misleading when applied directly to individuals.

For example:

“Japan is collectivistic, therefore this Japanese participant values group harmony.”

This conclusion jumps from:

GROUP-LEVEL TENDENCY

to:

INDIVIDUAL PSYCHOLOGY

without sufficient evidence.

People within the same society can differ enormously.

Psychologists should therefore avoid treating cultural averages as descriptions of every individual.

Imagine two students from the same country.

One grew up in a large multigenerational household.

The other grew up in a small household and attended international schools for most of their life.

Their expectations about:

  • independence
  • family obligations
  • friendship
  • communication
  • romantic relationships

may differ considerably.

Knowing someone's nationality does not reveal their entire cultural experience.

Psychologists can also exaggerate cultural differences.

Two people from different countries may share:

  • similar values
  • similar educational experiences
  • similar online communities
  • similar socioeconomic backgrounds
  • similar relationship expectations

Culture matters, but national borders do not divide humanity into completely separate psychological types.

Cultural explanations can become outdated if culture is treated as fixed.

Norms surrounding:

  • dating
  • marriage
  • family roles
  • communication
  • gender expectations
  • technology
  • independence

can change across generations.

Example:

Relationship formation through dating apps may be viewed very differently by two generations living within the same society.

Psychologists therefore need to consider WHEN cultural evidence was collected as well as WHERE.

People increasingly interact across cultural settings.

Someone may simultaneously participate in:

  • a national culture
  • a family culture
  • an international school culture
  • a religious community
  • a sports community
  • online communities

Different cultural identities may become more important in different situations.

This makes simple labels such as:

“individualistic person”

or:

“collectivistic person”

potentially misleading.

Suppose researchers compare relationship satisfaction across ten countries.

They use exactly the same questionnaire.

That sounds standardized.

But standardization alone does not guarantee valid comparison.

Researchers still need to ask:

  • Does “relationship satisfaction” have comparable meaning?
  • Are the translated questions equivalent?
  • Are participants using the rating scale similarly?
  • Are culturally important aspects of relationships missing?

A measurement problem can appear to be a cultural difference.

Even careful translation can create difficulties.

A word such as:

“support”

may have several meanings.

Does support mean:

  • emotional encouragement?
  • practical help?
  • financial assistance?
  • advice?
  • spending time together?

Literal translation does not guarantee psychological equivalence.

Researchers also bring cultural assumptions into research.

They decide:

  • what questions matter
  • which behaviours to measure
  • how concepts are defined
  • what counts as a “healthy” relationship
  • how results are interpreted

Emic approaches can help researchers identify assumptions that might otherwise remain unnoticed.

Suppose researchers find that participants in Culture A report greater family obligation than participants in Culture B.

Can we conclude:

“Culture caused the difference”?

Not automatically.

The groups may also differ in:

  • age
  • education
  • socioeconomic conditions
  • family structure
  • religion
  • migration history
  • other social experiences

Culture is not a single experimental variable that can easily be isolated.

Imagine research finds that people in one country report less open disagreement with family members.

A weak conclusion is:

“People in that culture avoid conflict.”

A stronger analysis asks:

  • How was disagreement measured?
  • Does less open disagreement mean less conflict?
  • Could disagreement be communicated indirectly?
  • What cultural meaning does direct disagreement have?
  • Is there substantial variation within the culture?
  • Were equivalent groups compared?

The finding becomes psychologically meaningful only when interpreted carefully.

A study finds an average cultural difference between two countries. Why can you not automatically use that difference to explain the behaviour of one individual from either country?
The finding describes a group-level pattern, not every individual. There may be substantial variation within each culture, and the individual may have experiences, identities and values that differ from the group average.
Researchers use exactly the same translated relationship questionnaire in 15 countries. Why does this not automatically guarantee a fair comparison?
Standardized wording does not guarantee that the psychological construct or individual questions have equivalent meanings across cultures. Researchers need to consider translation, cultural interpretation, response styles and whether the measure captures locally meaningful aspects of relationships.
Why might research comparing “Western relationships” with “Asian relationships” immediately make you cautious?
Both categories contain enormous cultural diversity and risk treating very different populations as psychologically uniform. Strong research should define the populations precisely, justify the cultural comparison and avoid turning broad geographic labels into explanations.

Good cultural psychology does NOT ask only:

“How are cultures different?”

It also asks:

  • How large is the difference?
  • How much variation exists within each culture?
  • Was the same construct genuinely measured?
  • Could another variable explain the result?
  • Does the behaviour have the same meaning?
  • Are we discovering a cultural difference — or imposing one through our assumptions?

Culture is an important influence on relationships.

It should never become a shortcut for stereotyping individuals.

HL Extension: Technology: Technology and Relationship Formation

Can a meaningful relationship begin between two people who have never met face-to-face?

For much of human history, relationship formation usually required people to be physically close enough to interact.

Technology has changed that.

People can now meet through:

  • social media
  • online games
  • dating platforms
  • messaging apps
  • online communities
  • professional networks
  • shared-interest groups

Technology therefore changes the OPPORTUNITIES through which relationships can form.

People still seek many familiar social outcomes:

  • belonging
  • friendship
  • intimacy
  • support
  • identity
  • approval
  • shared interests

Technology changes HOW people can pursue these goals.

For example:

A teenager who loves a rare hobby may know nobody locally who shares the interest.

An online community can connect them with people who do.

Technology has expanded the possible SOCIAL ENVIRONMENT.

Offline relationship formation is often constrained by:

LOCATION.

You are more likely to meet people who:

  • attend your school
  • work with you
  • live nearby
  • participate in the same activities

Online environments can weaken this geographical limitation.

Someone living in Suzhou could develop a friendship with someone living thousands of kilometres away because both participate in the same online community.

The probability of meeting is no longer determined entirely by physical proximity.

Technology can also make it easier to locate people with similar:

  • interests
  • identities
  • experiences
  • values
  • goals

Example:

A student interested in an unusual form of music may struggle to find other fans at school.

Online, they can deliberately enter a community organized around that exact interest.

This can increase opportunities for interaction between psychologically similar people.

Similarity can facilitate relationship formation because shared interests or experiences provide:

  • conversation topics
  • common activities
  • mutual understanding
  • expectations of similarity

Technology can make similarity easier to detect before a relationship becomes established.

A profile, community or shared activity can provide information about another person before direct interaction develops.

Online interaction also allows people greater control over how they present themselves.

Someone can choose:

  • photographs
  • profile information
  • messages
  • what experiences to share
  • what information to hide

This is SELECTIVE SELF-PRESENTATION.

Offline interaction also involves self-presentation.

But digital environments can provide additional opportunities to edit and manage the information other people receive.

No.

Imagine someone chooses a photograph where they look particularly good.

That is selective.

But it is not necessarily false.

People manage impressions offline as well:

  • choosing clothes for a date
  • preparing what to say in an interview
  • behaving differently with friends and teachers

The psychological question is not:

“Is online self-presentation fake?”

It is:

“How does greater control over available information influence impression formation and relationships?”

Some people communicate differently online than they would face-to-face.

Reduced physical presence, perceived anonymity or psychological distance may make people more willing to:

  • disclose personal information
  • express emotions
  • discuss difficult experiences
  • behave aggressively
  • say things they would normally inhibit

This is often discussed as ONLINE DISINHIBITION.

It can have positive OR negative consequences.

Someone may find it easier to discuss:

  • anxiety
  • loneliness
  • identity
  • personal difficulties

through text than face-to-face.

Greater disclosure can sometimes help people develop closeness.

The same psychological distance may also contribute to:

  • hostility
  • impulsive comments
  • cyberbullying
  • aggressive arguments

Technology therefore changes some of the social cues and constraints surrounding interaction.

It does not guarantee better or worse behaviour.

A major mistake is treating:

“online relationships”

and:

“real relationships”

as opposites.

Many modern relationships move between both.

Example:

Two students meet through an online game.

They begin messaging.

Later they video call.

Eventually they meet in person.

They continue communicating online afterward.

Which part is the “real” relationship?

The distinction is often much less clear than it appears.

Imagine Maya joins an online community for international students.

She begins talking regularly with Sofia because they share similar experiences.

A psychological analysis could examine:

OPPORTUNITY:

Technology allowed them to meet despite geographical distance.

SIMILARITY:

Shared experiences gave them common ground.

SELF-PRESENTATION:

Both controlled what information they initially revealed.

SELF-DISCLOSURE:

Private messaging allowed increasingly personal conversation.

BELONGING:

The relationship may help satisfy a need for social connection.

Technology is therefore part of a wider psychological process rather than a complete explanation by itself.

Why is “technology causes people to form more relationships” too simplistic?
Technology can increase opportunities for interaction by reducing geographical barriers and helping people find others with shared interests or experiences. Whether a meaningful relationship actually develops depends on additional factors such as communication, similarity, trust, self-disclosure, motivation and individual circumstances.
Think of a relationship that began or developed partly through technology. What did the technology actually change about the opportunity for interaction?
Possible changes include allowing people to meet despite distance, making repeated communication easier, revealing shared interests, allowing asynchronous communication or providing a space for self-disclosure. The important question is the mechanism through which technology changed the social interaction.
Why should psychologists avoid assuming that online self-presentation is automatically fake?
People manage impressions in both online and offline settings. Digital environments can increase control over what information is presented, but selective presentation is not necessarily deception. Psychologists should investigate what is selected, why it is selected and how this influences the impressions and relationships that develop.

Do not explain relationship formation as:

TECHNOLOGY → RELATIONSHIP

A stronger psychological explanation is:

Technology changes opportunities and features of interaction ↓ this influences processes such as similarity, self-presentation, disclosure and communication ↓ these processes can contribute to relationship formation

Technology changes the CONTEXT in which familiar psychological processes operate.

HL Extension: Technology: Benefits and Risks

Is technology improving human relationships or damaging them?

This question sounds simple.

Psychologically, it is the wrong question.

Technology can strengthen some relationships, weaken others, create new opportunities and introduce new problems.

The outcome depends on:

WHAT technology is being used

HOW it is being used

WHY it is being used

WHO is using it

and

WHAT relationship context already exists.

Technology allows people to maintain regular contact despite physical separation.

Example:

A student moves overseas but continues speaking with their grandparents through:

  • video calls
  • messaging
  • shared photographs
  • voice messages

Without digital communication, contact might occur far less frequently.

Technology can therefore help maintain existing relational bonds.

Online communities can connect people experiencing similar circumstances.

Someone dealing with an unusual experience may have nobody nearby who understands it.

Online interaction can provide:

  • information
  • emotional support
  • shared experience
  • reassurance
  • belonging

This can be particularly valuable when appropriate offline support is difficult to access.

Technology can help people find communities that may not exist locally.

Examples include:

  • hobbies
  • gaming communities
  • academic interests
  • language learning
  • cultural communities
  • professional networks

The benefit is not simply “more screen time.”

It is access to particular forms of SOCIAL INTERACTION.

Digital communication can remove or reduce cues such as:

  • tone of voice
  • facial expression
  • timing within a live conversation
  • gesture
  • immediate feedback

Consider the message:

“Fine.”

It could mean:

“Everything is okay.”

or:

“I am extremely annoyed.”

Without additional cues, the receiver may rely more heavily on interpretation.

Existing schemas and expectations can therefore influence how ambiguous messages are understood.

Social platforms can expose users to carefully selected information about other people's lives.

Someone might repeatedly see:

  • holidays
  • relationships
  • achievements
  • social events
  • attractive photographs

and compare those selected moments with their own everyday experience.

This can influence perceptions such as:

“Everyone else has a better social life than me.”

However, the effect is unlikely to be identical for every person.

What users view, how they interpret it and why they use the platform all matter.

Digital platforms can make social approval highly visible through features such as:

  • likes
  • comments
  • follower counts
  • views
  • shares

This can make social feedback measurable and public.

For some users, these signals may become psychologically important.

Example:

A teenager uploads a photograph and repeatedly checks how many people have liked it.

The technology has not created the human desire for approval.

It has created a new system through which approval can be displayed and monitored.

Imagine discovering that your entire friendship group attended an event after seeing photographs online.

Social exclusion existed before social media.

But technology can make exclusion:

  • visible
  • immediate
  • repeated
  • difficult to avoid

A person may repeatedly encounter reminders of an event from which they were excluded.

Technology can therefore change the EXPERIENCE of an existing social process.

One concern is that time spent using technology could replace some forms of face-to-face interaction.

This is sometimes called DISPLACEMENT.

But the assumption:

“online time replaces relationships”

is too simplistic.

A student might spend two hours online:

CASE A:

Watching videos alone.

CASE B:

Video calling their closest friend who moved overseas.

The amount of “screen time” is identical.

The social behaviour is completely different.

This distinction demonstrates why psychologists need to examine HOW technology is used.

ACTIVE SOCIAL USE might include:

  • messaging friends
  • video calling
  • participating in group conversations
  • creating and sharing content with others

PASSIVE USE might include:

  • scrolling without interacting
  • repeatedly viewing other people's content

These behaviours may have different psychological consequences.

Simply measuring:

“HOURS ONLINE”

can hide important differences.

Suppose researchers find:

Heavy social-media users report greater loneliness.

Possible explanation 1:

Social-media use increases loneliness.

Possible explanation 2:

Lonely people use social media more.

Possible explanation 3:

Another variable influences both.

A correlation cannot by itself determine which explanation is correct.

Technology may affect people differently according to:

  • age
  • personality
  • existing relationships
  • motives for use
  • social skills
  • mental state
  • cultural context
  • type of platform
  • type of activity

This means average effects can hide important individual differences.

Imagine two students each spend three hours per day using their phones.

STUDENT A:

Video calls distant family, messages close friends and collaborates on a group project.

STUDENT B:

Mostly scrolls through photographs of other people's social lives and repeatedly compares themselves with others.

Writing:

“Both students have three hours of screen time”

tells us very little about the psychological processes involved.

Researchers need to measure the QUALITY, PURPOSE and CONTEXT of technology use.

Why is measuring only “daily screen time” often a weak way to investigate the effect of technology on relationships?
The same amount of screen time can involve very different behaviours. Video calling a close friend, participating in a supportive group and passively scrolling through strangers' posts may involve different psychological processes. Researchers need to consider what people are actually doing, why they are doing it and the relationship context.
Think about your own technology use. Name one way it could strengthen a relationship and one way the same technology could create difficulty.
For example, messaging can maintain regular contact with someone who lives far away, but ambiguous messages can also create misunderstandings because non-verbal cues are reduced. The important point is that the effect depends on how the technology is used within the relationship.
A study finds that teenagers who use social media more report greater loneliness. Give TWO explanations other than “social media causes loneliness.”
Lonelier teenagers may turn to social media more frequently, reversing the proposed causal direction. A third variable, such as existing social difficulties or limited offline support, could also contribute to both greater social-media use and greater loneliness.

HL Extension: Technology: Evaluation

Technology is deeply integrated into modern relationships.

But statements such as:

“Social media damages relationships”

or:

“Technology makes people more connected”

are usually too broad to be psychologically useful.

Evaluation requires us to ask what the evidence actually shows.

Imagine two participants both report:

3 HOURS OF PHONE USE PER DAY.

Participant A spends much of that time:

  • video calling family
  • messaging close friends
  • collaborating with classmates

Participant B spends much of that time:

  • scrolling through strangers' posts
  • comparing themselves with other people
  • watching videos alone

The numerical measure is identical.

The psychological experiences are not.

Researchers therefore need to operationalize technology use carefully.

A study could measure:

  • total screen time
  • social-media frequency
  • number of messages
  • active communication
  • passive browsing
  • number of online contacts
  • perceived online support

These variables are NOT interchangeable.

A headline claiming:

“Screen time harms relationships”

may hide important information about what participants were actually doing.

Suppose a study finds:

Greater social-media use is associated with greater loneliness.

There are several possible explanations.

POSSIBILITY 1:

Greater social-media use contributes to loneliness.

POSSIBILITY 2:

Loneliness encourages people to use social media more frequently.

POSSIBILITY 3:

A third variable influences both.

For example, existing social difficulties might contribute to both loneliness and greater online activity.

A correlational study cannot by itself determine which causal explanation is correct.

Technology research often asks participants to estimate:

“How many hours per day do you use social media?”

But people may not accurately remember their usage.

They may:

  • underestimate it
  • overestimate it
  • forget brief periods of checking
  • interpret “social media” differently

Self-report measures can therefore introduce measurement error.

Researchers can sometimes use automatically recorded information such as:

  • usage time
  • number of interactions
  • frequency of app use

This may provide more objective behavioural measurement.

However, behavioural data still does not automatically reveal psychological meaning.

Knowing that someone sent 80 messages does not tell us:

  • who received them
  • what was communicated
  • whether the interaction was supportive
  • how the participant experienced it

More precise measurement is not automatically more psychologically meaningful.

Average effects can hide major differences between people.

The same online interaction may affect individuals differently depending on:

  • personality
  • age
  • motives for use
  • existing relationships
  • previous experiences
  • social support
  • cultural context

Example:

An online community may provide valuable belonging for someone who lacks similar peers locally.

For another person, the same platform may encourage repeated social comparison.

Therefore:

THE SAME TECHNOLOGY ≠ THE SAME PSYCHOLOGICAL EFFECT

Technology rarely operates separately from offline life.

Imagine a teenager receives little support at school but develops several close online friendships.

For this person, online communication may ADD social connection.

Another teenager spends increasing amounts of time online while withdrawing from previously supportive offline friendships.

For this person, the relationship between technology and social connection may be different.

The existing social context matters.

Technology develops faster than many traditional areas of psychological research.

Platforms change.

Features change.

Social norms change.

The people using them change.

Research about one platform or form of technology may therefore become less applicable as digital behaviour changes.

Psychologists need to be cautious about generalizing findings from:

ONE PLATFORM

ONE AGE GROUP

ONE TIME PERIOD

to all technology use.

Technology can influence relationships.

Relationships can also influence technology use.

Example:

A friendship becomes closer.

↓

The friends begin messaging more frequently.

If researchers later find:

MORE MESSAGING ↔ CLOSER FRIENDSHIP

it would be incorrect to assume that messaging alone created the closeness.

The relationship itself changed the digital behaviour.

This is a BIDIRECTIONAL relationship.

Despite these challenges, studying technology has enormous ecological importance.

Digital communication is now part of everyday:

  • friendship
  • family relationships
  • romantic relationships
  • group membership
  • workplace interaction
  • social support

Psychological research that ignores technology would miss an important part of modern human relationships.

Instead of asking:

“Is social media bad for relationships?”

ask:

“How is passive social comparison on image-based social media associated with perceived friendship satisfaction among adolescents?”

Now the researcher has identified:

  • a specific behaviour
  • a psychological mechanism
  • a relationship outcome
  • a population

The question becomes much more scientifically useful.

Imagine a newspaper headline says:

“Scientists prove smartphones are destroying teenage friendships.”

Before accepting it, ask:

  • What smartphone behaviour was measured?
  • How was friendship quality measured?
  • Was the research experimental or correlational?
  • Who participated?
  • How large was the effect?
  • Could loneliness or existing relationship difficulties influence phone use?
  • Were active and passive uses distinguished?
  • Does the headline accurately represent the study?

This is psychological evaluation rather than technological panic.

Why is “screen time” often a weak variable when investigating relationships?
Screen time combines many psychologically different activities into one number. Video calling a close friend, participating in a supportive group and passively scrolling through social-media content may involve different mechanisms and relationship outcomes. Researchers should measure the specific behaviour relevant to their hypothesis.
A study finds that teenagers who message their closest friends more frequently report stronger friendships. Give TWO possible causal directions.
Frequent communication might contribute to maintaining or strengthening the friendship. Alternatively, people with stronger friendships may naturally communicate more frequently. Both directions could operate simultaneously, so correlational evidence alone cannot establish which process produced the association.
Why might a study of one social-media platform conducted ten years ago have limited generalizability to teenagers today?
Platforms, features, social norms and patterns of use can change substantially over time. The psychological processes may not disappear, but the specific digital environment in which they operate may be different. Researchers should therefore be cautious when generalizing across technologies and time periods.

Do not evaluate technology by asking:

“Is it good or bad?”

Ask:

  • WHAT behaviour was measured?
  • HOW was it measured?
  • WHY was the person using the technology?
  • WHAT psychological mechanism is proposed?
  • WHAT relationship outcome was measured?
  • WHO was studied?
  • WHAT does the research design allow us to conclude?

Technology is a context for behaviour.

Psychologists still need to explain the psychological processes occurring within that context.

QUESTION 1 — SOCIAL IDENTITY THEORY
A school divides students randomly into two groups for a competition. Within a short time, students begin saying: “We're better than them.” Which psychological theory could help explain this?
Social Identity Theory. Social categorisation creates an in-group/out-group distinction, and identification with the in-group can make group membership psychologically meaningful. Under some conditions, this can contribute to in-group favouritism or competitive intergroup behaviour.
QUESTION 2 — SOCIAL LEARNING THEORY
A younger student begins copying sarcastic comments used by older students after seeing their friends laugh and approve of them. Which process is particularly important?
Observational learning and vicarious reinforcement. The student observes a model's behaviour and sees that it receives social approval, which may increase motivation to reproduce the behaviour.
QUESTION 3 — CONFORMITY
A student privately disagrees with their friendship group's opinion but publicly agrees because they do not want to be rejected. What type of social influence is most relevant?
Normative social influence. The student conforms in order to gain acceptance or avoid social rejection rather than because they necessarily believe the group is correct.
QUESTION 4 — COMPLIANCE
Someone first asks you to sign a petition. Several days later, they ask you to volunteer for the campaign. Which compliance technique does this resemble?
Foot-in-the-door. Agreement with a smaller initial request is followed by a larger related request.
QUESTION 5 — CULTURE
A researcher concludes: “Everyone from collectivistic cultures values family obligations more than personal choice.” What is the major problem?
The claim turns a broad cultural tendency into a stereotype about individuals. There is substantial within-culture variation, and cultural dimensions should not be treated as deterministic descriptions of every person.
QUESTION 6 — ACCULTURATION
Someone maintains strong involvement with their heritage culture while also participating actively in the wider society. Which Berry acculturation strategy does this resemble?
Integration — maintaining the heritage culture while also participating in the wider society.
QUESTION 7 — COGNITION
Your friend does not reply to a message. You immediately think: “They are deliberately ignoring me.” What psychological process should you examine?
Interpretation and attribution. The observable fact is that the friend has not replied. The belief that they are deliberately ignoring you is an interpretation that may be influenced by schemas, expectations and previous experiences.
QUESTION 8 — COMMUNICATION
During an argument someone says: “You are selfish. You always make everything about yourself.” Which of Gottman's Four Horsemen does this most closely resemble?
Criticism, because the statement attacks the person's character rather than focusing specifically on the behaviour or problem.
QUESTION 9 — CHEMICAL MESSENGERS
Why is this statement too simplistic? “Oxytocin is the love hormone, so more oxytocin makes people more loving.”
Oxytocin has been associated with processes including bonding, affiliation, social recognition and trust, but its effects are context-dependent. Biological processes interact with cognitive and social factors, so oxytocin should not be treated as producing one automatic social behaviour.
QUESTION 10 — IMPROVING RELATIONSHIPS
Two hostile groups are forced to sit in the same room every day but remain unequal in status and continue competing. Why does this NOT provide a strong test of Allport's Contact Hypothesis?
Contact alone is not the complete hypothesis. Favourable conditions include equal status, common goals, cooperation and support from relevant authorities or norms. Simply placing hostile groups together may not create the conditions expected to reduce prejudice.
QUESTION 11 — MOTIVATION
A student helps organize a school event because they genuinely enjoy working with the other students. Is this primarily intrinsic or extrinsic motivation?
Intrinsic motivation, because the activity itself is experienced as enjoyable or satisfying rather than being performed primarily to obtain a separable external outcome.
QUESTION 12 — CULTURAL RESEARCH
A friendship questionnaire developed in one country is used in another culture without checking whether “friendship closeness” has the same meaning there. What methodological problem could this create?
An imposed etic. Researchers may be imposing a concept or measurement developed in one cultural context onto another without establishing that the construct has equivalent meaning.
QUESTION 13 — TECHNOLOGY
Two students both use their phones for three hours per day. One video calls friends. The other mainly scrolls passively through social-media posts. Why is “three hours of screen time” psychologically incomplete?
The same amount of screen time can contain very different behaviours and psychological processes. Researchers need to examine the type, purpose and context of technology use rather than treating all screen time as equivalent.
QUESTION 14 — CAUSALITY
A survey finds: MORE SOCIAL SUPPORT ↔ GREATER SCHOOL BELONGING. Can we conclude that social support causes school belonging?
No. The variables are associated, but the causal direction is uncertain and third variables could influence both. A correlational survey does not by itself establish causation.
QUESTION 15 — MEASUREMENT
A researcher says: “I measured friendship quality.” What should you immediately ask?
“How was friendship quality operationalized?” Friendship quality is an abstract psychological construct, so researchers must explain how it was turned into something measurable.
QUESTION 16 — EVIDENCE
Why is: “Tajfel proved that groups cause prejudice” a poor psychological conclusion?
Tajfel's minimal-group studies provide evidence that social categorisation can be sufficient to produce in-group favouritism under controlled conditions. They do not prove that all group membership causes prejudice or hostility in real-world relationships.
QUESTION 17 — PERSPECTIVE
A student changes their behaviour after joining a new friendship group. Could Social Identity Theory, Social Learning Theory and conformity ALL contribute to an explanation?
Yes. Social Identity Theory could explain identification with the group, Social Learning Theory could explain modelling and reinforcement, and conformity could explain behavioural change under group pressure. Different psychological processes can interact.
QUESTION 18 — RESEARCH METHODS
Why might a questionnaire about prejudice underestimate participants' actual negative attitudes?
Social desirability bias. Participants may provide answers they believe are socially acceptable rather than reporting their attitudes completely accurately.
QUESTION 19 — EVALUATION
What is wrong with evaluating a study only by writing: “Strength: laboratory experiment. Limitation: small sample.”
Evaluation should explain why a methodological feature matters for the specific conclusions being made. Students should connect control, sampling, measurement, ecological validity, bias and other issues directly to the evidence and psychological claim.
QUESTION 20 — THE BIG PICTURE
Someone says: “People behave differently in relationships because of their culture.” How could you turn this into a stronger psychological explanation?
Identify the specific cultural norm or value, explain the psychological mechanism through which it may influence expectations, interpretations or behaviour, consider individual and contextual variation, and support the explanation with appropriate evidence. Culture should be treated as an influence rather than a deterministic label.
