Welcome to Health & Well-being

Health and well-being are shaped by interactions between the mind, body and social environment.

Psychologists therefore study health holistically rather than assuming that mental and physical health are completely separate.

In this unit, we will investigate why health problems develop, why their prevalence may change or differ between populations, and how psychologists evaluate attempts to prevent or treat them.

Health, Mental Health and Well-being

Physical health concerns the functioning and condition of the body.

Mental health concerns psychological and emotional functioning.

Well-being is related to both, but it also includes a person’s subjective experience of how pleasant, satisfying and manageable their life feels.

This distinction matters because health can sometimes be assessed using relatively objective indicators, while well-being often depends on what the individual reports about their own experience.

A complete psychological understanding of health therefore needs to consider both measurable health outcomes and subjective experience.

Health outcomes rarely have one simple cause.

Psychologists may investigate:

  • Biological factors — such as genetics, neural processes or other physiological mechanisms.
  • Cognitive factors — such as beliefs, interpretations and patterns of thinking.
  • Environmental factors — such as stressful experiences or features of a person’s surroundings.
  • Sociocultural factors — such as social relationships, cultural expectations and population context.

These influences can interact.

For example, a stressful experience may affect people differently depending on their biological vulnerability, how they interpret the situation, the support available to them and the wider social environment.

The strongest psychological explanations therefore avoid automatically reducing a health problem to one isolated cause.

A psychological explanation attempts to identify processes that may contribute to a health problem.

Different explanations can focus on different levels.

A biological explanation might investigate vulnerability or physiological processes.

A cognitive explanation might investigate how patterns of thinking contribute.

An environmental explanation might investigate experiences or conditions associated with the problem.

A social explanation might investigate learning from other people or the influence of relationships and social environments.

These explanations do not necessarily compete.

More than one process can contribute to the same health outcome, and the importance of different influences can vary between individuals.

Prevalence refers to how common a health problem is within a particular population during a specified period.

Psychologists may ask why prevalence:

  • changes over time within a population, or
  • differs between populations.

A difference in recorded prevalence can reflect genuine differences in risk or exposure.

However, psychologists must also consider factors such as:

  • how the health problem is defined
  • how it is measured
  • access to diagnosis or healthcare
  • willingness to report symptoms
  • cultural interpretations of health and distress

A recorded difference between populations therefore needs to be explained carefully rather than automatically treated as proof that one population genuinely experiences more of the problem.

The Three Parts of This Unit

1. Mental Health Disorders

“How can psychologists understand a mental health disorder?”

Our focus: Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

We will investigate:

Biological Explanations
How biological processes may contribute to MDD.

Cognitive Models
How patterns of thinking can help us understand MDD.

Environmental Factors
How experiences and environmental conditions may contribute.

Cultural Differences
Why prevalence may differ between populations and how approaches to mental health can vary.

2. Health Problems

“How can psychologists understand a health problem?”

Our focus: Problematic Social-Media Use

We will investigate:

Prevalence
Why recorded rates may change over time or differ between populations.

Social Learning
How observing and responding to other people can contribute to health-related behaviour.

Stress and Health
How stress can contribute to problematic patterns of behaviour.

3. Prevention & Treatment

“How can health problems be prevented or treated?”

We will investigate:

Biological Treatment for MDD
How a biological treatment works and how effectively it reduces symptoms.

Psychological Treatment for MDD
How a psychological treatment works and how effectively it reduces symptoms.

Prevention or Treatment of Problematic Social-Media Use
How strategies attempt to reduce the problem and how their effectiveness can be evaluated.

Knowing that a treatment or prevention strategy exists is not enough.

Psychologists need to ask:

How is it supposed to work?

What psychological or biological process is the intervention trying to change?

Does it actually work?

What evidence shows improvement, and how was that improvement measured?

For whom does it work?

Average effectiveness does not mean every individual responds in the same way.

What can we conclude from the evidence?

Researchers must consider research design, measurement, alternative explanations and the size and consistency of the observed effect.

Throughout this unit, we will therefore study both the explanation and the effectiveness of prevention and treatment.

The Questions to Keep Asking

How can psychology explain, measure and improve health and well-being?

  1. WHAT health or well-being outcome are we trying to understand?
  2. HOW is that outcome being measured?
  3. WHAT factors could contribute to it?
  4. HOW might those factors interact?
  5. WHAT does the evidence actually allow us to conclude?
  6. HOW could the problem be prevented or treated — and how do we know whether that works?

Keep these questions in mind throughout Health & Well-being. The theories, research and interventions in this unit are tools for answering them.
