Human Development: How Do We Become Who We Are?

Developmental psychology investigates how and why behaviour and cognition change across the lifespan — and why people can follow different developmental pathways.

Same Person. Different Abilities.

Imagine the same person at three different points in development.

Age 3

A child can speak in sentences and understand that other people want different things.

But they may still struggle to understand that another person can believe something that is false.

Age 8

The same child can reason about other people’s beliefs, follow increasingly complex social rules and describe themselves using abilities, relationships and group memberships.

Age 16

The same person can think about increasingly abstract possibilities, compare themselves with peers and make more complex judgements about identity, relationships and the future.

The developmental question

What changed — and what produced that change?

Simply saying that the person ‘got older’ is not an explanation.

Developmental psychologists try to identify the biological, cognitive and sociocultural processes that could explain how these changes occur.

1. Development Is More Than Getting Bigger

Development involves changes in behaviour and cognition, but not every developmental change is the same kind of change.

Abilities Can Change

Children may become able to remember more information, use more complex language, regulate behaviour more effectively or reason about situations they could not previously understand.

Social Understanding Can Change

Development also changes how people understand relationships, social expectations, other minds and themselves.

The Form of Thinking May Change

Some theories propose that development is not simply ‘more of the same’. They argue that the way a person thinks can become qualitatively different.

For example

A younger child failing a false-belief task may not simply know fewer facts than an older child. The difference could involve how the child represents another person’s mind.

That possibility creates an important question:

Is development always gradual, or can the way people think become qualitatively different?

2. Development Has Multiple Influences

Developmental psychologists do not usually expect one factor to explain an entire developmental outcome.

Biological maturation changes what the developing brain and body are capable of doing.

Experience provides opportunities to learn and adapt.

Other people create relationships, feedback and social experiences.

Culture provides practices, expectations, values and meanings through which development occurs.

The important question is therefore often not:

‘Is development caused by biology OR environment?’

but:

How do biological, individual and sociocultural influences work together over time?

Example: Learning to Speak

Biological Maturation

The developing brain and nervous system increasingly support the perception, production and organisation of language.

→

Experience

A child needs opportunities to hear and use language. Experience provides the information from which language can develop.

→

Social & Cultural Environment

Children learn language through interaction with people who speak particular languages, respond to them and use language for culturally meaningful purposes.

What does this show?

None of these influences needs to operate alone.

Maturation can create changing capacities, while experience and social interaction provide opportunities through which those capacities develop.

Development can therefore emerge from interaction between the developing person and their environment.

3. Gradual Change or Different Stages?

Continuous Development

A continuous model describes development as gradual change over time.

For example, vocabulary might increase progressively as a child learns and uses more words.

The amount or sophistication of an ability changes, but development does not require a sudden shift into a completely different stage.

Stage Development

A stage theory proposes that development includes qualitatively different forms of functioning.

The important claim is not simply that an older child knows more.

It is that the organisation or form of thinking changes in a meaningful way.

Why does the difference matter?

Suppose a six-year-old and a twelve-year-old solve the same problem differently.

A continuous explanation might argue that the older child has gradually developed stronger knowledge or cognitive abilities.

A stage explanation might argue that the older child is using a qualitatively different form of reasoning.

The same behaviour can therefore lead to different models of how development occurs.

4. Development Happens Over Time — So Timing May Matter

The effect of an experience may depend partly on when it occurs.

The developing brain changes through maturation, but it also remains capable of changing in response to experience. This capacity for change is called neuroplasticity.

This creates an important developmental question:

Are there periods when particular experiences have an especially strong effect on development?

Critical Period

A critical-period explanation proposes that a particular experience must occur within a limited developmental window for typical development of an ability to occur.

Sensitive Period

A sensitive period is less absolute. It describes a developmental period when experience may have a particularly strong influence, while development may still remain possible later.

The important caution

Finding that early experience matters does not automatically prove that development becomes impossible after a particular age.

Psychologists need evidence before concluding that a developmental window is truly critical rather than sensitive.

What Does Development Mean?

Key concepts for understanding psychological development.

  • Development: progressive changes in abilities, behaviour, and understanding over time
    Example: a toddler babbles single words, then builds two-word phrases, then full sentences with grammar — each stage building on the last.
  • Maturation: biologically influenced developmental changes that create readiness for new abilities
    Example: changes in the nervous system, muscles and body help make sitting, standing and walking possible. Infants usually follow broadly similar developmental sequences, but the exact timing also varies with individual and environmental factors.
  • Continuity: gradual, incremental change (like a plant growing taller)
    Example: vocabulary grows steadily month by month — each new word adds to the total without changing what a word IS.
  • Qualitative change: real shifts in kind, not just amount
    Example: a preoperational child thinks flattening clay reduces its amount; a concrete-operational child knows it stays the same — different KIND of thinking.
  • Individual differences: people develop at different rates and in different ways
    Example: one toddler walks at 10 months; another at 15 months — both within normal range, both walking equally well eventually.
  • Biological: genetics, brain maturation, hormones, critical periods
    Example: genetic and hormonal processes strongly influence the timing of puberty, while factors such as nutrition, health and environment can also affect when development occurs. Culture then influences what adolescence and independence look like socially.
  • Environmental: experiences, parenting, education, adversity
    Example: two identical twins raised apart develop different vocabularies depending on the language richness of their respective homes.
  • Sociocultural: culture, language, social norms, peer groups
    Example: children raised in multigenerational households learn different social norms than those in nuclear families.

Development usually reflects interaction rather than a simple nature-versus-nurture choice. Biological maturation can create readiness or constraints, while experience and sociocultural context influence how developmental potential is expressed.

The same biological potential leads to different outcomes depending on environment and culture.

Quick check: a toddler raised bilingual reaches language milestones on the same timetable as monolingual toddlers, yet ends up with two languages. Split that outcome into maturation versus environment.
Maturation supplies the readiness timetable (brain areas for language prime on schedule). Environment fills in WHICH languages. Same biological window, different cultural input — interaction in one example.
A newborn walks earlier than another but talks later. Is that evidence AGAINST maturation timetables?
No — individual differences sit comfortably within maturation: biological timetables set typical RANGES, not identical dates, and exact timing can also be influenced by individual and environmental factors. Different systems also mature on partly independent schedules.
Which lens would you cite for: (a) puberty timing, (b) vocabulary size, (c) politeness norms?
(a) Biological/maturation; (b) environmental/learning input; (c) sociocultural norms. Most real outcomes blend all three — which is why integration earns top marks.
Two children begin walking at different ages. One student says, “That proves walking is environmental rather than maturational.” What is wrong with this conclusion?
Individual differences in timing do not disprove maturation. Biological development creates the physical and neurological readiness for walking, but the exact timing can also be influenced by health, experience, opportunity for movement and other environmental factors. The strongest developmental explanation therefore considers interaction rather than treating biology and environment as competing explanations.

Why definitions matter

IB answers reward precision: calling something “maturation” when it is learned (or vice-versa) changes which evidence you must cite.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Development = psychological change across time.
  2. Maturation = biologically influenced developmental change/readiness.
  3. Continuous development = gradual quantitative change.
  4. Qualitative change = change in the nature or organisation of thinking/behaviour.
  5. Biological, environmental and sociocultural influences interact.
  6. Individual differences mean developmental patterns are not identical for everyone.

Brain Maturation

How the brain changes during development — tested longitudinally.

Brain maturation refers to biologically guided changes in the developing brain.

Different neural systems mature at different rates. These changes can affect abilities such as attention, memory, emotional regulation and decision-making.

However, maturation does not occur separately from experience. Development reflects an interaction between biological change and the environments in which the developing brain is used.

Imagine a young child and an adolescent are both asked to ignore distractions while completing a difficult task.

The adolescent may be better able to maintain attention, plan what to do and control an immediate response.

One reason is that brain systems involved in executive functions continue to mature across childhood and adolescence.

But age alone does not determine performance. Experience, sleep, stress, motivation, education and individual differences can also affect how well each person performs.

Brain maturation therefore helps explain developmental change without implying that behaviour is controlled by biology alone.

Brain development does not occur everywhere at the same rate.

Systems involved in basic sensory and motor functions tend to mature relatively early, while some networks involved in planning, inhibition and other executive functions continue developing for much longer.

This helps psychologists understand why some psychological abilities show different developmental trajectories.

However, a difference in brain maturation should not automatically be treated as the cause of every age-related behavioural difference. Behaviour also depends on learning, experience and context.

Giedd and colleagues used repeated MRI scans to study how the brain changes across childhood and adolescence.

Their longitudinal research showed that brain development follows changing trajectories rather than simply increasing steadily in size. Different regions and tissues show different patterns of development over time.

This provides evidence that brain maturation is a prolonged and dynamic process.

A major strength of longitudinal research is that researchers can track developmental change over time rather than comparing only different age groups.

However, MRI shows patterns of brain structure; it does not by itself prove that a particular brain change caused a particular behaviour.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Brain maturation continues throughout childhood and adolescence; different regions and networks follow different developmental trajectories.
  2. Sensory/motor systems generally develop earlier, while networks involved in complex executive control show more prolonged development.
  3. Giedd et al. (1999) used longitudinal structural MRI and provided important evidence that brain structure continues changing across childhood and adolescence.
  4. Grey- and white-matter development reflects processes including synaptic refinement and myelination; developmental change is NOT simply “brain growth.”
  5. Longitudinal research measures within-person change but does not automatically establish causation.
  6. Brain maturation may contribute to adolescent behaviour, but complex behaviour emerges from interacting biological, cognitive and sociocultural influences.

Synaptogenesis & Synaptic Pruning

How the brain builds and refines its connections — counted synapse by synapse.

During development, the brain forms large numbers of connections between neurons. The formation of these connections is called synaptogenesis.

As development continues, some connections are strengthened while others are reduced or eliminated through synaptic pruning.

These processes help neural networks become organised through the interaction of biological development and experience.

More connections do not automatically mean a more effective brain.

Connections that are repeatedly used may be strengthened, while some that are used less may be reduced.

For example, repeated practice of a skill can strengthen the neural systems involved in performing it efficiently.

Pruning therefore should not be understood simply as ‘losing brain cells.’ It is part of the organisation and refinement of developing neural networks.

Brain development is shaped partly by what neural systems are repeatedly required to do.

A child who repeatedly practises language, movement, music, problem-solving or social interaction is repeatedly activating particular networks.

This does not mean that experience can produce any developmental outcome at any time. Biological maturation and developmental timing also matter.

The important idea is that brain development reflects an interaction between maturation and experience.

Huttenlocher & Dabholkar (1997) measured how synaptic density changes with age by examining human brain tissue at different points in development. They found that synaptic density in the visual cortex peaks within the first year of life and then falls as connections are pruned, while density in the prefrontal cortex peaks years later and prunes gradually through adolescence. Different regions therefore follow different developmental timetables.

This provides direct evidence that synaptic development and pruning follow organised developmental patterns — although these data cannot show how synaptic density causes particular behaviours.

The Developing Brain: Key Regions

Brain regions whose development helps explain psychological change.

The prefrontal cortex forms part of networks involved in executive functions such as planning, inhibition, working memory and decision-making. These networks continue developing across adolescence and into young adulthood.
Example: an adolescent can solve abstract maths problems yet sometimes struggle to weigh long-term consequences in a socially intense moment — the skill and the context are both part of the explanation.

The prefrontal cortex makes important contributions to executive control, but it does not single-handedly control behaviour.

The hippocampus is especially important for the formation and organisation of certain long-term memories and for spatial processing. It continues developing during childhood and adolescence and interacts with other brain regions during learning and memory.
Example: healthy development of memory networks supports the gradual accumulation of knowledge across the school years.

Prolonged or severe stress has been associated with changes in hippocampal structure and memory-related functioning. Stress hormones such as cortisol are one possible mechanism, but human evidence does not justify saying that cortisol simply “damages the hippocampus” in every stressed child.

The amygdala contributes to detecting and processing emotionally significant information, including potential threats and rewards. It interacts with prefrontal and other neural systems involved in interpreting and regulating emotional responses.
Example: a critical text can quickly produce an emotional reaction, but evaluating whether the message was really meant as criticism involves wider networks.

Brain regions do not work independently. For example, successful emotional regulation can involve communication between prefrontal regions, the amygdala and other neural systems. Memory similarly depends on interactions between the hippocampus and distributed cortical networks.

Localisation helps identify important contributions of particular structures, but reducing a complex behaviour to one region is an example of biological reductionism.

During adolescence, systems involved in emotion, reward and executive control follow different developmental trajectories. Their interaction may contribute to changes in emotional responding and risk-related decision-making, particularly in socially or emotionally intense situations.

A 15-year-old becomes extremely angry after receiving a critical message online. Someone explains, “That happened because the teenager’s amygdala is developed but the prefrontal cortex is not.” Evaluate this explanation.
The explanation identifies relevant neural systems but is too reductionist. Emotional processing and executive control involve interacting networks, and the prefrontal cortex is developing rather than simply “undeveloped.” The teenager’s interpretation of the message, previous experiences, peer context, emotional arousal and individual differences may also influence the response. Brain maturation may contribute without being the sole cause.
A brain-imaging study finds that adolescents showing greater activity in a particular region also take more risks in a laboratory task. Can researchers conclude that activity in that region CAUSED the risk-taking?
Not from an association alone. The neural measure and behaviour may be related, but correlation does not establish that one caused the other. Other neural systems, individual differences and situational factors may contribute.

Strength: brain imaging and developmental neuroscience allow researchers to examine biological changes associated with psychological development.

Limitation — Reductionism: complex behaviours cannot usually be explained by one brain structure.

Limitation — Causality: associations between brain development and behaviour do not automatically demonstrate causation.

Limitation — Individual differences: developmental patterns describe averages; individuals do not all mature at identical rates.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Prefrontal regions contribute to executive-control networks.
  2. The hippocampus is important for memory formation and spatial processing.
  3. The amygdala contributes to processing emotionally significant information.
  4. These structures work through interacting neural networks rather than independently.
  5. Different neural systems follow different developmental trajectories.
  6. Brain development can contribute to adolescent behaviour without determining it.
  7. Avoid biological reductionism and unjustified causal claims.

Neuroplasticity & Development

Why the developing brain is especially responsive to experience.

Neuroplasticity is the brain’s ability to change its organisation or functioning in response to experience.

Plasticity is especially important during development because experience can strengthen, modify and reorganise developing neural networks.

This helps explain how learning and environmental experience can contribute to developmental change.

Imagine a child learning to play the piano.

At first, coordinating both hands, reading music and controlling timing requires considerable effort.

With repeated practice, the neural systems involved are repeatedly activated and become more efficiently organised.

The child’s improving performance is therefore not simply the result of ‘getting older.’ Experience is contributing to changes in the systems supporting the skill.

This illustrates the developmental importance of neuroplasticity: experience can contribute to changes in how the brain functions.

Psychologists sometimes distinguish between two ways experience can influence development.

Experience-expectant plasticity involves experiences that developing brains normally expect to receive, such as visual input, language exposure and ordinary social interaction.

Experience-dependent plasticity involves changes associated with experiences that differ between individuals, such as learning a musical instrument or developing a specialised skill.

This distinction helps explain why some experiences are important for typical development while other experiences contribute to individual differences.

Rosenzweig, Bennett and Diamond randomly assigned young rats to housing conditions that differed in the amount of stimulation available.

Rats in enriched environments were housed in groups with toys and other opportunities for exploration and activity, while rats in less stimulating environments were housed individually with little opportunity for social interaction, exploration or play.

After the housing period, measurable brain differences were found: animals in the enriched conditions developed heavier, thicker cortex than animals in the less stimulating conditions.

These measurable differences provide evidence that experience can contribute to biological change in the brain.

Animal research provides controlled evidence that experience can influence brain development, but findings from rats cannot simply be assumed to describe complex human development in exactly the same way.

Critical and Sensitive Periods

Why timing of experience matters for development.

The Core Idea

The timing of experience can matter for development.

A critical period is a limited developmental window in which particular experience is necessary for typical development. If the required experience is absent, later recovery may be severely limited.

A sensitive period is a time when the developing system is especially responsive to particular experiences, although development may still occur outside that period.

For complex human development, evidence often supports sensitive periods more strongly than completely fixed critical periods.

Consider language development.

Young children are highly responsive to the language they hear around them. Early exposure provides repeated opportunities for developing neural systems to become organised around speech sounds, vocabulary and grammar.

Learning can still occur later, so language development is not best understood as simply switching ‘off’ after one birthday.

However, some aspects of language are generally acquired more easily during earlier development.

This illustrates the idea of a sensitive period: timing matters without making later change impossible.

The distinction depends on how strongly development depends on timing.

A strict critical-period explanation predicts that without the necessary experience during a particular window, normal development cannot later occur.

A sensitive-period explanation predicts something less absolute: experience has especially strong effects during a particular developmental period, but later experience can still produce development or recovery.

When evaluating human development, psychologists therefore ask not simply whether early experience matters, but how much later change remains possible.

The Bucharest Early Intervention Project studied children who had experienced severe early psychosocial deprivation in Romanian institutions.

Some children moved into specially created high-quality foster care, while a comparison group continued to receive institutional care. Children’s developmental outcomes in areas such as cognitive ability, language and social and emotional behaviour were compared over time.

On average, children placed into foster care at younger ages showed stronger recovery.

Substantial recovery was possible: children placed later could still improve.

Not every developmental outcome followed the same timetable, because the effects of timing differed across different areas of development.

BEIP shows why human development is better understood in terms of developmental risk and opportunity than rigid destiny.

Children exposed to severe early deprivation were at increased risk of developmental difficulties, while children placed into supportive foster care showed evidence of recovery.

Earlier intervention was often associated with better outcomes, supporting the importance of developmental timing.

But recovery was possible, and different abilities showed different patterns. This is more consistent with sensitive developmental periods and continuing plasticity than with the idea that one missed early window permanently determines the whole child.

BEIP provides unusually important evidence about early experience, but human development is complex.

Children differed in their experiences before, during and after institutional care, and different developmental outcomes were measured in different ways.

Researchers should therefore avoid turning a group-level finding into a prediction about every individual child.

The evidence supports the importance of early experience and intervention without showing that early adversity permanently determines later development.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Critical and sensitive periods describe developmental timing.
  2. Critical periods involve relatively restricted windows in particular systems.
  3. Sensitive periods involve heightened responsiveness rather than an absolute deadline.
  4. Neural plasticity changes across development.
  5. Early experience can have lasting effects without development being completely predetermined.
  6. Human evidence must be evaluated carefully because deprivation cases often contain multiple confounding variables.
  7. Timing effects are probabilistic and outcome-specific, not “before age X = normal, after age X = permanently damaged.”

Evidence for Critical/Sensitive Periods

Two very different kinds of evidence — an experiment and a case study.

Hubel and Wiesel experimentally manipulated early visual experience in kittens: one eye was deprived of patterned visual input during an early developmental window.

Later visual development was affected. The kittens showed poor vision through the deprived eye even though the eye itself was healthy, because the visual cortex had not developed normal wiring for that eye.

The timing of visual input therefore mattered for this system.

This provides strong evidence that early experience can be crucial for the development of some sensory systems. However, findings from non-human visual development should not automatically be generalised to complex human abilities such as personality, intelligence or social development.

Genie was discovered at age 13 after severe isolation and abuse during which she received virtually no language exposure. Researchers later observed rapid vocabulary growth, but her grammar never became fluent.

Genie’s severe early deprivation and later difficulties are consistent with the possibility that early experience is important for language and social development.

However, the case cannot isolate lack of language exposure from severe abuse, malnutrition, social deprivation and other developmental influences.

A single extreme case therefore cannot prove that a fixed critical period caused her later difficulties.

Evidence Check — timing evidence

Weigh experiments against case studies.

Brain maturation, neuroplasticity and developmental timing describe different but connected parts of development.

Maturation changes the biological systems available for development.

Neuroplasticity allows experience to contribute to changes in those systems.

Sensitive periods help explain why some experiences may have stronger developmental effects at particular times.

Human development therefore cannot be explained as either ‘biology’ or ‘environment.’ Biological development changes what the brain is ready to do, while experience helps shape how developing systems are organised and used.

Imagine two children begin learning a second language.

One begins during early childhood and receives frequent exposure through school, friends and everyday conversation. The other begins much later and receives only a few lessons each week.

Their eventual differences cannot automatically be attributed to age alone.

Brain maturation and developmental timing may influence how readily some aspects of language are learned, while the amount and quality of experience also matter. Continued neuroplasticity means later learning remains possible.

A strong developmental explanation therefore considers both timing and experience rather than assuming that one factor determines the outcome.

Childhood Environment & Brain Development

How early environments — especially severe deprivation — can influence development.

Development reflects an interaction between biology and environment. The brain is shaped through experience and plasticity. A useful way to think about this is as a chain:

Early environment → amount and quality of stimulation and responsive caregiving → repeated patterns of neural activation → experience-dependent strengthening and refinement of developing systems → possible differences in cognitive, emotional and social development.

This chain explains how early conditions can influence development without assuming that any one experience mechanically produces one particular brain outcome. Individual development is the product of many interacting influences.

Supportive / Enriched Environments

Supportive environments typically provide responsive caregiving, individual attention, language interaction, play and exploration, predictable relationships and opportunities for learning. These repeatedly activate developing cognitive and emotional systems in ways that can support their growth.

Severely Deprived Environments

Severe deprivation is not simply “fewer toys.” It commonly involves very limited responsive caregiving, little individual attention, low stimulation, unstable relationships, and chronic stress or neglect. For human development, responsive social and emotional interaction is especially important — its absence can mean developing systems receive much less of the experience they normally rely on.

Focus for this page: severe psychosocial deprivation provides important evidence about what can happen when children receive unusually limited responsive caregiving, social interaction and cognitive stimulation.

Children who had experienced severe early institutional deprivation in Romania were followed over time after some experienced a major improvement in caregiving through placement in specially created foster care. Researchers examined their developmental outcomes.

You have already studied this project in detail on the earlier page about sensitive periods. Here it is used as a human example of neuroplasticity rather than repeated as a full study.

The important pattern was:

  • severe early deprivation → developmental difficulties
  • but improved caregiving → meaningful developmental recovery
  • and, for several outcomes, earlier intervention → stronger developmental benefit.

Why this connects to neuroplasticity: the environment changed, and development subsequently changed. Development is therefore not simply fixed by early biological maturation. At the same time, earlier intervention often produced stronger outcomes, showing that plasticity is greater during some developmental periods than others.

Put together, the findings spell out a simple chain:

Severe deprivation → important developmental input is reduced → developing systems receive atypical experience.

Supportive foster care → richer and more responsive environmental input → developmental systems remain capable of change → recovery becomes possible.

But longer deprivation → some developmental systems may become less responsive → later recovery may be less complete for some outcomes.

This is why BEIP is useful for understanding both:

  • Neuroplasticity — development can change in response to experience.
  • Sensitive periods — the timing of that experience affects how much change occurs.

Careful: this does not mean every BEIP outcome was a direct measurement of neural structural change. Most outcomes studied were developmental (cognitive, social, emotional) rather than direct measures of brain structure, so be cautious about turning them into claims about synaptic density or specific brain regions.

A child receives responsive caregiving, language interaction and stimulation, while another experiences severe institutional deprivation. What can we defensibly predict about their development?
A child receiving responsive caregiving, language interaction and stimulation repeatedly activates developing cognitive and emotional systems, providing experience that can support their development. Severe deprivation removes much of this expected input and may disrupt development. BEIP shows that improving the environment can lead to substantial recovery, demonstrating continuing plasticity — but this comparison does not directly measure specific changes in synaptic density or pruning.
Does neuroplasticity mean a neglected child’s brain can always recover fully with later enrichment?
No — plasticity changes across development, and sensitive periods mean some systems are harder to change after early windows. Later enrichment helps but cannot guarantee full recovery of lost potential. The BEIP data support partial but not necessarily complete catch-up.

What Should I Remember?

  1. The developing brain is plastic: experience can influence development.
  2. Severe deprivation can disrupt development because children receive less of the responsive caregiving and stimulation normally available during important developmental periods.
  3. BEIP showed that development can improve substantially when the caregiving environment improves, demonstrating continuing plasticity.
  4. Timing matters: earlier intervention often produces stronger recovery for some outcomes, connecting neuroplasticity with sensitive periods.

Stage vs Continuous Development

The fundamental question about how development proceeds.

Stage and Continuous Models of Development

Psychologists disagree about the pattern that development takes.

Stage theories propose that development involves qualitatively different forms of thinking or behaviour. Development therefore involves changes in kind, not simply increases in amount.

Continuous models describe development as a more gradual process in which abilities change, accumulate or become more sophisticated over time.

The important question is not simply which description sounds better. Psychologists evaluate how effectively each model explains evidence about real human development.

A qualitative change means that development produces a different way of thinking or functioning.

For example, a stage theorist might argue that a child has developed a new form of reasoning that was not previously available in the same form.

A quantitative change means that an existing ability gradually increases, becomes faster, more accurate or more sophisticated.

For example, vocabulary can increase gradually as a child learns more words.

Real development can contain both kinds of change. This is one reason psychologists should evaluate stage and continuous models rather than assuming that all development must follow only one pattern.

Imagine two children developing mathematical ability.

One possibility is that their reasoning changes in important qualitative ways. They may eventually become capable of understanding relationships or operations that they could not previously represent in the same way.

Another possibility is that development also involves gradual increases in knowledge, practice, processing efficiency and strategy use.

The same area of development can therefore contain evidence of both qualitative change and continuous improvement.

Stage and continuous models are psychological models of developmental patterns; they do not have to describe every aspect of development perfectly.

A child says her first word around 12 months, then vocabulary grows slowly — until a sudden explosion of two-word sentences near 18 months. Stage or continuous?
Both, honestly: vocabulary accumulates gradually (continuous) while the grammar jump looks stage-like. Development often mixes modes — citing BOTH patterns earns evaluation credit.

A Different Question from Critical/Sensitive Periods

Stage vs continuous development asks HOW developmental change occurs. Critical/sensitive periods ask WHEN particular experiences have especially important developmental effects. These are different questions.

Example: a child showing gradual improvement in working-memory capacity concerns continuity of development; a child experiencing poorer visual development after early visual deprivation concerns developmental timing.

A researcher measures vocabulary size every month from age 1 to 6 and finds perfectly steady growth. Does this prove development IS continuous?
No — it shows THIS measure changed gradually. Underlying reorganisations (grammar rules, category learning) may still occur in jumps that smooth averages hide. Measure choice shapes which pattern you see.
A researcher tests children once each year and finds that performance suddenly increases between ages 6 and 7. Does this prove that children entered a new cognitive stage at age 7?
No. The result is consistent with relatively rapid developmental change, but it does not by itself prove a distinct stage. The apparent jump could depend on the task, measurement intervals, practice, schooling or other developmental changes. Researchers need evidence that thinking has changed qualitatively rather than simply improved quantitatively.
From ages 5 to 10, Maya becomes steadily faster and more accurate at solving increasingly difficult memory problems. Another student says, “She must have moved through several cognitive stages.” Is that conclusion justified?
Not necessarily. Gradual increases in speed, accuracy or capacity are more consistent with continuous quantitative development. Evidence for a stage would require a qualitative change in the organisation or type of thinking, not simply better performance.
A younger child believes a taller glass contains more water. Later, the child understands that the amount remains the same despite the change in appearance.
Piaget interpreted this as evidence of qualitative cognitive change associated with movement toward concrete operational thinking. However, researchers must still test whether the change is genuinely stage-like or whether performance develops more gradually than the task suggests.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Stage theories propose relatively distinct qualitative changes in development.
  2. Continuous models propose more gradual quantitative or incremental change.
  3. Piaget is an important example of a stage theory because he proposed changes in the organisation of children’s thinking.
  4. Stage vs continuous development concerns the PATTERN of developmental change.
  5. Critical/sensitive periods concern the TIMING and developmental importance of experience — they are not the same debate.
  6. Different psychological abilities may show different developmental patterns.
  7. Whether development appears stage-like can partly depend on how psychologists measure it.

Piaget’s Theory of Cognitive Development

How children actively construct knowledge through stages of thinking.

Piaget proposed that children actively construct knowledge as they interact with their environment.

He argued that cognitive development involves changes in the way children organise and understand information.

Piaget described development through a sequence of stages. Each stage represents a characteristically different way of thinking rather than simply an older child knowing more facts.

Piaget described mental frameworks for organising knowledge as schemas.

When new experience fits an existing schema, the child can interpret it through assimilation.

When existing schemas are not sufficient, they may need to be changed through accommodation.

Piaget proposed that children continually organise and adjust their understanding as they encounter experiences that do not fit what they already know.

Development therefore involves active construction of knowledge rather than children simply receiving information from adults.

Imagine a young child has learned that four-legged household animals are ‘dogs.’

The child sees a cow for the first time and calls it a dog. The child is trying to understand the new animal using an existing schema.

This is assimilation.

After learning that cows are a different category of animal, the child modifies their understanding.

This is accommodation.

The example illustrates Piaget’s broader idea that cognitive development involves actively organising and reorganising knowledge in response to experience.

Piaget proposed four broad stages of cognitive development.

Sensorimotor stage — approximately birth to 2 years

Infants understand the world primarily through sensory experience and physical action. An important development is object permanence: understanding that objects continue to exist when they cannot be directly perceived.

Preoperational stage — approximately 2 to 7 years

Children develop rapidly in language and symbolic thought but may still have difficulty considering perspectives or transformations that conflict with immediate appearance.

Concrete operational stage — approximately 7 to 11 years

Children become increasingly capable of logical reasoning about concrete situations. They can understand ideas such as conservation and can consider several features of a problem at once.

Formal operational stage — approximately 11 years onward

Piaget proposed that adolescents become increasingly capable of abstract and hypothetical reasoning.

The ages are approximate developmental patterns, not exact deadlines that every child must follow.

Imagine a child sees two identical glasses containing the same amount of water.

The water from one glass is then poured into a taller, thinner glass.

A younger child may say that the taller glass now contains more water because the height of the liquid has changed.

An older child may understand that the amount remains the same despite the change in appearance.

Piaget interpreted this as evidence of a developmental change in the way children reason about transformations.

The task is useful because the child must reason beyond what is immediately visible.

How does equilibration drive cognitive growth?
When experience cannot be assimilated, disequilibrium motivates accommodation — schemas rebuild until the world makes sense again, at a higher level of understanding. Learning is driven by this tension-resolution cycle.
A toddler calls every four-legged animal “doggie” until corrected at a farm. Name the processes in order.
Assimilation (horse → dog schema), disequilibrium (correction conflicts), accommodation (new “farm animals” distinctions), equilibration at a higher level.
Why might Piaget’s own-children observations over- OR under-state abilities?
Over: privileged, verbally rich infants may advance early. Under: unfamiliar testers/tasks suppress performance. Either way, n=3 limits universality — which later research exploited.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Children construct knowledge actively via schemas: assimilation vs accommodation resolves disequilibrium.
  2. Four stages: sensorimotor → preoperational → concrete → formal; logic matters more than dates.
  3. Evidence base: observations/clinical interviews incl. his own three infants — rich but unrepresentative.
  4. Legacy: qualitative difference confirmed; timing and universality challenged.

Assimilation, Accommodation & Equilibration

Interactive practice with Piaget’s core mechanisms.

Assimilation: interpret something new THROUGH existing understanding (dog schema absorbs a Great Dane).

Accommodation: new experience will not fit, so the SCHEMA changes (a separate “cat” schema forms).

Equilibration: the drive resolving that tension, upgrading thinking overall.

Scenario 1: A child who has only seen small dogs meets a Great Dane and calls it a “big dog.”
Assimilation. The Great Dane fits the existing dog schema — nothing about the schema needed to change, only scaled up.
Scenario 2: The child sees a cat, is told “that is NOT a dog,” and builds a new cat category.
Accommodation. The old schema failed; a new schema had to be constructed — genuine structural change.
Scenario 3: A student applies a physics formula confidently, gets it wrong, then reworks their whole approach.
Equilibration in action. Wrong answer created disequilibrium; re-examining the approach is the accommodating resolution.

Students often label ANY learning assimilation. Test: did the existing schema SURVIVE unchanged (assimilation) or did it have to CHANGE (accommodation)? Only the drive to resolve the mismatch is equilibration.

A child sees a zebra and says “horse with stripes.” Assimilation or accommodation?
First attempt = assimilation (zebra forced into horse schema). If told “that is a zebra, not a horse” and a zebra-schema forms — THAT was accommodation. Same moment can start one process and trigger the other.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Assimilation = fit into old schema; accommodation = schema changes; equilibration = the resolving drive.
  2. Test question: did the schema survive or change?
  3. Use concrete child examples (dog/cat, zebra/horse) to demonstrate the distinction under exam pressure.

Testing Piaget

Evidence for and against the stage theory.

Piaget’s theory was valuable because it treated children as active thinkers rather than incomplete versions of adults.

The theory drew attention to important developmental changes in reasoning and generated testable predictions about how children of different ages might approach cognitive problems.

Research supports the broader idea that children’s thinking changes substantially during development.

Piaget’s work also had practical influence on education by encouraging learning activities that allow children to explore, manipulate information and construct understanding.

Later research suggests that development does not always occur in the neat, universal stages Piaget proposed.

Children may succeed on some cognitive tasks earlier when instructions are clearer, the situation is more familiar or the task places fewer demands on memory and language.

Performance can also vary across different tasks and cultural or educational contexts.

This suggests that Piaget identified important developmental changes, but the exact stage boundaries may be less rigid than his original theory proposed.

A child can fail a psychological task for more than one reason.

They might genuinely lack the underlying cognitive ability.

But they might also misunderstand the question, forget part of the instructions, become distracted or find the task unfamiliar.

This creates an important measurement problem: does failure on a Piagetian task show what the child cannot understand, or only what the child could not demonstrate under those particular testing conditions?

Researchers therefore need to distinguish underlying cognitive competence from performance on a specific task.

Baillargeon (1987) — object expectations without search behaviour

Question: Do infants understand the continued existence of hidden objects earlier than Piaget’s sensorimotor-stage timing predicts, when the task does not require reaching or searching?

Finding: In violation-of-expectation looking-time tasks, infants aged around 3.5–6 months looked significantly longer at impossible events (a rotating screen appearing to pass through a hidden object) than at possible events.

What it means for Piaget: It supports the broader idea that infants actively represent objects, but it challenges Piaget’s timing: his search-based tasks may have hidden earlier competence behind motor and task demands.

Dasen (1994) — cross-cultural review

Question: Do children master concrete-operational abilities on the same timetable across different cultures?

Finding: In a review of cross-cultural studies, the timing and profile of concrete-operational skills (such as conservation) varied with schooling and cultural practice, with some spatial abilities appearing relatively stronger than quantity-based ones.

What it means for Piaget: The broad stages capture real qualitative changes in reasoning, but the exact timing and universality of the stages are less fixed than the original model proposed.

Piaget’s theory remains useful because it identifies important changes in children’s reasoning and provides a clear model for thinking about cognitive development.

However, development appears more variable and context-sensitive than a strict stage account suggests.

The strongest conclusion is therefore not that Piaget was simply ‘right’ or ‘wrong.’ His theory captures important qualitative changes in cognition, but the timing and organisation of those changes are less rigid than the original stage model proposed.

Commit first: WHY does measuring LOOKING TIME solve Piaget’s original testing problem with young infants?
Piaget required infants to SEARCH — but infants lack coordinated reaching and grasping, so failure may reflect motor immaturity, not ignorance. Looking needs no motor skill, which separates knowledge from performance. This is exactly a measurement point: task demands can hide competence.
A critic says infants stared because the bridge stopping looked odd, not because they understood objects. How could researchers answer this?
Control conditions: the same stop looks equally odd when NO box is present yet does not produce extra looking in variants where reasoning is impossible — plus replication with different stimuli and measures strengthens the object-permanence reading.
An infant looks longer when an object appears to pass through a solid barrier. A researcher concludes, “The infant fully understands the physical laws governing solid objects.” What is wrong with this conclusion?
Looking time is the measured behaviour; understanding is the psychological construct being inferred. Longer looking suggests that the event violated an expectation, but it does not tell researchers exactly how sophisticated that expectation is. Researchers must avoid claiming more about cognition than the measure can demonstrate.
One sentence combining Baillargeon and Dasen as balanced evaluation of Piaget.
“While Baillargeon’s drawbridge study shows core competencies emerge far earlier than Piaget claimed, Dasen’s cross-cultural review shows their TIMING varies with environment — supporting Piaget’s qualitative insight while rejecting strict universal timetables.”
Why was Baillargeon’s finding significant for Piaget’s theory?
It demonstrated object permanence months earlier using a method immune to infant motor limitations — challenging Piaget’s TIMING while confirming his deeper claim that infants actively represent objects.
A 4-year-old fails a difficult conservation task but succeeds when the same underlying idea is tested using simpler language and fewer distracting features. What does this suggest about Piaget’s stage explanation?
It suggests that task demands may partly influence apparent developmental differences. Failure on one task does not necessarily mean the child completely lacks the underlying concept. Language, attention, memory and familiarity with the task may affect performance.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Later research suggests Piaget may have underestimated some early cognitive abilities.
  2. Violation-of-expectation studies infer knowledge from looking behaviour rather than direct verbal or motor responses.
  3. Measurement method can affect conclusions about what children understand.
  4. Cross-cultural evidence challenges rigid universal timing.
  5. Evidence challenging one prediction does not invalidate an entire theory.
  6. Piaget remains important, but modern evidence supports a more flexible and gradual picture of some aspects of cognitive development.

Vygotsky’s Sociocultural Theory

How social interaction and culture shape cognitive development.

Vygotsky proposed that cognitive development is strongly shaped by social interaction and culture.

Children develop ways of thinking through participation with other people and through the cultural tools available to them, especially language.

Abilities that are first supported socially can gradually become part of the child’s independent thinking.

Imagine a child learning how to solve a difficult puzzle.

At first, an adult may ask questions, demonstrate strategies and direct the child’s attention to important features.

With experience, the child begins using some of those strategies without assistance.

Eventually, the child may talk themselves through the problem or use the strategy silently.

Vygotsky described development as a process in which socially supported ways of thinking can become internalised and used independently.

Development does not take place in a cultural vacuum.

Children learn to use tools created and valued by the communities around them.

These can include language, writing systems, number systems, diagrams, technologies and culturally developed strategies for solving problems.

Different environments therefore provide children with different experiences and tools for organising thought.

Vygotsky’s theory helps explain why cognitive development can be influenced by the social and cultural context in which a child grows.

Vygotsky viewed language as an especially important psychological tool.

Children initially use language socially to communicate with other people.

They may later use private speech — talking aloud to guide their own behaviour — while completing a difficult task.

Over time, this guidance can become increasingly internalised.

For example, a child assembling a model might initially hear an adult say, ‘Check the picture first.’ Later, the child may say the same instruction aloud to themselves and eventually use the strategy silently.

Language can therefore become a tool for organising thought and regulating behaviour.

The zone of proximal development (ZPD) describes the gap between what a learner can currently do independently and what they can accomplish with appropriate support.

Vygotsky argued that development can be promoted when assistance targets abilities that are emerging rather than tasks that are either already mastered or far beyond the learner’s current capability.

A more knowledgeable other (MKO) is someone who can provide relevant guidance. This could be a teacher, parent, peer or another person with greater expertise in the particular task.

Scaffolding refers to temporary support that helps a learner perform a task they could not yet complete independently.

For example, a teacher helping a student write an essay might initially model how to organise an argument, provide guiding questions and give frequent feedback.

As the student becomes more capable, that support can gradually be reduced.

Effective scaffolding therefore changes with the learner’s developing competence rather than doing the task for them.

Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976) — responsive tutoring

Question: How do effective tutors support young children attempting a task they cannot yet complete independently?

Finding: Effective tutoring was responsive and contingent: tutors chose the least intrusive level of help that kept the child engaged and progressing, and gradually handed control back as the child’s competence grew.

What it means for Vygotsky: The study provides an operational example of guided support within the ZPD. It introduced the term ‘scaffolding’; it did not set out to test Vygotsky’s theory, so it should not be described as proving it.

Vygotsky’s theory is valuable because it explains development as a socially supported process rather than treating cognition as something that develops entirely inside the individual.

It helps explain why instruction, collaboration, language and cultural experience can influence what children learn and how they approach problems.

The theory also has practical educational value. It suggests that effective support should respond to what the learner can almost do independently and should change as competence develops.

Vygotsky’s theory gives social and cultural processes a central role, but these processes can be difficult to measure precisely.

A learner’s zone of proximal development is not a fixed object that can be directly observed. Researchers infer it from performance under different levels of support.

The effectiveness of guidance can also depend on the learner, the task, the person providing support and the wider cultural context.

The theory therefore provides a powerful framework for understanding socially supported development, but predicting exactly how much support will produce a particular developmental outcome can be difficult.

Vygotsky did not propose a fixed sequence of universal cognitive stages like Piaget.

His theory instead emphasises ongoing development through social interaction, language, cultural tools and increasingly independent performance.

This makes Vygotsky useful when considering more continuous and context-dependent developmental change.

However, it would be too simplistic to claim that every aspect of Vygotsky’s theory represents perfectly smooth, continuous development.

For the stage-versus-continuous debate, the important contrast is that Piaget proposed a universal sequence of qualitatively distinct stages, whereas Vygotsky placed much greater emphasis on socially supported and culturally variable developmental processes.

Match-to-need is the heart of scaffolding. What would happen if a tutor always intervened at the most direct level (doing the task for the child) with a capable 5-year-old?
Disengagement and a lost learning opportunity: the child never exercises the target skill. Contingency cuts both ways — too little support frustrates, too much removes the challenge the learner is ready for.

Piaget: the lone explorer whose development LEADS what they can learn (discovery after readiness). Vygotsky: learning WITH guidance LEADS development — the ZPD means assistance pulls cognition forward before maturation alone would allow it.

No. Scaffolding involves temporary, responsive support that enables the learner to perform increasingly independently. Simply supplying the answer may complete the task without developing the learner’s ability. Effective support would provide prompts, modelling or guidance and then reduce assistance as Leo becomes more capable.

Her ZPD lies approximately between the tasks she can already perform independently and the more difficult tasks she can successfully complete with appropriate assistance — in this example, around Levels 4–5. Level 8 currently lies beyond her demonstrated ZPD.

  1. Vygotsky emphasised social and cultural influences on cognitive development.
  2. Cultural tools — especially language — shape thinking.
  3. ZPD = what a learner can accomplish with assistance beyond current independent performance.
  4. An MKO possesses task-relevant greater knowledge or skill.

Piaget vs Vygotsky

A genuine comparison of two foundational theories.

Both Piaget and Vygotsky viewed children as active participants in development, but they emphasised different processes.

Piaget focused strongly on how children construct understanding through interaction with the world and proposed a sequence of qualitatively different stages.

Vygotsky focused more strongly on how cognition develops through social interaction, language and culturally organised activity.

Piaget therefore provides a clearer stage model, while Vygotsky provides a more context-sensitive account of development through continuing social participation.

Neither model needs to explain every aspect of development in order to be useful.

Effectiveness should be judged by what a model helps psychologists explain and how well its predictions fit evidence.

Piaget is useful for identifying important developmental changes in reasoning, but the timing and rigidity of his stages have been challenged.

Vygotsky is useful for explaining the role of social interaction, language and culture, but concepts such as the ZPD can be difficult to define and measure precisely.

The models therefore illuminate different aspects of development. Evidence can be used to evaluate where each explanation works well and where it becomes incomplete.

Sociocultural Factors in Development

How culture, language and social context shape development — through mechanisms, not just labels.

  • Caregiving: responsive interaction and co-regulation can help children gradually develop emotional and behavioural regulation
    Example: a caregiver calmly naming a child’s feelings while helping them soothe models how to label and re-regulate emotion.
  • Language & cultural tools: language and culturally transmitted systems such as counting, writing and symbols provide tools that children can internalise and use in thinking
    Example: finger counting and written numerals are cultural tools that support arithmetic before strategies become internalised.
  • Social norms: children learn culturally expected behaviours through modelling, reinforcement, correction and participation in social practices
    Example: greetings, turn-taking and politeness conventions are learned through everyday participation rather than being simply told.
  • Peers: peers provide opportunities for modelling, social comparison, cooperation, conflict resolution and identity development
    Example: negotiating rules during group games requires coordinating with others and practising conflict resolution.
  • Education: instruction can provide structured guidance, cultural knowledge and opportunities to practise cognitive strategies
    Example: schooling teaches literacy, abstract reasoning and formal testing conventions.

A mechanism explains HOW a factor influences development. Naming a sociocultural factor without its mechanism — “the environment matters” — explains nothing at HL.

Development emerges through interaction between biological characteristics and sociocultural experience.

Children with broadly similar developmental capacities may develop differently when they experience different languages, caregiving practices, educational opportunities, peer groups and cultural expectations.

Biological maturation creates developmental possibilities and constraints, while sociocultural experiences influence how abilities are learned, expressed and developed.

What Is Theory of Mind?

The ability to understand that others have mental states different from your own.

Theory of Mind

Theory of mind (ToM) is the ability to understand that people have mental states — such as beliefs, desires, intentions and knowledge — that influence their behaviour.

Crucially, another person’s mental state may be different from our own and may even be different from reality.

Theory of mind therefore helps us interpret and predict behaviour by considering what another person thinks, knows, wants or believes.

Imagine Maya places her phone on the kitchen table and then leaves the room.

While she is away, her brother moves the phone to the sofa.

You know that the phone is now on the sofa.

But if Maya returns and wants her phone, you can predict that she will probably look on the kitchen table first.

Why?

You can separate what you know from what Maya believes.

Understanding behaviour from another person’s mental state rather than from reality alone is a central feature of theory of mind.

Theory of mind is important because human behaviour is often easier to understand when we consider mental states.

For example, understanding another person’s beliefs and intentions can help a child explain why someone acted in a particular way, predict what they may do next and recognise misunderstandings.

Theory of mind therefore contributes to social cognition — the psychological processes involved in understanding other people and social situations.

As children become better able to distinguish their own knowledge from another person’s perspective, their understanding of social behaviour becomes more sophisticated.

Simple example: you know that a toy has been moved from a drawer to a cupboard. Your friend did not see it being moved. What does ToM allow you to understand?
That your friend can hold a false belief and still think the toy is in the drawer — so your friend is likely to look there. This is exactly the kind of understanding tested by false-belief tasks.
Apply it: a friend hides your phone as a prank and rings it. You look under the cushion where YOU left it — wrong place. What just happened?
Your friend represented YOUR false belief about location in order to predict your search — their model of your belief differed from reality. This shows how understanding beliefs can make strategic deception possible; the prank works precisely because your belief, not reality, guided your behaviour.
Ben understands that his friend falsely believes a test is on Friday when it is actually on Thursday. Ben does not feel concerned about his friend. Does this mean Ben lacks Theory of Mind?
Not necessarily. Ben correctly represents his friend’s false belief, which demonstrates a ToM-related ability. His lack of emotional concern concerns empathy or motivation rather than necessarily showing an inability to represent another person’s mental state.
A 3-year-old fails a false-belief task. Can we conclude that the child has no understanding whatsoever of other minds?
No. Failure on one explicit task does not establish the complete absence of Theory of Mind. Performance may depend on language comprehension, memory, inhibition, attention and the specific demands of the task. Theory of Mind also contains multiple aspects that develop over time.

  1. Theory of Mind involves representing mental states.
  2. People can hold beliefs that differ from reality and from our own beliefs.
  3. Explicit false-belief performance typically improves during early childhood.
  4. ToM is related to, but distinct from, empathy.
  5. ToM contributes to social understanding but does not single-handedly determine social behaviour.
  6. Performance on one task should not automatically be treated as a complete measure of a person’s social understanding.

False-Belief Tasks & Theory of Mind Research

How psychologists test whether children understand that others can be wrong.

A particularly important test of theory of mind asks whether a child understands that another person can hold a false belief.

To succeed, the child must separate three things:

  • what is actually true
  • what the child knows
  • what another person believes

If the child predicts behaviour using the other person’s mistaken belief rather than reality, this provides evidence that the child can represent another person’s mental state.

Suppose Daniel puts a toy inside a blue box and leaves the room.

While Daniel is away, another child moves the toy into a red box.

Daniel does not see this happen.

When Daniel returns, where will he look for the toy first?

The toy is really in the red box, but Daniel believes it is still in the blue box.

Predicting that Daniel will look in the blue box requires the child to answer according to Daniel’s belief, not according to what the child knows is actually true.

Success on a false-belief task provides evidence that the child can distinguish another person’s belief from reality and from their own knowledge.

That is important evidence for theory of mind.

However, theory of mind is broader than one false-belief question.

Understanding desires, intentions, knowledge, emotions and more complex social situations also involves reasoning about mental states.

Psychologists should therefore avoid treating performance on one task as a complete measurement of a person’s entire social understanding.

The study: children heard a false-belief story in which Maxi puts chocolate in a green cupboard and leaves; while he is away, his mother moves it to a blue cupboard. The child was asked where Maxi will look.

Finding: most younger children (around 4 years) answered according to where the chocolate really was, whereas most older children (around 5–6 years) answered according to what Maxi believed — an age-related developmental pattern.

The findings support the idea that children become increasingly able to represent beliefs that differ from reality.

However, performance on the task may depend on more than theory of mind alone. Children also need to understand the question, remember what happened and resist answering according to what they themselves know.

The study (Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith, 1985): participants watched a false-belief scenario in which Sally puts a marble in her basket and leaves; Anne moves the marble to a box. The child was asked where Sally will look for the marble first.

Success: predicting that Sally will look in the basket — answering according to Sally’s belief rather than according to where the marble actually is. The study was used to investigate theory-of-mind reasoning.

Finding: the autistic group was substantially less likely than the comparison groups (typically developing children and children with Down syndrome) to predict the character’s behaviour from her false belief.

Performance differences can provide evidence about theory-of-mind reasoning, but one false-belief task should not be treated as a complete measure of a person’s social cognition.

Theory of mind involves several cognitive processes working together.

A person must represent information about another person’s mental state, keep relevant information in mind and avoid automatically answering from their own knowledge.

For example, in a false-belief task the child knows where an object really is but must suppress that knowledge when predicting where another person will search.

This helps explain why theory-of-mind performance can develop alongside abilities such as language, memory and executive control.

It also means that poor performance on a theory-of-mind task does not automatically show that the person lacks all understanding of other minds.

Theory of mind allows behaviour to be interpreted in terms of mental states rather than physical events alone.

Suppose a student walks past a friend without saying hello.

One explanation is simply behavioural: the student did not respond.

A theory-of-mind explanation considers possibilities such as the student not seeing the friend, mistakenly believing the friend was someone else, being distracted or intending to avoid a conversation.

This does not mean we can know another person’s mental state with certainty.

It means that representing beliefs, knowledge and intentions allows more sophisticated predictions and explanations of social behaviour.

Leo’s performance can be explained through interacting developmental processes rather than simply through biological maturation. His puzzle performance fits Vygotsky’s concept of the Zone of Proximal Development because he cannot initially complete the harder task independently but can do so with support from a more knowledgeable peer. What Is the Self?

Introducing self-concept and how it develops.

  • Self-concept: beliefs and knowledge about who you are, including characteristics, abilities, roles and values
    Example: “I am hardworking, curious, a good friend, and I want to study psychology.”
  • Identity: an organised sense of who you are that can include personal characteristics, roles and group memberships
    Example: “I am a student, a swimmer, British, and a big sister.”
  • Self-esteem: the evaluative component of the self — how positively or negatively someone evaluates themselves
    Example: feeling proud after scoring well OR inadequate after social rejection.
  • Social identity: the part of self-concept connected to membership of social groups
    Example: identifying strongly as “a member of the debate team” shapes behaviour and self-view.

Self-concept develops through interaction between individual characteristics and experience. Relationships, culture, social feedback and personal reflection all contribute to how people understand themselves.

Biological characteristics and temperament can influence developmental experiences, but the content of self-concept develops substantially through social and cultural interaction.

  • Attachment → emotional security, relationship expectations
  • Enculturation → norms, values, cultural identity
  • Peers → comparison, belonging, exploration
  • Childhood experiences → early events shaping self-understanding

These are important influences explored in the following pages, not forces that completely determine the self. Their effects are shaped by individual characteristics, other experiences and the surrounding context.

Why is self-esteem only ONE component of the self?
Self-esteem evaluates worth; self-CONCEPT describes who you believe you are (roles, traits, identities). “I am good at mathematics” is a self-concept belief. “I feel proud of being good at mathematics” is the evaluative / self-esteem component. You can hold accurate self-knowledge with low esteem, or high esteem built on distorted self-knowledge — concept and evaluation are separable.
A teenager moves to a new country and begins changing how they speak, dress and describe themselves while also maintaining important aspects of their original cultural identity. Which developmental processes could be influencing the self?
Several processes may interact. Earlier enculturation shaped the teenager’s existing cultural knowledge and identity, while acculturation involves adapting to contact with a new cultural environment. Peer feedback, family relationships, language, social comparison and group belonging may also influence self-concept. We should not assume that one factor automatically “dominates.”
A student receives a poor exam result and says, “I failed one test, so I am an unintelligent person.” What distinction about the self is useful here?
One experience can influence self-evaluation, but it does not automatically define the person’s entire self-concept or identity. Self-concept contains multiple beliefs, roles and characteristics, and self-esteem concerns evaluation of worth. The example also shows how experiences can influence self-perception without completely determining it.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Self-concept = beliefs and knowledge about oneself.
  2. Identity includes personal and social aspects of who someone understands themselves to be.
  3. Self-esteem = evaluation of self-worth.
  4. Social identity concerns self-definition through group membership.
  5. The self develops through interaction among individual characteristics, relationships, culture and experience.
  6. Attachment, enculturation/acculturation, peers and childhood experiences can influence self-development without individually determining it.

Attachment and the Development of Self

Attachment is an emotional bond between a child and caregiver.

For human development, the important question is not simply whether a child becomes attached. Early attachment relationships provide repeated experiences of how caregivers respond to the child’s needs.

These experiences may contribute to developing expectations about the self, other people and relationships.

Attachment can therefore influence the development of self without permanently determining the person a child will become.

Imagine two-year-old Leo becomes frightened in an unfamiliar room.

He moves toward his caregiver, who responds calmly and comforts him. Once reassured, Leo begins exploring the room again.

Repeated experiences like this can teach more than ‘my caregiver comforts me.’

Leo may gradually develop expectations such as:

‘I can seek help when I need it.’

‘Other people can respond to me.’

‘It is safe for me to explore.’

These expectations can contribute to how Leo experiences himself and relationships as he develops.

This does not mean one comforting interaction creates a particular personality. Development emerges from patterns of experience over time.

Attachment relationships can contribute to development through several connected processes.

A responsive caregiver can provide emotional security, allowing the child to regulate distress with support.

The caregiver can also provide a secure base from which the child explores unfamiliar situations.

Exploration creates opportunities for the child to act independently, solve problems, interact with other people and learn what they can do.

Repeated interactions with caregivers may also contribute to expectations about whether the child is worthy of care and whether other people are likely to be available and responsive.

Attachment can therefore contribute to both self-related expectations and expectations about relationships with other people.

Attachment is one influence on the development of self, not a fixed developmental programme.

Later relationships, friendships, family changes, culture, school experiences and other important life experiences can reinforce or change earlier expectations.

A child with difficult early attachment experiences is therefore not destined to develop low self-worth or unhealthy relationships.

Likewise, secure attachment does not guarantee positive outcomes in every area of later development.

The strongest developmental conclusion is that early attachment experiences can influence developmental pathways while later experience continues to matter.

A caregiver can function as a secure base: a source of safety from which the child explores and to which the child can return when distressed.

Exploration matters for development of self because it creates opportunities for the child to act on the environment, solve problems, interact with other people and experience increasing independence.

For example, a toddler at a playgroup may repeatedly move away from a caregiver to investigate toys, return briefly for reassurance and then explore again.

The attachment relationship does not directly create abilities or confidence.

Instead, emotional security may provide conditions that support exploration and experiences through which the child learns more about themselves and their environment.

Children also receive social feedback from caregivers. Repeated feedback about their behaviour, abilities and value can contribute to developing self-perceptions.

Caregiver feedback does not completely create self-worth: peers, teachers, culture and later experience also contribute to how children come to see themselves.

A toddler falls, looks toward a caregiver, receives reassurance and then returns to exploring. What can we reasonably infer?
The behaviour is consistent with the caregiver functioning as a secure base in that situation. Repeated experiences of responsive support may contribute to expectations that help is available and may support confident exploration. However, one observation cannot establish the child’s attachment classification, self-concept or future personality.
A child experiences inconsistent caregiving in early childhood. A psychologist predicts that the child will definitely become insecure, distrustful and attention-seeking as a teenager. What is wrong with this prediction?
Attachment theory suggests that repeated caregiving experiences may influence expectations about self and relationships, but developmental outcomes are probabilistic. Temperament, later caregiving, friendships, culture, life experiences and supportive relationships can all influence development. Early experience can matter without determining a fixed future personality.
A study finds that securely attached children later report higher self-esteem. Can researchers conclude that secure attachment CAUSED the higher self-esteem?
Not from an association alone. Attachment and later self-esteem may be related, but other factors such as caregiver behaviour, temperament, family environment, peer relationships and socioeconomic conditions could influence both. Strong causal claims require designs that address alternative explanations.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Attachment is an emotional bond with a caregiver; it is not itself a personality type or measure of self-worth.
  2. Responsive caregiving may contribute to expectations that others are available and support is effective.
  3. Bowlby called developing expectations about self and relationships an internal working model.
  4. A secure base may support exploration, giving children opportunities to develop skills and self-knowledge.
  5. Attachment may contribute to later self-development, but relationships are probabilistic rather than deterministic.
  6. Temperament, culture, peers, later relationships and life experiences also influence the developing self.
  7. Correlation between attachment and later outcomes does not automatically establish causation.
  8. Internal working models can change — early experience matters without making later development inevitable.

Bowlby’s Attachment Theory

Attachment as an evolved behavioural system that may influence later relationships.

Bowlby proposed that repeated attachment experiences contribute to an internal working model — mental expectations about the self, other people and relationships.

For example, when a caregiver responds consistently to distress, the child repeatedly experiences that their needs can matter and that another person can be available.

These experiences may contribute to expectations such as:

‘I am someone worth responding to.’

‘Other people can be trusted to help.’

Relationships therefore provide information that can become part of the child’s developing understanding of themselves and other people.

The internal working model is useful for explaining HOW attachment could contribute to development of self rather than simply claiming that early attachment predicts later behaviour.

Amelia repeatedly experiences a caregiver who notices when she is distressed, comforts her and encourages her to explore.

As Amelia grows older, she begins nursery and encounters unfamiliar adults and children.

Her earlier attachment experiences do not mechanically determine what she will do.

However, expectations that other people can be responsive and that she can seek support may influence how confidently she approaches these new relationships.

The internal working model therefore describes a possible developmental mechanism linking repeated early relationships with later expectations about the self and others.

The internal working model is useful because it provides a psychological mechanism linking early attachment experience with later expectations about the self and relationships.

It also produces testable predictions: if attachment experiences contribute to these expectations, researchers should find some relationship between earlier attachment and later social or self-related outcomes.

However, the internal working model cannot be observed directly. Psychologists infer it from behaviour, interviews or other measures.

Later experiences can also modify relationship expectations.

Evidence that early attachment is associated with a later outcome therefore supports developmental influence, but it does not show that attachment permanently caused that outcome.

Separation — physical absence from the caregiver.

Deprivation — loss or disruption of an attachment relationship without adequate substitute emotional care.

Separation does not automatically equal emotional deprivation. A child may be physically separated from one caregiver while receiving warm, responsive substitute care from someone else.

Research question: was prolonged early separation from the mother associated with later delinquency and with the pattern Bowlby called “affectionless” (apparent lack of affection, guilt or empathy)?

Method: 44 adolescent thieves referred to a London child-guidance clinic were compared with 44 matched controls — emotionally disturbed but non-delinquent adolescents. Interviews plus school and social-worker records were used to reconstruct each adolescent’s history of separation from the mother for longer than six months before age five.

Finding: Bowlby classified 14 of the 44 thieves as showing “affectionless” characteristics, and 12 of those 14 had histories of prolonged early separation. Prolonged early separation was reported in only a small minority of the control adolescents.

Contribution to the attachment–self question: the study reports an association between early caregiving disruption and later behaviour, while also showing why caution is needed: the histories were retrospective and assessed by the researcher holding the theory, the design is correlational rather than causal, and separation is not automatically the same as deprivation.

Strength: Bowlby systematically investigated developmental histories and drew attention to possible long-term consequences of early caregiving disruption.

Limitation: the theory emphasises the mother figure; fathers, other caregivers and wider relationships matter too.

Delinquent/affectionless adolescents should show far more prolonged early separations than controls — exactly what he reported (12 of 14 vs 2 of 44).
Why was Bowlby’s comparison group useful, but still insufficient for establishing causation?
The comparison showed that Bowlby’s reported pattern was not simply present in every young person attending the clinic. However, participants were not randomly assigned to early caregiving experiences, and the groups could differ in many other ways. The comparison strengthens the observed association but does not establish that separation caused later behaviour.

Second, separation from a mother is not automatically the same as attachment deprivation because children may receive responsive substitute care. Expectations about the self, other people and relationships proposed by Bowlby to develop partly through caregiving experience. Internal working models may influence later social relationships, but they are not fixed for life — later relationships and experiences may reinforce or modify them.

  1. Bowlby proposed attachment as an evolved behavioural system.

Ainsworth’s Strange Situation

A standardised observation used to classify patterns of attachment behaviour.

Ainsworth and colleagues developed the Strange Situation, a structured observation used to investigate patterns of infant attachment behaviour.

The procedure places an infant in a mildly unfamiliar situation involving the caregiver, a stranger, separation and reunion.

Researchers observe behaviour such as:

  • exploration
  • response to separation
  • response to the stranger
  • behaviour when the caregiver returns

The important psychological point is that attachment is not measured directly. Researchers observe behaviour in a standardised situation and use that behaviour to infer an attachment pattern.

Researchers observed three main patterns of infant attachment behaviour in the Strange Situation.

Secure: the caregiver functions as a secure base; distress may occur during separation; contact or reassurance is generally accepted on reunion.

Avoidant: relatively little overt distress or proximity-seeking is shown, including avoidance or limited contact on reunion.

Resistant / ambivalent: strong distress may occur and the infant may seek contact while also resisting or remaining difficult to soothe.

Disorganised: contradictory, disoriented or apprehensive behaviour; identified as a later additional classification and not part of Ainsworth’s original three-category system.

These classifications describe patterns of behaviour observed under particular conditions, not fixed personality descriptions.

The Strange Situation does not directly measure a child’s self-concept.

Its importance here is that it provides a systematic way of studying patterns in attachment relationships.

Psychologists can then investigate whether these early relationship patterns are associated with later expectations, exploration, social behaviour or self-related development.

This distinction matters: the attachment classification is the observed pattern; the developmental explanation concerns how repeated relationship experiences may contribute to the developing self.

Researchers therefore need evidence connecting the two rather than assuming that an attachment classification automatically becomes a personality type.

Ainsworth’s Baltimore research involved extensive naturalistic home observations of caregiver–infant interaction. The Strange Situation was later used as a standardised laboratory assessment of attachment behaviour.

HOME OBSERVATION: caregiver sensitivity in everyday interaction

STRANGE SITUATION: standardised attachment-related behaviour under mild stress

The Strange Situation was developed within a particular cultural context. The meaning of separation, stranger interaction and independence can differ across cultures.

Strengths: the standardised episodes allow systematic comparison across studies, and can improve reliability and replication.

Limitation — artificiality and validity: the procedure is a brief, unfamiliar laboratory situation; behaviour there may not fully capture an enduring relationship.

US distributions, not universal norms: in Ainsworth’s original US samples, secure attachment was the most common classification, with smaller proportions classified as avoidant or resistant. These percentages should not be taught as universal human attachment frequencies.

Temperament: temperament provides a plausible alternative or contributing explanation because infants differ biologically in emotional reactivity and behavioural inhibition. However, associations between caregiver sensitivity and Strange Situation classifications support the view that attachment behaviour also reflects characteristics of the caregiver–infant relationship. Modern developmental explanations can consider temperament and caregiving as interacting rather than mutually exclusive explanations.

Which episode carries the most diagnostic weight, and why?
The reunion episodes. Separation distress varies for many reasons, but how a child responds to the returning caregiver — comfort-seeking vs avoidance vs ambivalence — best discriminates the attachment pattern.
Why did Ainsworth carry out naturalistic home observations as well as the laboratory procedure?
To develop measures of caregiver sensitivity in everyday interaction. Home observation and the Strange Situation measure related but different things, and associations between them supported the idea that the laboratory procedure captured something genuine about the caregiver–infant relationship rather than a one-off reaction to the artificial setting.
Could an infant’s temperament explain a resistant-looking classification?
Temperament provides a plausible alternative or contributing explanation because infants differ biologically in emotional reactivity and behavioural inhibition. However, associations between caregiver sensitivity and Strange Situation classifications support the view that attachment behaviour also reflects characteristics of the caregiver–infant relationship. Modern accounts treat temperament and caregiving as interacting rather than mutually exclusive explanations.
A researcher observes that an infant shows little distress when the caregiver leaves. Can the researcher immediately classify the infant as avoidant?
No. Attachment classification is based on a pattern of behaviour across the procedure, with reunion behaviour especially important. Low separation distress alone could reflect temperament, familiarity with separation or other factors.
The Strange Situation lasts only a short time in an unfamiliar laboratory. Why might this create a construct-validity problem?
Researchers want to measure the quality of an enduring attachment relationship, but they operationalise it using behaviour during a brief artificial situation. If laboratory behaviour is strongly influenced by temperament, unfamiliarity or cultural expectations about separation, the procedure may not measure attachment in exactly the same way for every infant.

What Should I Remember?

  1. The Strange Situation is a standardised observation of attachment-related behaviour.
  2. Researchers infer attachment classifications from observed behaviour.
  3. The eight episodes involve exploration, stranger contact, separation and reunion.
  4. Reunion behaviour is particularly informative.
  5. The original classifications were secure, avoidant and resistant/ambivalent; disorganised was added later.
  6. Standardisation improves reliability and replication.
  7. Artificiality, temperament and culture raise validity questions.
  8. Behavioural measures should not automatically be equated with the underlying psychological construct.

Attachment & Research Evidence

Evaluating key studies that support, challenge and extend attachment theory.

Research question: does attachment form around feeding (drive-reduction learning) or contact comfort?

Method: 8 newborn rhesus monkeys were raised with two surrogate “mothers”: a wire-mesh surrogate that dispensed milk and a soft cloth surrogate that did not. Time spent with each surrogate was measured daily, and a frightening mechanical toy was introduced to test refuge preference.

Finding: the monkeys spent far more time clinging to the cloth surrogate (around 17–18 hours a day) than to the wire surrogate (under 1 hour), even though only the wire provided milk. When frightened they ran to the cloth surrogate and then ventured out and returned — secure-base-like behaviour.

Contribution to the attachment–self question: the findings challenge simple feeding-based explanations and show that contact comfort strongly influences clinging and refuge behaviour — evidence that attachment involves more than feeding.

Limitation and caution: findings come from monkeys raised under highly artificial conditions, so generalisation to human attachment requires caution. Monkeys raised under severe social deprivation later showed serious social and behavioural difficulties; the procedures caused severe and lasting distress and raise major contemporary animal-welfare concerns.

Lorenz’s observations of imprinting in greylag geese demonstrated biologically prepared following behaviour that develops rapidly during an early sensitive period.

Imprinting is not equivalent to human attachment. It provides comparative evidence that some social behaviours can be biologically prepared and sensitive to developmental timing.

Research question: how similar are Strange Situation attachment classifications across different cultural samples?

Method: meta-analysis of published Strange Situation results: 32 studies from 8 countries, pooling approximately 2,000 infant classifications.

Finding: secure attachment was the most common classification across the samples, but within-country variation was substantial; for example, some German samples showed relatively more avoidant classifications and some Japanese samples relatively more resistant classifications.

Contribution to the attachment–self question: attachment behaviour can vary across contexts; culturally different caregiving practices and experiences with separation, along with the meaning and validity of the measure itself, may influence behaviour. A classification system developed in one cultural context should not automatically be assumed to have identical meaning everywhere.

Limitation: the meta-analysis cannot establish exactly why the distributions differ; cultural practices were proposed as possible explanations rather than demonstrated causes.

Harlow: what functions besides feeding may contribute to attachment behaviour?

Lorenz: can some early social behaviours show biological preparedness and sensitivity to timing?

Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg: how similar are Strange Situation classifications across cultural samples, and does the same measure mean the same thing everywhere?

Together these studies provide different kinds of evidence. None alone proves a complete theory of human attachment.

Attachment classifications describe patterns of behaviour within attachment relationships.

They should not be treated as fixed personality labels.

A child classified as securely attached is not guaranteed to become confident, sociable or psychologically healthy.

Likewise, an insecure attachment classification does not mean a child will inevitably develop low self-esteem or relationship problems.

Development continues through later family relationships, peers, culture, education and other experiences.

Attachment is therefore best understood as one developmental influence on the self rather than a permanent identity assigned in infancy.

An association between early attachment and later development does not show that attachment alone caused the later outcome.

Caregiving, temperament, family circumstances, later relationships and other experiences may contribute to both attachment behaviour and later development.

The evidence can therefore support attachment as a developmental influence without showing that early attachment determines the later self.

Harlow’s monkeys spent far more time with the cloth surrogate even when the wire surrogate provided milk. What conclusion is justified?
The finding strongly challenges a simple drive-reduction account in which attachment preference is explained primarily by feeding. Contact comfort clearly influenced surrogate preference. However, the study does not establish that feeding has no role in natural caregiver–infant attachment.
Harlow used an experiment, Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg used meta-analysis, and Bowlby used retrospective correlational evidence. Which is the “best” method?
There is no universally best method. Harlow’s experimental control strengthens causal inference about the manipulated conditions but has major ethical and generalisability limitations. Meta-analysis can identify patterns across many studies but inherits weaknesses and cultural biases from the included research. Bowlby’s retrospective evidence provides information about real developmental histories but cannot establish causation. Method quality depends on the research question and the conclusions being drawn.
Some German samples in the meta-analysis showed relatively more avoidant classifications. Does this mean German parents are coldly rejecting?
No. One proposed interpretation is that caregiving practices in some German samples value independence, and differences in separation experience and familiarity with the procedure could also affect behaviour. These are possible explanations rather than established causes — the meta-analysis itself cannot establish exactly why the distributions differ.
Japanese infants in some samples show more resistant classifications than US infants. A researcher concludes that Japanese infants have poorer-quality attachments. What is wrong with this conclusion?
The Strange Situation was developed within a US cultural context, and separation may be less familiar or more stressful for infants from some caregiving environments. Different behaviour in the procedure may therefore partly reflect cultural experience and measurement validity rather than poorer attachment quality. The result requires interpretation rather than assuming one cultural pattern is the standard.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Harlow challenged simple feeding-based explanations of attachment using rhesus monkeys.
  2. Contact comfort strongly influenced surrogate preference and refuge behaviour.
  3. Severe social deprivation produced later difficulties, but animal findings require cautious generalisation to humans.
  4. Lorenz demonstrated early imprinting in birds, providing comparative evidence for biological preparedness and developmental timing.
  5. Imprinting is NOT equivalent to human attachment.
  6. Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg found secure attachment was the most common classification across the samples they analysed.
  7. Within-country variation was substantial, challenging simplistic assumptions that a nation has one attachment style.
  8. Cultural differences may partly reflect caregiving practices AND how the Strange Situation functions as a measure.
  9. Different research methods answer different questions and have different strengths and limitations.
  10. Strong HL evaluation asks: “What exactly does this evidence allow us to conclude?”

Enculturation

How culture shapes the developing self.

Enculturation of Social Norms

Enculturation is the process through which people learn the values, beliefs, behaviours and social norms of their cultural environment.

Social norms are shared expectations about behaviour within a group or culture — what people are generally expected to do, avoid or value in particular situations.

Children encounter these expectations through everyday participation in family, school, friendships, communities and other cultural settings.

Over time, learned norms can contribute to the developing self by influencing how children understand appropriate behaviour, relationships, roles and personal goals.

Imagine Mei grows up in an environment where children are repeatedly encouraged to consider how their behaviour affects the family.

She sees adults praise children for helping relatives, sharing responsibilities and considering other family members when making decisions.

She is also corrected when she behaves in ways considered disrespectful or excessively self-focused.

Over time, Mei may do more than learn individual rules.

Ideas about responsibility to other people may become part of how she evaluates her own behaviour and understands the kind of person she should be.

This illustrates how learning social norms can contribute to development of self.

At first, a child may follow a social norm mainly because another person expects it.

For example, a young child may say thank you because a parent reminds them.

Through repeated social experience, the child can learn that gratitude is expected and valued in particular situations.

Eventually, the child may use that expectation to regulate their own behaviour without being reminded.

A socially learned rule has therefore become an internal standard the child can use when judging their own behaviour.

Enculturation can contribute to development of self when cultural expectations become part of how a person understands and evaluates themselves.

Social norms can contribute to many aspects of the developing self.

They may influence:

  • which behaviours a child considers appropriate
  • which personal qualities are valued
  • how independence and responsibility are understood
  • expectations about family and social roles
  • how emotions should be expressed
  • which goals are encouraged or discouraged

This does not mean every member of a culture develops the same self.

People experience cultural expectations differently, participate in multiple social groups and respond to norms in different ways.

Children learn social norms through repeated participation in social life.

They can observe how other people behave and what behaviour receives approval or disapproval.

Other people may also directly reinforce behaviour through praise, correction, attention or other social consequences.

Children participate in routines and practices — at home, at school, with friends and in the wider community — that repeatedly communicate what behaviour is expected.

Language also communicates cultural expectations through instructions, stories, explanations and everyday conversation.

Enculturation therefore occurs through continuing social experience rather than through one formal lesson about ‘how to behave.’

Enculturation becomes especially important for development of self when social expectations are internalised.

Internalisation occurs when a person begins using a socially learned expectation as their own standard for interpreting or regulating behaviour.

For example, a child may initially avoid interrupting adults because a parent will correct them.

Later, the child may avoid interrupting even when the parent is absent because the expectation has become part of how the child understands appropriate behaviour.

The child is no longer responding only to immediate external control.

A cultural expectation has become part of the psychological standards the child uses to guide themselves.

The developing self includes more than knowing facts about who we are.

People also develop ideas about who they should be, which behaviours fit their roles and which qualities they value in themselves.

Enculturation can contribute to these standards.

For example, a child repeatedly taught that being a good older sibling involves protecting and helping younger siblings may eventually incorporate responsibility into how they understand themselves.

Social norms can therefore contribute to self-development by becoming part of the standards, roles and values through which people interpret their own behaviour.

Cross-cultural research has often found higher average individualism in some societies such as the United States and Australia and higher average collectivism/interdependence in some East Asian societies. These are population-level tendencies, not descriptions of every individual. There is substantial variation within every culture.

Individualism and collectivism are dimensions rather than two mutually exclusive types. Individuals and communities can show elements of both, and patterns can vary by context, generation, region and social group. Nationality alone does not label an individual person.

Markus & Kitayama (1991) distinguished two self-construals:

  • Independent self-construal: greater emphasis on internal attributes, personal goals and uniqueness.
  • Interdependent self-construal: greater emphasis on relationships, social roles and adjustment to others.

These are theoretical patterns of self-construal, not fixed personality types or exact equivalents of nationality.

Independent/interdependent self-construals are related to broader ideas about individualism and collectivism, but the terms should not be treated as perfectly interchangeable.

How might enculturation affect self-concept differently across cultures?
In some cultural settings children may learn to describe themselves through personal traits and achievements; in others, through roles and relationships. Neither is deficient — they reflect different cultural answers to “who am I?” Remember these are population-level tendencies, not descriptions of every individual.
A child describes themselves through roles and relationships (“a daughter, a team member”); another through personal traits and qualities (“funny, athletic”). What does this illustrate?
Two self-construal patterns consistent with Markus & Kitayama’s framework: more interdependent (relationship-based) and more independent (trait-based) self-descriptions. These are theoretical patterns, not fixed personality types or nationality labels.
A student says, “Mei is Chinese, so she must have an interdependent self-concept.” What is wrong with this reasoning?
It treats a population-level cultural tendency as though it determines an individual person. Cultural frameworks describe average patterns and possible influences, but individuals vary according to family, region, generation, education, personal experience and other cultural influences. Nationality alone cannot determine someone’s self-construal.
A child begins helping serve food to older relatives at every family gathering. Adults praise this behaviour and explain that caring for family members is important. How could enculturation influence the child’s developing self?
Through repeated participation, modelling, explicit teaching and reinforcement, the child learns that family responsibility is culturally valued. Over time, this value may become incorporated into self-concept — for example, seeing oneself as a responsible family member.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Enculturation is the process through which individuals learn and participate in their cultural environment.

Peer Influence on Development

How peers shape identity, behaviour and self-concept.

Peer Influence and the Development of Self

Peers become important social reference points as children develop.

Through friendship groups and other peer relationships, children and adolescents encounter feedback, comparison, group expectations and models of behaviour.

These experiences can contribute to how people evaluate themselves, which groups they identify with and which behaviours or qualities become important to their developing self.

Peer influence does not mean that young people simply copy their friends. Individuals also choose peers, respond differently to group expectations and influence the people around them.

Peers provide opportunities for social comparison — evaluating aspects of ourselves in relation to other people.

For example, a student may compare their academic performance, athletic ability, appearance or social confidence with classmates.

These comparisons can provide information that contributes to self-evaluation.

A student who repeatedly sees themselves as successful in comparison with relevant peers may begin to view that ability as an important strength.

Another student may make comparisons that contribute to feelings of inadequacy.

The important developmental point is that self-understanding can be shaped partly through comparison with socially meaningful others.

Jamal joins a school basketball team.

At first, he thinks of basketball as something he simply enjoys.

Over time, teammates praise his defensive ability and regularly choose him for an important defensive role.

Jamal also notices that he performs this role effectively compared with many other players.

He gradually begins describing himself as ‘a strong defensive player.’

The peer group has not created Jamal’s ability, but comparison, feedback and his role within the group have contributed to how he understands himself.

Peers do more than provide people to compare ourselves with.

Acceptance, rejection, encouragement, teasing, praise and criticism can provide social feedback about how a person is perceived by others.

Belonging to a valued group can also become part of self-understanding.

For example, a student may increasingly think of themselves as an athlete, musician or high-achieving student partly because participation in a peer group repeatedly reinforces that identity.

However, peer feedback does not automatically become part of the self. Its influence depends on how important the group is to the person and how the individual interprets the experience.

Peer groups can develop norms — shared expectations about behaviour within the group.

These norms can communicate which behaviours, interests or qualities receive approval.

For example, one friendship group might value academic effort, while another may treat appearing uninterested in school as socially desirable.

Young people may adjust their behaviour in response to these expectations.

Repeated participation in a group can also contribute to self-development when group values become connected with how the person sees themselves.

Peer norms therefore provide one mechanism through which social relationships can contribute to both behaviour and self-understanding.

Peers can influence behaviour through processes such as modelling and social reinforcement.

Young people can observe what peers do and see which behaviours receive approval, attention or status within the group.

They may then be more likely to perform behaviours that appear socially rewarding.

For example, if classmates consistently praise a student for making other people laugh, humorous behaviour may become more frequent and may eventually become connected with the student’s self-description as ‘the funny one.’

This provides one way repeated peer interaction can contribute to development of self.

When friends behave similarly, psychologists need to consider at least two processes.

Peer selection occurs when people choose friends who are already similar to them.

Peer influence occurs when interaction with peers contributes to change in a person’s behaviour, attitudes or self-understanding.

For example, if several friends enjoy studying, this does not automatically mean the friendship group caused each person to become academically motivated. They may have become friends partly because they already shared that interest.

Both processes can also operate together.

Researchers therefore need evidence of change over time before making strong claims that peers caused a developmental outcome.

Peer influence is not a one-directional process.

A young person can be influenced by friends while simultaneously influencing those friends.

For example, joining a friendship group that enjoys exercise may increase someone’s physical activity, while that person may also introduce the group to a new sport.

Peer relationships are therefore interactive developmental contexts rather than situations in which one passive person simply copies the group.

Peer influence is not inherently positive or negative.

Peers can encourage behaviours such as studying, practising a skill, exercising, helping others or seeking support.

They can also reinforce risky, exclusionary or harmful behaviour.

For development of self, the important question is what qualities, behaviours and identities are being reinforced within a particular peer context.

The same psychological processes can therefore contribute to very different developmental outcomes.

Attachment-related expectations may be associated with later peer relationships, but the relationship is probabilistic.

Temperament, social skills, current friendships, family environment, culture and later experiences can all influence peer relationships.

We cannot predict a peer style directly from an early attachment classification.

What it contributes: it identifies a psychological mechanism through which peers can become reference points for self-evaluation.

Caution: comparison is common but does not automatically change self-concept; the effect depends on who the comparison is made with and how the result is interpreted.

The association could reflect peer influence, but it could also reflect peer selection: teenagers who already have similar attitudes may choose one another as friends. Shared school, family or neighbourhood factors could also influence both. Longitudinal evidence can help examine whether friendship predicts later behavioural change, but simple correlation cannot establish causation.

Childhood Experiences & Development of Self

Childhood Experiences and the Development of Self

Childhood provides repeated experiences through which people learn about themselves, other people and their environment.

Experiences of success, failure, support, responsibility, stress, opportunity or adversity can contribute to expectations about what a child can do and how they understand themselves.

However, childhood experiences do not write a permanent psychological script.

Their effects depend on factors such as timing, severity, duration, relationships, available support and later experiences.

Childhood experience is therefore best understood as contributing to developmental pathways rather than determining the final self.

Children repeatedly learn about themselves through what happens when they attempt activities.

Imagine a child learning to ride a bicycle.

At first, they struggle and need considerable help. With practice, they become able to balance and ride independently.

The experience provides more than a new physical skill. Successfully mastering the challenge can contribute to expectations such as:

  • ‘I can improve through practice.’
  • ‘I can handle difficult tasks.’
  • ‘I can do some things independently.’

Repeated mastery experiences can therefore contribute to developing beliefs about competence and the self.

Development of self is also shaped through ordinary interactions with other people.

Children receive feedback about their behaviour, abilities and social roles from caregivers, teachers, siblings and other important people.

They also encounter opportunities — or barriers — that affect what they are able to practise and experience.

For example, being trusted with gradually increasing responsibility may provide experiences of competence and independence.

The developmental importance lies not in one isolated event but in patterns of experience that provide information children can use when understanding themselves.

Some childhood experiences create substantial developmental risk.

Examples can include chronic family conflict, neglect, violence, severe deprivation or persistent instability.

Such experiences may affect opportunities to develop emotional security, relationships, coping strategies and positive expectations about the self.

However, adversity should be described as increasing risk, not producing an inevitable outcome.

Children exposed to similar adversity can follow different developmental pathways.

A risk factor changes the probability of an outcome; it does not guarantee it.

The developmental effect of adversity can depend on factors such as:

  • how severe and persistent the experience is
  • when it occurs
  • whether several risks occur together
  • the child’s other relationships
  • opportunities available later
  • access to support

Psychologists should therefore avoid turning a statistical relationship between childhood adversity and later outcomes into a prediction about an individual child.

Developmental risk can be reduced by protective factors — conditions or experiences associated with better outcomes despite adversity.

Examples can include a stable relationship with a supportive adult, effective coping skills, supportive friendships, opportunities for success and access to helpful community or educational resources.

Protective factors do not erase difficult experiences.

They can provide additional resources that help explain why people exposed to similar risks may develop differently.

Resilience refers to relatively positive adaptation despite significant adversity.

It should not be treated as a fixed personality trait that some children simply ‘have’ and others lack.

Resilience can depend on interactions between the person and their environment, including relationships, opportunities and support available over time.

This makes resilience important for understanding development of self because it shows that early adversity does not permanently determine later development.

Two children can encounter similar events but differ in:

  • previous experiences
  • relationships and support
  • interpretation of the event
  • other risks they face
  • opportunities available afterward

Development is therefore probabilistic rather than deterministic.

Childhood experiences provide information through which children can develop expectations about their abilities, relationships and place in the social world.

Experiences of support, mastery and responsibility can contribute to positive expectations about competence or belonging.

Experiences of persistent adversity may create developmental risks that affect self-evaluation, relationships or coping.

But these expectations remain open to later experience.

The self develops across time rather than being permanently created by one period of childhood.

It prevents fatalistic readings of adversity research: outcomes depend on the interaction between risk and protective factors, so supportive experiences and skill-building can genuinely change trajectories.

No. Parenting may contribute to development, but correlational evidence cannot isolate causation. Child temperament may affect how parents respond, and other family or environmental factors may influence both parenting style and self-esteem.

The relationship provides social support, while repeated successful performances provide mastery experiences and opportunities to develop self-efficacy. No. Similar risks can lead to different outcomes because development depends on interacting protective factors, individual characteristics and later experiences. Resilience means relatively positive adaptation despite adversity, not that the adversity had no effect.

  4. Resilience is relatively positive adaptation despite significant adversity.
  5. Resilience emerges through interacting individual, relational and environmental factors.

  7. Parenting research is often correlational, so causal conclusions require caution.
  8. Development involves risk × protection × individual characteristics × later experience.
  9. Early experiences matter, but developmental trajectories can change.

HL Extension: Culture & Human Development

HL extension: how cultural practices, values and meaning-making can shape developmental experiences — and why careful claims need careful interpretation.

Culture describes the shared practices, values, norms, beliefs and tools that children grow up within — and that children actively participate in through day-to-day interaction.

  • Cultural dimensions such as individualism and collectivism (or independent and interdependent patterns) describe tendencies observed across groups, not fixed types of individuals.
  • Nationality, ethnicity or community should never be used to assume how a particular child will behave.
  • Children within any cultural community vary, and many people show elements of both patterns across different situations.

Culture is therefore best treated as a context for development rather than a label that predicts any single child.

A student writes: “Children from collectivist cultures do not develop independent identities.” Why is this claim problematic?
It turns a group-level cultural pattern into a fixed trait of every individual — the ecological fallacy combined with cultural stereotyping. Dimensions describe tendencies observed across groups; individuals vary, and independence and interdependence are not opposites that exclude one another.

Ecological fallacy: drawing conclusions about every individual from a statistic averaged across a whole group.

Child-development example: a cross-cultural study may report that, on average, adolescents in Country A describe themselves with more relationship-focused terms than adolescents in Country B. That average says nothing about any particular adolescent — some in Country A will be highly individual-focused, and some in Country B highly relationship-focused.

The fallacy matters in developmental psychology because group averages are easily quoted as if they described all children — which is exactly how stereotypes form.

A study finds the average autonomy score is lower in Country A than Country B. A journalist writes: “Children in Country A lack autonomy.” What two problems should a psychologist identify?
First, the ecological fallacy: a group average is being applied to every child. Second, a measurement question: the self-report scale may not mean the same thing in both contexts, so the size of the difference may partly reflect how the construct was measured rather than a genuine difference in autonomy.

Piaget’s stage theory, the Strange Situation and other frameworks were developed mainly in particular cultural contexts and carry some assumptions from those settings.

  • Piaget: the timing of some stage behaviours can be influenced by schooling and by the kinds of experience children have.
  • Vygotsky: places cultural tools and social guidance at the centre, so cognitive development is understood as culturally situated.
  • Attachment: what separation and reunion mean, and how children express needs, can differ across caregiving contexts.

Key nuance: a model may identify useful developmental processes while still requiring cultural qualification. Showing that a finding varies across cultures does not prove the underlying processes do not exist — it shows the model and its measures need careful adaptation and interpretation.

Does evidence of cultural variation in attachment classifications disprove attachment theory?
No. It shows that the measure and some assumptions carry cultural assumptions: separation may have different meanings, and the same behaviour may express different things. A strong conclusion is that attachment processes may be universal while their expression and measurement require cultural qualification — not that the theory is invalid.
  • Emic approach: studying behaviour from within a culture, using categories and meanings that are meaningful to the people living in it.
  • Etic approach: studying behaviour from outside, using categories that can be applied across cultures for comparison.

Both contribute. Comparisons need some shared measurement (etic), but imposed categories can miss local meaning. The main risk is an imposed etic: applying a framework from one setting unchanged, so that unfamiliar behaviour is mislabelled or judged deficient.

Example: importing a self-esteem scale word-for-word and concluding a community has low self-esteem may reflect that the items do not measure self-esteem equivalently there — not that children genuinely have low self-esteem.

A researcher uses a US-developed self-esteem questionnaire word-for-word in a new cultural context and concludes children there have unusually low self-esteem. What is the main concern?
Imposed etic risk. The items may not carry the same meaning, and the way children respond may reflect communication style, language and familiarity with the task rather than low self-esteem. The conclusion assumes measurement equivalence that was never established.

No. A performance difference may reflect familiarity with the task, language, schooling, expectations or measurement validity — or genuine differences in developed skills.   2. The ecological fallacy: an average across a group describes nobody in particular.

HL Extension: Motivation & Human Development

HL extension: motivation as an influence on children’s learning, behaviour and development — before, during and after rewards.

Children are not passive receivers of development. They explore, practise, persist, imitate and seek approval. Motivation is what gives this activity direction and energy.

  • Exploring a new object is intrinsically driven curiosity.
  • Repeating a skill until it is mastered is practice with a purpose.
  • Approval-seeking reflects the child’s sensitivity to social feedback.

Motivation therefore helps explain why children engage with the world at all — the engine behind much of what developmental psychologists study.

A toddler builds and rebuilds the same block tower many times. Is this wasted effort?
No. From a developmental view, repeated practice is intrinsically motivated activity through which skills and expectations are consolidated. The behaviour is explained less by the outcome (a tower) than by the drive to master and understand — that drive is motivation.
  • Intrinsic motivation: behaviour is driven primarily by internal reasons — enjoyment, curiosity, personal value. Example: a child reads at bedtime because they genuinely enjoy the story.
  • Extrinsic motivation: behaviour is influenced by external rewards, consequences, pressure or approval. Example: a child completes reading pages because each completed page earns game time.

Do not assume one type is always good and the other always bad. External structure can start a behaviour that later becomes personally valued, and an intrinsic drive alone does not guarantee persistence or success.

A child who first did chores only for pocket money later does them unprompted. Which process has changed?
The behaviour has shifted from being maintained primarily by an external reward toward more internal reasons over time. This is a gradual process, not a switch — and it does not mean the reward was “good” or “bad.” It shows motivation is dynamic, not fixed.

Self-Determination Theory proposes that three psychological needs support sustained, self-directed engagement:

  • Autonomy — feeling the activity is genuinely chosen, not imposed.
  • Competence — feeling capable of making achievable progress.
  • Relatedness — feeling connected to and supported by others.

One continuous example: a child learning to read is more likely to keep reading when they help choose their own books (autonomy), experience success with texts that stretch them slightly (competence), and can talk about stories with family who listen and respond (relatedness).

SDT predicts that when these needs are supported, engagement is more likely to be maintained — even when external rewards are removed.

An app rewards reading with points. After the points stop, the child reads less. How should an SDT-trained psychologist interpret this?
The reward supported engagement while it was present. When it stops, reading may decline if the activity has not become internally valued or if the child’s needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness were not otherwise supported. This is not a claim that rewards “destroyed” motivation — the effect depends on the type of reward, the task, the context and how controlling the child experiences it.

Praise, stickers, points and consequences are common in families, classrooms and apps. Over time, externally supported behaviour can become internalised — the child comes to value the activity itself.

  • This internalisation is not automatic and does not happen at the same rate for every child.
  • It depends on the type of motivator (informative feedback versus controlling threats), the context, the task and how the individual interprets the situation.
  • Evidence of reduced interest after rewards in some conditions does not warrant the sweeping claim that “rewards destroy motivation.”
“Praise always undermines intrinsic motivation.” Evaluate this claim.
Too absolute. Effects depend on the type of praise (informative versus controlling), the task, the child’s age and individual differences. Some forms of feedback support perceived competence and motivation; other forms may feel controlling. The strong answer rejects universal rules and asks about the specific condition.

Motivation helps explain why children engage, practise and persist — but motivation is not the only influence on development. Biological (maturation, attention, health), cognitive (beliefs about ability, prior knowledge) and sociocultural (norms, models, expectations, teaching quality) factors all play a role.

Conclusion: motivation helps explain why children engage, but it is not a single cause of developmental outcomes. Strong explanations combine motivational processes with what else is operating in a child’s situation.

A teacher says: “Motivation alone determines how much a child learns.” Evaluate the claim.
Motivation is one important influence, but learning also depends on prior knowledge, task demands, cognitive capacity, teaching quality, attention, health and environment. Claiming a single cause ignores the interacting system of developmental influences.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Motivation gives children’s behaviour direction: intrinsic reasons come from within, extrinsic reasons from outside — neither is always better.
  2. Self-Determination Theory: autonomy, competence and relatedness support sustained engagement.
  3. Extrinsically motivated behaviour can become internalised, but the process is gradual, not automatic and not identical across children.
  4. Be cautious with “rewards backfire” claims — effects depend on type, context and individual interpretation.
  5. Motivation is one part of a wider developmental system, not a single cause.

HL Extension: Technology & Human Development

HL extension: technology as a possible influence on the developing self, attachment and learning — and how to reason carefully about the evidence.

For many adolescents, self-presentation, feedback (likes and comments), social comparison, belonging and identity exploration partly take place online.

  • Digital environments can shape how identity is presented, compared and evaluated.
  • Online contexts can add to the settings in which young people try out and reflect on who they are.

Critical caution: do not claim social media “creates” a self. Identity develops through ongoing interaction across many contexts — online settings are one influence among several, not a single cause.

A headline states: “Instagram creates teenagers’ identities.” Evaluate the claim.
It overclaims. Platforms may shape how identity is presented and socially compared, but identity construction is a long process involving relationships, family, school and culture. The claim also treats correlational evidence as if it showed a single causal mechanism. Stronger language: online platforms may influence identity development rather than create it.

Video calls: infants and young children can recognise and respond to far-away caregivers through screens (for example, a parent working away from home). Video contact can therefore support relationships across distance.

Caregiver distraction: some research has looked at caregivers using phones while caring for a toddler. Findings show associations with reduced attention and interaction in the moment.

  • Distraction during a short observation is not evidence of insecure attachment.
  • Attachment classification depends on the broader pattern of behaviour, especially how the child uses the caregiver as a secure base and responds at reunion.
  • Association is not causation: caregiver stress, workload or family circumstances could influence both phone use and child behaviour.
A study finds that caregivers who checked phones more often had toddlers who showed more resistant behaviour during an observation. Can we conclude phone use caused insecure attachment?
No. This is an association from a limited observation. “Attachment” was not fully assessed, direction is ambiguous, and third variables such as caregiver stress or family circumstances may influence both phone use and child behaviour. The evidence justifies cautious, associative language — not a claim of causation.

“Screen time” bundles very different activities: a video call with a grandparent, an educational app, gaming, passive clips, homework and messaging peers. These affect development through different mechanisms — so sweeping claims such as “screen time damages development” are too broad to be psychologically useful.

A stronger psychological question is: Which technology? How is it used? At what age? With whom? For how long? What is it replacing? With what outcome?

“Screen time damages children’s development.” Why is this claim too broad to be useful?
Because “screen time” is not one behaviour. A video call with a relative, an educational task, gaming and passive viewing involve different processes. The strong claim treats very different activities as a single variable. Replacing it with specific questions (which technology, how, with whom, how long, replacing what, with what outcome) makes the claim testable.

AI models can formalise and simulate developmental processes, making assumptions explicit and generating testable predictions — for example, models of language acquisition or of strategies children use in tasks.

They are useful thinking tools, but three limits stay central:

  • Similar performance is not the same psychological process. A model that succeeds at a task may do so through entirely different computations.
  • The learning mode differs: models are trained on data in ways that do not reproduce a child’s embodied, social, maturational experience.
  • Models do not reproduce attachment relationships, culture, embodiment or the lived experience of developing.

Always ask of an AI demonstration: How does it learn? What experience does it assume? What is left out?

“An AI model that solves conservation tasks better than most six-year-olds has the same cognitive development as a six-year-old.” Evaluate the claim.
No. Similar output does not demonstrate the same underlying process. The model’s learning mode and assumptions differ fundamentally from a child’s embodied, social and maturational development. The claim confuses a model’s performance with the development of a human mind.

Evidence on technology and development has recurring problems that a careful psychologist always considers:

  • Most findings are correlational — association alone cannot establish causation.
  • Self-selection: children who already behave or feel differently may choose different technology use.
  • Rapidly changing technology makes findings date quickly.
  • Measurement: self-reported screen time is unreliable, and different measures ask different questions.
  • Sampling bias: convenience samples may not represent the population.
  • Bidirectional ambiguity: the same correlation can be read in more than one direction.

Social media and self-esteem example: a survey finds that teenagers who spend more time on social media report lower average self-esteem. The same correlation fits at least four stories:

WHY THE SAME CORRELATION FITS FOUR STORIES

• MORE SOCIAL MEDIA USE → LOWER SELF-ESTEEM

• LOWER SELF-ESTEEM → MORE SOCIAL MEDIA USE

• BOTH INFLUENCE EACH OTHER

• A THIRD VARIABLE (FOR EXAMPLE SLEEP OR OFFLINE RELATIONSHIPS) INFLUENCES BOTH

An association alone cannot distinguish these possibilities.

A survey shows that teenagers who spend more time on social media report lower average self-esteem. Does this prove social media lowered self-esteem?
No. The finding is correlational and fits at least four explanations: social media use lowers self-esteem, lower self-esteem leads to more use, both influence each other, or a third variable such as poor sleep or weak offline relationships influences both. Strong evidence would require designs that track individuals over time or manipulate the variable while controlling others.

IB Concepts in Human Development

Six theoretical tools for analysing evidence and claims.

Bias refers to systematic influences that may distort psychological research, interpretation or conclusions.

  • The Strange Situation may carry cultural assumptions because it was developed in a particular cultural context.
  • Bowlby’s theoretical expectations may have influenced interpretation in retrospective research.
  • Cultural stereotypes can bias interpretation of group differences.
  • Sampling from narrow populations can limit generalisability.
A researcher assumes that the US Strange Situation pattern is the ideal standard and describes every cultural difference from it as deficient. Which concept is most relevant?
Bias. The researcher is treating one cultural pattern as the universal standard and interpreting differences through that assumption.

Causality concerns whether evidence demonstrates that one variable actually produces change in another.

  • Early attachment correlated with later outcomes does NOT automatically mean attachment caused them.
  • Parenting style correlated with self-esteem does NOT establish parenting → self-esteem.
  • Peer similarity may reflect peer influence, peer selection or both.
  • Experiments provide stronger causal inference about manipulated variables, but conclusions remain limited to what was actually manipulated and measured.
Children receiving highly responsive caregiving later show higher average social competence. Can we conclude that responsive caregiving caused the difference?
Not from a correlation alone. Child temperament, family circumstances or other variables could influence both caregiving and later social competence. The evidence may support an association without establishing causation.

Change concerns how psychological characteristics and behaviour develop, remain stable or alter over time.

  • Neuroplasticity
  • Synaptogenesis and pruning
  • Cognitive development
  • Changing theory-of-mind performance
  • Resilience
  • Internal working models that can be modified by later relationships
  • Developmental trajectories altered by protective experiences

Development does not mean that early experience fixes the future. Psychology investigates both continuity AND change.

A child experiences severe early adversity but later develops strong relationships and positive self-esteem. Which concept helps us analyse this?
Change. Early experiences can influence developmental trajectories without permanently fixing them. Later protective relationships, mastery experiences and changing environments can contribute to different outcomes.

Measurement concerns how psychologists operationalise and assess psychological constructs.

THEORY OF MIND → false-belief tasks

ATTACHMENT → Strange Situation behaviour

OBJECT PERMANENCE → search behaviour vs looking-time paradigms

SELF-ESTEEM → questionnaire scales

Psychologists cannot usually observe constructs such as attachment, self-esteem or theory of mind directly. They operationalise them using observable behaviour or responses.

This creates the HL question: does the operationalisation validly represent the underlying construct?

Looking-time paradigms infer infants’ expectations from patterns of attention, whereas Piaget’s search tasks require infants to produce coordinated behavioural responses. Different operationalisations can therefore lead researchers to different conclusions about when an ability appears.

A baby looks longer at an apparently impossible event. Does this directly prove the baby possesses an adult-like understanding of object permanence?
No. Looking time is an observable behaviour from which researchers infer expectations. The interpretation depends on whether the measure validly captures the psychological ability being claimed.

Perspective concerns how psychological phenomena can be understood through different theoretical, cultural or disciplinary viewpoints.

  • Piaget → emphasis on active cognitive construction
  • Vygotsky → greater emphasis on social interaction and cultural tools
  • Bowlby → evolutionary/attachment perspective
  • Cultural psychology → cultural context and self-construal

Different perspectives may emphasise different mechanisms without one necessarily explaining the whole of development.

Piaget and Vygotsky explain cognitive development differently. Does one perspective automatically make the other useless?
No. Piaget highlights active cognitive construction, while Vygotsky gives greater emphasis to social interaction and cultural tools. Comparing perspectives can reveal different mechanisms and limitations.

Responsibility concerns the obligations psychologists have when conducting research, interpreting evidence and communicating conclusions.

Research ethics:

  • Harlow → severe harm to animals
  • Strange Situation → deliberately produces mild infant distress
  • Research with children → consent from guardians, assent where appropriate, protection from harm and special care with vulnerable participants
  • Autism / deprivation / attachment → avoid deterministic, stigmatising or culturally biased interpretations

Communicating evidence: researchers and psychologists have a responsibility not to present correlational findings as causal facts or group averages as descriptions of every individual.

A headline says, “Insecure attachment permanently damages children’s future relationships.” Even if based on a real correlational study, what responsibility problem exists?
The claim overstates the evidence and presents a probabilistic association as developmental destiny. Responsible communication should acknowledge uncertainty, alternative explanations and the possibility of later developmental change.

Use the concept or concepts that genuinely help analyse the evidence or question. Do not force every study through every concept.

Scenario: Two children begin at a new school. One quickly explores the classroom and approaches classmates. The other initially remains withdrawn and cautious.

Could attachment theory help explain the difference?
Bowlby proposed that repeated attachment experiences may contribute to internal working models — expectations about the self and relationships. Different expectations could therefore be one possible influence on how children approach unfamiliar social situations. However, the behaviour in this scenario cannot establish the children’s attachment classifications or show that attachment caused the difference. Temperament, previous school experiences, language, peers and other factors could also contribute.

IMPORTANT:

Do NOT write: “secure attachment causes confidence” or “insecure attachment causes withdrawal.”

Instead: “attachment may contribute to…”

Scenario: Lena watches Amir place a toy in the blue box. Amir leaves the room. While he is away, another child moves the toy into the red box. Lena is asked where Amir will look for the toy and answers, “the red box.”

How could Theory of Mind research help explain Lena’s answer?
A false-belief task tests whether a child can represent another person’s belief when that belief differs from reality and from the child’s own knowledge. Amir believes the toy is still in the blue box, while Lena knows it is now in the red box. Predicting that Amir will search the red box may therefore indicate difficulty representing his false belief. However, performance on one task should not be treated as proof that Lena completely lacks Theory of Mind because language, memory, executive demands and task understanding can also affect performance.

WHAT WAS ACTUALLY OBSERVED?

Lena predicted the red box.

WHAT IS INFERRED?

Possible difficulty representing Amir’s false belief.

This distinction is extremely important.

Scenario: A longitudinal study finds that children who experienced more responsive caregiving in early childhood later report higher average self-esteem.

A student concludes: “Responsive caregiving causes high self-esteem.” Evaluate the conclusion.
The association is consistent with caregiving being one developmental influence, but the finding alone does not establish causation. Child temperament, family environment, socioeconomic conditions and later experiences could influence both caregiving and self-esteem. The direction may also be bidirectional because children’s characteristics can influence caregiver behaviour.

OBSERVED: responsive caregiving is associated with later self-esteem.

NOT ESTABLISHED: responsive caregiving alone caused the difference.

Scenario: Researchers use the Strange Situation in two cultural groups. One group shows substantially more resistant classifications.

Can the researchers conclude that this cultural group has poorer attachment?
Not automatically. The Strange Situation operationalises attachment through behaviour during separation and reunion. If separation has different meanings or familiarity across cultural contexts, the same procedure may not measure attachment equivalently in both groups. Researchers should consider cultural validity before interpreting the classification difference as poorer attachment.

MEASUREMENT + BIAS + PERSPECTIVE

Measurement asks whether the instrument works equivalently, Bias asks whether one behavioural pattern is being treated as a universal standard, and Perspective asks how caregiving practices may differ between cultures.

Scenario: A child experiences substantial adversity early in life but later develops supportive friendships, succeeds in school and reports increasingly positive self-beliefs.

How does this scenario illustrate development rather than contradict early-experience research?
Early adversity can increase developmental risk without permanently determining an outcome. Supportive relationships, mastery experiences and changing environments may act as protective factors and alter developmental trajectories. This illustrates resilience and the IB concept of Change.

When applying psychology to an unseen situation:

  1. Start with the scenario, not the researcher.
  2. Select psychology that genuinely explains something in it.
  3. Name the mechanism.
  4. Connect the mechanism to specific scenario details.
  5. Use evidence/theory where useful.
  6. Separate observation from inference.
  7. Calibrate causal language.

Language guide

STRONG: “may contribute to” · “is consistent with” · “is associated with” · “could help explain” · “the evidence suggests”

USE ONLY WHEN JUSTIFIED: “caused” · “proved” · “determines” · “will lead to”

What Should I Remember?

  1. Application means USING psychology to explain specific scenario details.
  2. Naming a theory or researcher alone is not enough.
  3. Explain the mechanism.
  4. Separate what was observed from what psychology allows you to infer.
  5. Consider alternative explanations.
  6. Correlation does not automatically establish causation.
  7. Psychological explanations are often probabilistic.
  8. Use evidence to support reasoning rather than simply dropping study names.
  9. Strong HL answers integrate knowledge with concepts such as causality, measurement, change, bias, perspective and responsibility.
