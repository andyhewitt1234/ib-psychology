Human Development: What You Will Learn

An overview of how psychology examines human development across the lifespan.

Human Development examines how people change psychologically across development and how biological, cognitive, and sociocultural influences interact. This unit has two broad strands:

Models of Development

How development is understood — through stages or continuously, through biological maturation or social interaction, through Piaget or Vygotsky.

Development of Self

How the self develops through attachment, enculturation of social norms, peer influence, and childhood experiences.

The central question of this unit is:

How do biological, cognitive, and sociocultural factors interact to shape psychological development?

Every lesson in this unit connects back to this question. Development is not driven by a single factor — it emerges from the interaction of maturation, experience, social interaction, and culture.

Why does development require understanding multiple perspectives?
Biological maturation creates potential; experience shapes which potentials are realised; social interaction and culture determine what is learned and how. No single perspective captures the full picture of development.

What Does Development Mean?

Key concepts for understanding psychological development.

  • Development: progressive changes in abilities, behaviour, and understanding over time
    Example: a toddler babbles single words, then builds two-word phrases, then full sentences with grammar — each stage building on the last.
  • Maturation: biologically influenced developmental changes that create readiness for new abilities
    Example: changes in the nervous system, muscles and body help make sitting, standing and walking possible. Infants usually follow broadly similar developmental sequences, but the exact timing also varies with individual and environmental factors.
  • Continuity: gradual, incremental change (like a plant growing taller)
    Example: vocabulary grows steadily month by month — each new word adds to the total without changing what a word IS.
  • Qualitative change: real shifts in kind, not just amount
    Example: a preoperational child thinks flattening clay reduces its amount; a concrete-operational child knows it stays the same — different KIND of thinking.
  • Individual differences: people develop at different rates and in different ways
    Example: one toddler walks at 10 months; another at 15 months — both within normal range, both walking equally well eventually.
  • Biological: genetics, brain maturation, hormones, critical periods
    Example: genetic and hormonal processes strongly influence the timing of puberty, while factors such as nutrition, health and environment can also affect when development occurs. Culture then influences what adolescence and independence look like socially.
  • Environmental: experiences, parenting, education, adversity
    Example: two identical twins raised apart develop different vocabularies depending on the language richness of their respective homes.
  • Sociocultural: culture, language, social norms, peer groups
    Example: children raised in multigenerational households learn different social norms than those in nuclear families.

Development usually reflects interaction rather than a simple nature-versus-nurture choice. Biological maturation can create readiness or constraints, while experience and sociocultural context influence how developmental potential is expressed.

The same biological potential leads to different outcomes depending on environment and culture.

Quick check: a toddler raised bilingual reaches language milestones on the same timetable as monolingual toddlers, yet ends up with two languages. Split that outcome into maturation versus environment.
Maturation supplies the readiness timetable (brain areas for language prime on schedule). Environment fills in WHICH languages. Same biological window, different cultural input — interaction in one example.
A newborn walks earlier than another but talks later. Is that evidence AGAINST maturation timetables?
No — individual differences sit comfortably within maturation: biological timetables set typical RANGES, not identical dates, and exact timing can also be influenced by individual and environmental factors. Different systems also mature on partly independent schedules.
Which lens would you cite for: (a) puberty timing, (b) vocabulary size, (c) politeness norms?
(a) Biological/maturation; (b) environmental/learning input; (c) sociocultural norms. Most real outcomes blend all three — which is why integration earns top marks.
Two children begin walking at different ages. One student says, “That proves walking is environmental rather than maturational.” What is wrong with this conclusion?
Individual differences in timing do not disprove maturation. Biological development creates the physical and neurological readiness for walking, but the exact timing can also be influenced by health, experience, opportunity for movement and other environmental factors. The strongest developmental explanation therefore considers interaction rather than treating biology and environment as competing explanations.

Why definitions matter

IB answers reward precision: calling something “maturation” when it is learned (or vice-versa) changes which evidence you must cite.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Development = psychological change across time.
  2. Maturation = biologically influenced developmental change/readiness.
  3. Continuous development = gradual quantitative change.
  4. Qualitative change = change in the nature or organisation of thinking/behaviour.
  5. Biological, environmental and sociocultural influences interact.
  6. Individual differences mean developmental patterns are not identical for everyone.

Brain Maturation

How the brain changes during development — tested longitudinally.

Different brain regions and networks follow different developmental trajectories. Sensory and motor systems generally develop earlier, while association regions involved in complex cognition and executive control show more prolonged development.

  • Survival functions (brainstem) mature earliest
    Example: newborns regulate breathing and heart rate from birth — no learning required.
  • Sensory processing next
    Example: by 6 months, infants distinguish faces and track moving objects smoothly.
  • Motor control progressively
    Example: reaching (~4 months) → sitting (~6 months) → crawling (~9 months) → walking (~12 months).
  • Association regions, including the prefrontal cortex, develop over a longer period
    Example: the networks supporting planning, inhibition and decision-making continue changing through adolescence and into young adulthood. “Mid-20s” is an approximate description of prolonged development — not a switch that suddenly turns on at a set age.

Younger children therefore have capable sensory/motor systems but more limited planning and impulse control — a developmental fact, not a character flaw.

Aim

To investigate structural brain development across childhood and adolescence using repeated MRI measurements.

Who was studied

A longitudinal sample of children and adolescents who received repeated structural MRI scans across development.

Procedure

  • Structural MRI used to examine developmental changes in brain regions and tissue volumes.
  • Repeated scans within individuals allowed developmental trajectories to be tracked across time.

Key finding

Brain development did not consist simply of steady growth. Different brain structures showed different developmental trajectories, and grey- and white-matter measures changed substantially across childhood and adolescence.

Importance

The study provided longitudinal evidence that human brain structure continues developing throughout childhood and adolescence.

The more detailed picture — in which higher-order association areas mature later than primary sensory and motor regions — comes from this programme of longitudinal neuroimaging research and subsequent studies, rather than from the 1999 paper alone.

Predict before the findings: which brain systems would you expect to show the most prolonged development?
Networks supporting planning, inhibition and decision-making — these are among the slowest abilities to emerge. Later longitudinal neuroimaging research showed higher-order association regions maturing later than primary sensory and motor regions; the pattern is gradual and varies across individuals.
Why does repeated scanning of the SAME children beat comparing different ages of different children?
Longitudinal design follows the same individuals over time, allowing researchers to measure within-person developmental change. This reduces the cohort-confounding problem found in simple cross-sectional age comparisons — although attrition, repeated-testing effects and the time/cost of long studies are limitations to remember.
Does longitudinal MRI evidence showing brain development across adolescence prove that these structural changes CAUSE adolescent risk-taking?
No. MRI can demonstrate that brain structure changes across development, but observing structural development alongside age does not by itself establish that a particular brain change causes a complex behaviour such as risk-taking. Behaviour is influenced by interacting biological, cognitive and sociocultural factors. To make stronger brain–behaviour claims, researchers need evidence directly linking neural measures with behavioural measures while considering alternative explanations.

Grey matter contains many neuronal cell bodies, dendrites and synapses. Grey-matter measures change across development partly because of processes including synaptic development and pruning.

White matter contains many myelinated axons connecting brain regions. White-matter development generally continues across adolescence and young adulthood as neural communication pathways become more efficiently organised.

Brain maturation therefore involves both refinement of local neural connections and continued development of communication between regions — developmental change is NOT simply “brain growth.”

  • Prolonged development of prefrontal and wider control networks may contribute to developmental differences in planning, inhibition and decision-making
  • However, adolescent risk-taking is influenced by multiple interacting factors, including reward sensitivity, peers, social context, experience and individual differences
  • Maturation creates developmental windows of maximum impact → critical/sensitive periods (next lessons)

Brain development contributes to behaviour — but a single brain region alone does not cause a complex behaviour.

Strength — Longitudinal evidence: repeated MRI scans allow researchers to observe developmental change within individuals rather than inferring development from different age groups.

Strength — Objective measurement: structural MRI provides quantitative measurements of brain anatomy.

Limitation — Causality: structural change occurring with age does not prove that a particular neural change causes a particular behavioural change.

Limitation — Reductionism: explaining adolescent behaviour only through brain maturation can neglect peers, culture, learning, motivation and situational factors.

Limitation — Individual differences: developmental trajectories are averages; individuals vary substantially, so brain-development findings should not be used to judge the abilities of a particular teenager simply from their age.

A 16-year-old makes excellent decisions when completing a quiet classroom problem but takes a dangerous risk when friends are watching. A student says, “His prefrontal cortex must not be developed enough.” Evaluate this explanation.
Brain maturation may contribute because executive-control networks continue developing during adolescence, but the explanation is incomplete. The teenager demonstrated effective decision-making in the classroom, showing that ability depends on context. Peer presence, reward sensitivity, emotional arousal, experience and individual differences may interact with developing control systems. A stronger psychological explanation therefore treats adolescent behaviour as the result of interacting biological and social influences rather than an “immature brain” alone.

Synaptogenesis & Synaptic Pruning

How the brain builds and refines its connections — counted synapse by synapse.

Synaptogenesis

The infant brain massively overproduces synaptic connections — far more than will survive to adulthood.

Pruning

Connections that are repeatedly activated tend to be strengthened or stabilised, while others are reduced. “Use it or lose it” is a useful shorthand, but pruning is not controlled by experience alone. Genetic programmes, neural activity and experience interact in determining which connections are retained.

Refinement chain

Refinement chain: synaptic overproduction → patterns of neural activity and experience influence which connections are repeatedly activated → some connections are stabilised/strengthened while others are eliminated → neural networks become more specialised and efficient.

Pruning does not mean the developing brain simply becomes less capable. It is selective refinement: unnecessary or inefficient connections are reduced while frequently coordinated networks become more specialised. More synapses is not automatically a better brain.

Aim

To count how synaptic density actually changes with age in different cortical regions.

Who was studied

Post-mortem human brain tissue spanning infancy to adulthood (decades of collected cases, examined region by region under electron microscopy).

Procedure

  • Synapses per unit of tissue counted in visual cortex and prefrontal cortex at each age.
  • Density curves plotted per region to locate peaks and declines.

Key findings

  • Visual-cortex synapse density peaks within the first year at roughly 150% of adult levels, then prunes down.
  • Prefrontal density peaks years later and prunes gradually through adolescence.

Conclusion

The cortex blooms then prunes, with region-specific timing. The pattern is consistent with both biological developmental processes and experience-dependent activity shaping which connections are retained.

Before the numbers: should visual cortex and prefrontal cortex hit their synaptic peaks at the same age? Why or why not?
No. Sensory regions serve early needs (recognising faces, objects) while prefrontal functions mature slowly. Different jobs → different schedules — exactly what the counts showed.
What does “150% of adult density” actually mean for a toddler’s learning?
Toddlers carry far more connections than adults, providing abundant raw material for refinement. Patterns of neural activity, shaped partly by experience, contribute to which connections are strengthened, stabilised or eliminated during development.
Post-mortem counting is powerful but limited. Name the main constraint.
Each brain contributes one age-point after death; the curves therefore come from many different individuals, and no behaviour was measured alongside the anatomy — so these data cannot show how synaptic density causes particular behaviours.
  • Language exposure strengthens language circuits
  • Motor practice refines motor pathways
  • Emotional experience shapes regulation circuits
  • Sensory deprivation reduces sensory pathways

Direct bridge to critical and sensitive periods: timing decides what gets built vs refined.

A student says, “Children who receive more stimulation keep more synapses, so their brains must become better developed.” What is wrong with this explanation?
More synapses are not automatically better. Healthy development involves both synaptogenesis and selective pruning. Experience can influence patterns of neural activity, but pruning is also biologically regulated. Efficient development involves refining neural networks, not simply preserving the maximum number of connections.

Strength: physical mechanism connecting experience and neural activity to brain structure — not metaphor.

Limitation: much supporting detail comes from animal work; human mechanisms are still being refined.

Limitation: pruning is not simply good — over-pruning has been implicated in some developmental disorders.

  2. Pruning = selective reduction/refinement of connections.
  3. Development involves both biological regulation and experience-dependent neural activity.
  4. Different cortical regions follow different developmental timelines.
  5. Huttenlocher & Dabholkar (1997) provide direct post-mortem anatomical evidence, but these data do not establish how synaptic density causes particular behaviours.

The Developing Brain: Key Regions

Brain regions whose development helps explain psychological change.

The prefrontal cortex forms part of networks involved in executive functions such as planning, inhibition, working memory and decision-making. These networks continue developing across adolescence and into young adulthood.
Example: an adolescent can solve abstract maths problems yet sometimes struggle to weigh long-term consequences in a socially intense moment — the skill and the context are both part of the explanation.

The prefrontal cortex makes important contributions to executive control, but it does not single-handedly control behaviour.

The hippocampus is especially important for the formation and organisation of certain long-term memories and for spatial processing. It continues developing during childhood and adolescence and interacts with other brain regions during learning and memory.
Example: healthy development of memory networks supports the gradual accumulation of knowledge across the school years.

Prolonged or severe stress has been associated with changes in hippocampal structure and memory-related functioning. Stress hormones such as cortisol are one possible mechanism, but human evidence does not justify saying that cortisol simply “damages the hippocampus” in every stressed child.

The amygdala contributes to detecting and processing emotionally significant information, including potential threats and rewards. It interacts with prefrontal and other neural systems involved in interpreting and regulating emotional responses.
Example: a critical text can quickly produce an emotional reaction, but evaluating whether the message was really meant as criticism involves wider networks.

Brain regions do not work independently. For example, successful emotional regulation can involve communication between prefrontal regions, the amygdala and other neural systems. Memory similarly depends on interactions between the hippocampus and distributed cortical networks.

Localisation helps identify important contributions of particular structures, but reducing a complex behaviour to one region is an example of biological reductionism.

During adolescence, systems involved in emotion, reward and executive control follow different developmental trajectories. Their interaction may contribute to changes in emotional responding and risk-related decision-making, particularly in socially or emotionally intense situations.

A 15-year-old becomes extremely angry after receiving a critical message online. Someone explains, “That happened because the teenager’s amygdala is developed but the prefrontal cortex is not.” Evaluate this explanation.
The explanation identifies relevant neural systems but is too reductionist. Emotional processing and executive control involve interacting networks, and the prefrontal cortex is developing rather than simply “undeveloped.” The teenager’s interpretation of the message, previous experiences, peer context, emotional arousal and individual differences may also influence the response. Brain maturation may contribute without being the sole cause.
A brain-imaging study finds that adolescents showing greater activity in a particular region also take more risks in a laboratory task. Can researchers conclude that activity in that region CAUSED the risk-taking?
Not from an association alone. The neural measure and behaviour may be related, but correlation does not establish that one caused the other. Other neural systems, individual differences and situational factors may contribute.

Strength: brain imaging and developmental neuroscience allow researchers to examine biological changes associated with psychological development.

Limitation — Reductionism: complex behaviours cannot usually be explained by one brain structure.

Limitation — Causality: associations between brain development and behaviour do not automatically demonstrate causation.

Limitation — Individual differences: developmental patterns describe averages; individuals do not all mature at identical rates.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Prefrontal regions contribute to executive-control networks.
  2. The hippocampus is important for memory formation and spatial processing.
  3. The amygdala contributes to processing emotionally significant information.
  4. These structures work through interacting neural networks rather than independently.
  5. Different neural systems follow different developmental trajectories.
  6. Brain development can contribute to adolescent behaviour without determining it.
  7. Avoid biological reductionism and unjustified causal claims.

Neuroplasticity & Development

Why the developing brain is especially responsive to experience.

Neuroplasticity is the brain’s capacity to change its structure, organisation or functioning in response to development, experience, learning or injury. Plasticity occurs across the lifespan but varies by neural system, age and developmental timing — the brain is a construction site, not a finished building.

A familiar illustration is experience-dependent change in adulthood: London taxi drivers develop larger posterior hippocampal volumes after years of navigation practice. This illustrates adult plasticity, but it does not by itself prove that navigation practice alone caused every observed difference.

Experience-expectant plasticity

The developing brain is biologically prepared to receive common species-typical experiences during particular developmental periods. Examples: patterned visual input, exposure to language, ordinary social interaction. The brain “expects” these inputs because they are normally available during development.

Experience-dependent plasticity

Neural change produced by experiences that differ between individuals. Examples: learning an instrument, extensive navigation practice, learning specialised skills.

Experience-expectant = common input expected during development. Experience-dependent = individual experiences produce individual neural adaptations.

Experience-expectant plasticity connects directly to the upcoming critical and sensitive period pages: the developing brain is primed for particular inputs at particular times.

Two children both begin violin lessons at age 8. After five years, one shows much greater skill and different patterns of brain activation. Can we conclude that age 8 was a critical period for violin learning?
No. This is better understood as experience-dependent plasticity. Their brains can adapt through repeated individual experience, but the differences could reflect amount and quality of practice, teaching, motivation, prior ability and other factors. A critical period requires evidence that the relevant ability depends on input within a restricted developmental window.

Aim

To test experimentally whether housing conditions affect measures of brain development in laboratory rats.

Who was studied

Laboratory rats — litters matched, then randomly assigned to housing conditions.

Procedure

  • Enriched condition (EC): groups of ~10–12 rats with daily toy rotation.
  • Impoverished condition (IC): single rats, no toys.
  • After several weeks: brains weighed, cortex measured, enzyme activity analysed.

Key findings

  • EC rats developed heavier, thicker cortex than IC rats.
  • Greater acetylcholinesterase activity (chemical marker of transmission) in EC brains.

Note: “enrichment” combined several features — social interaction, sensory stimulation, opportunities for exploration and physical activity. We can therefore infer that the overall housing manipulation affected neural development, but it is harder to identify which specific component produced the effect.

Conclusion

Experimentally manipulated housing conditions produced measurable differences in the rats’ brains, providing causal evidence that environmental experience can influence neural development in this animal model.

Predict the direction before results: which group — enriched or impoverished — should end up with thicker cortex, and why does random assignment matter for saying so?
Enriched, because stimulation drives circuit use and retention. Random assignment helps distribute pre-existing individual differences across conditions, reducing systematic participant differences as an alternative explanation — it does not guarantee that every difference disappears.
These are rats. State the generalisation problem precisely.
Rat cortex differs from human cortex in scale and complexity; enrichment toys hardly equal parenting or education. The study demonstrates the PRINCIPLE experimentally; applying magnitudes to children is extrapolation.
Why does Rosenzweig et al. provide stronger causal evidence than simply comparing adults who choose stimulating lifestyles with adults who do not?
Rosenzweig et al. experimentally manipulated housing conditions and used assignment procedures to reduce systematic pre-existing differences. An observational comparison of humans would contain self-selection and many possible confounding variables. However, the animal experiment has lower direct generalisability to complex human environments.

Developing brains often show substantial plasticity, but the degree and consequences of plasticity depend on the neural system involved, developmental timing, type of experience or injury, and individual differences.

  • Critical/sensitive periods = windows of heightened plasticity
  • Language and music are typically easier to acquire during peak windows
  • High plasticity creates both opportunity and vulnerability: experience can support adaptation, but adverse or missing input during sensitive developmental periods may also have lasting effects
  • Plasticity does not mean unlimited recovery or guaranteed rewiring
  • Genetic/biological constraints remain
  • Plasticity varies across neural systems and across development
  • Some effects of early deprivation can persist — though later improvement is still possible
Plasticity is often sold as “the brain can do anything.” Give the two-part correction.
(1) Biological constraints bound what experience modifies; (2) timing matters — windows close and costs become harder to reverse. Plasticity means RESPONSIVENESS, not unlimited rewrites.

  1. Neuroplasticity = capacity for neural change.
  2. Experience-expectant plasticity involves common developmental input; experience-dependent plasticity reflects individual experience.
  3. Plasticity occurs across the lifespan but varies with developmental timing and neural system.
  4. Rosenzweig et al. provides experimental animal evidence that environmental conditions can alter brain development.
  5. Random assignment strengthens causal inference but does not magically eliminate every difference.
  6. Animal evidence gives stronger experimental control but requires caution when generalising to humans.
  7. Plasticity means responsiveness — NOT unlimited change or guaranteed recovery.

Critical and Sensitive Periods

Why timing of experience matters for development.

The Core Idea

Experience matters throughout development, but it often matters most during particular developmental windows. What arrives early — and what does not — can influence how a system develops.

Critical Period

A relatively restricted developmental window during which particular biological or environmental input is necessary for typical development of a specific system. Severe disruption during this period can produce substantial and potentially lasting effects.

Critical periods are strongest and clearest for particular biological and sensory systems. It does not follow that every psychological ability has a rigid developmental deadline.

Real-life example: early visual development.

A congenital cataract is a clouding of the eye’s lens present from birth. If it severely blocks patterned visual input during early development, normal visual processing may be disrupted. Earlier treatment generally produces better visual outcomes because appropriate visual input reaches the developing system sooner. Later correction may not completely reverse developmental effects, particularly after prolonged early deprivation.

Sensitive Period

A developmental period during which the brain or behaviour is especially responsive to particular experiences. Learning or development can still occur later, but the timing of experience can influence the developmental outcome.

Real-life example: the Bucharest Early Intervention Project (BEIP).

After Romania’s communist regime fell, thousands of children were found living in severely deprived institutions. Many received the basics of care — food, shelter, hygiene — but very little of the stable, responsive, one-to-one caregiving and stimulation that typically supports development. This unusual situation offered researchers a rare window into how severe early deprivation affects later development.

Very important: the researchers did not create this deprivation, and they did not place these children in the institutions. The institutional neglect already existed before the research began. BEIP’s real question was whether moving an already-deprived child into stable foster care could promote recovery — and whether the age of that placement mattered.

Three groups

BEIP studied children who had spent substantial time in Romanian institutions. After an initial assessment, many were randomly assigned either to remain in institutional care (care as usual) or to be moved into specially created foster care. A comparison group of children who had never been institutionalised was also studied. Because children were randomly assigned to foster care or care as usual, later differences between those two groups cannot easily be explained away by pre-existing differences — this is what gives BEIP unusually strong causal evidence for a teaching page about sensitive periods.

What did they measure?

Children were followed over time and assessed across several areas — cognitive ability, language, social and emotional behaviour, and aspects of brain development. Measuring multiple outcomes matters, because deprivation and recovery do not necessarily affect every system in the same way or at the same speed.

Before reading the findings — predict: if some children were placed into foster care early in life and others remained institutionalised for longer, which group would you expect to show stronger cognitive recovery on average, and why is this an empirical question rather than a certainty?
It is reasonable to predict that earlier placement into stable, responsive foster care supports better cognitive recovery, because it gives the developing brain more time inside an environment better equipped to provide the stimulation that supports development. But this is an empirical question, not a certainty — the answer observed depends on the data, and many individual and contextual factors influence the outcome.

On average, children placed into foster care showed better developmental outcomes than children who remained institutionalised longer — for example, higher cognitive performance and, in general, fewer developmental difficulties across the measures studied.

Timing mattered, but flexibly. Children placed into foster care at younger ages tended to show the strongest recovery. However, children placed later could still improve. There was not one single universal age after which recovery became impossible — timing effects differed across developmental outcomes, and development continued to change after early childhood.

A concrete way to think about it. Imagine two illustrative children (these are teaching examples, not named participants). Child A is placed into stable foster care early in infancy; Child B remains in institutional care for several more years before being placed. On average the research suggests Child A makes stronger and more complete cognitive progress than Child B. The key teaching point is not that Child B cannot improve — Child B can and often does — but that earlier placement was associated with more complete recovery for more of the measures.

Key sentence: children who remained institutionalised longer STILL improved when eventually placed in foster care — just less completely.

That pattern is the reason BEIP counts as evidence for a sensitive period rather than a strict critical period. A strict critical period would imply that missing the early window makes typical development largely impossible afterwards. BEIP instead shows that development remained possible after the optimal early window had narrowed — children placed later still improved, they just recovered less completely on average. This is the classic “heightened responsiveness rather than an absolute deadline” pattern.

Natural deprivation plus intervention: it studies a real, severe deprivation that researchers could never create, while still testing the effect of a supportive intervention on children who had already experienced it.

Random assignment to foster care versus care as usual strengthens causal inference about the intervention far more than simply comparing children whose families independently chose different types of care.

Longitudinal follow-up and multiple outcome measures let researchers examine whether effects hold over time and across different areas of development.

Careful limits: random assignment strengthens conclusions about the overall foster-care package, not about one isolated ingredient (affection, language, stimulation). And ethical constraints meant researchers could only study children already in institutions — the deprivation itself could not be generated for research.

Student check: If a child who remained institutionalised for several years later improved substantially after foster placement, does that DISPROVE the existence of a sensitive period for development?
No. A sensitive period does not mean development becomes impossible afterwards — it means timing influences how readily or completely some abilities develop. Late improvement is fully consistent with a sensitive-period account; what would be harder to reconcile with one is evidence that timing made no difference to outcomes at all.

Critical period = relatively restricted window; missing necessary input can severely disrupt typical development.
Sensitive period = heightened responsiveness; development remains possible outside the period.

Childhood Environment & Brain Development

How early environments — especially severe deprivation — can influence development.

Development reflects an interaction between biology and environment. The brain is shaped through experience and plasticity. A useful way to think about this is as a chain:

Early environment → amount and quality of stimulation and responsive caregiving → repeated patterns of neural activation → experience-dependent strengthening and refinement of developing systems → possible differences in cognitive, emotional and social development.

This chain explains how early conditions can influence development without assuming that any one experience mechanically produces one particular brain outcome. Individual development is the product of many interacting influences.

Supportive / Enriched Environments

Supportive environments typically provide responsive caregiving, individual attention, language interaction, play and exploration, predictable relationships and opportunities for learning. These repeatedly activate developing cognitive and emotional systems in ways that can support their growth.

Severely Deprived Environments

Severe deprivation is not simply “fewer toys.” It commonly involves very limited responsive caregiving, little individual attention, low stimulation, unstable relationships, and chronic stress or neglect. For human development, responsive social and emotional interaction is especially important — its absence can mean developing systems receive much less of the experience they normally rely on.

Focus for this page: severe psychosocial deprivation provides important evidence about what can happen when children receive unusually limited responsive caregiving, social interaction and cognitive stimulation.

Children who had experienced severe early institutional deprivation in Romania were followed over time after some experienced a major improvement in caregiving through placement in specially created foster care. Researchers examined their developmental outcomes.

You have already studied this project in detail on the earlier page about sensitive periods. Here it is used as a human example of neuroplasticity rather than repeated as a full study.

The important pattern was:

  • severe early deprivation → developmental difficulties
  • but improved caregiving → meaningful developmental recovery
  • and, for several outcomes, earlier intervention → stronger developmental benefit.

Why this connects to neuroplasticity: the environment changed, and development subsequently changed. Development is therefore not simply fixed by early biological maturation. At the same time, earlier intervention often produced stronger outcomes, showing that plasticity is greater during some developmental periods than others.

Put together, the findings spell out a simple chain:

Severe deprivation → important developmental input is reduced → developing systems receive atypical experience.

Supportive foster care → richer and more responsive environmental input → developmental systems remain capable of change → recovery becomes possible.

But longer deprivation → some developmental systems may become less responsive → later recovery may be less complete for some outcomes.

This is why BEIP is useful for understanding both:

  • Neuroplasticity — development can change in response to experience.
  • Sensitive periods — the timing of that experience affects how much change occurs.

Careful: this does not mean every BEIP outcome was a direct measurement of neural structural change. Most outcomes studied were developmental (cognitive, social, emotional) rather than direct measures of brain structure, so be cautious about turning them into claims about synaptic density or specific brain regions.

A child receives responsive caregiving, language interaction and stimulation, while another experiences severe institutional deprivation. What can we defensibly predict about their development?
A child receiving responsive caregiving, language interaction and stimulation repeatedly activates developing cognitive and emotional systems, providing experience that can support their development. Severe deprivation removes much of this expected input and may disrupt development. BEIP shows that improving the environment can lead to substantial recovery, demonstrating continuing plasticity — but this comparison does not directly measure specific changes in synaptic density or pruning.
Does neuroplasticity mean a neglected child’s brain can always recover fully with later enrichment?
No — plasticity changes across development, and sensitive periods mean some systems are harder to change after early windows. Later enrichment helps but cannot guarantee full recovery of lost potential. The BEIP data support partial but not necessarily complete catch-up.

What Should I Remember?

  1. The developing brain is plastic: experience can influence development.
  2. Severe deprivation can disrupt development because children receive less of the responsive caregiving and stimulation normally available during important developmental periods.
  3. BEIP showed that development can improve substantially when the caregiving environment improves, demonstrating continuing plasticity.
  4. Timing matters: earlier intervention often produces stronger recovery for some outcomes, connecting neuroplasticity with sensitive periods.

Stage vs Continuous Development

The fundamental question about how development proceeds.

  • Development follows an organised sequence
  • Each stage builds on the previous
  • Qualitative shifts, not just growth
  • Piaget and Erikson are stage theorists

Example: a concrete-operational child can sort objects by size but cannot systematically test all variable combinations in a science task; a formal-operational adolescent can — a difference in KIND of thinking, not just amount of knowledge.

Quantitative vs Qualitative Change

Quantitative (continuous): more, faster, or greater amount.

Qualitative (stage): a different form or organisation of thinking.

Piaget proposed that children move through qualitatively different stages of cognitive development: a child in a later stage does not simply possess MORE knowledge — the organisation of thinking itself has changed.

Example: a child remembering progressively more items as working-memory capacity increases could illustrate quantitative continuous change. A child beginning to understand conservation in a fundamentally different way represents the kind of qualitative change Piaget proposed.

  • Gradual, incremental change
  • No sharp boundaries between periods
  • Learning and environment drive smooth accumulation
  • Associated with information-processing approaches

Piaget = stage theorist; Vygotsky emphasises continuous social learning. Your framework shapes which evidence you seek and how you interpret it.

Reality: some domains show stage-like transitions, others change gradually — the debate is emphasis, not either/or.

Not all or nothing: different aspects of development may show different patterns. Some abilities appear to change gradually, while others may show periods of relatively rapid qualitative reorganisation.

Modern developmental psychology therefore often asks which pattern best describes a particular ability rather than assuming that all human development follows one universal pattern.

A child says her first word around 12 months, then vocabulary grows slowly — until a sudden explosion of two-word sentences near 18 months. Stage or continuous?
Both, honestly: vocabulary accumulates gradually (continuous) while the grammar jump looks stage-like. Development often mixes modes — citing BOTH patterns earns evaluation credit.

A Different Question from Critical/Sensitive Periods

Stage vs continuous development asks HOW developmental change occurs. Critical/sensitive periods ask WHEN particular experiences have especially important developmental effects. These are different questions.

Example: a child showing gradual improvement in working-memory capacity concerns continuity of development; a child experiencing poorer visual development after early visual deprivation concerns developmental timing.

A researcher measures vocabulary size every month from age 1 to 6 and finds perfectly steady growth. Does this prove development IS continuous?
No — it shows THIS measure changed gradually. Underlying reorganisations (grammar rules, category learning) may still occur in jumps that smooth averages hide. Measure choice shapes which pattern you see.
A researcher tests children once each year and finds that performance suddenly increases between ages 6 and 7. Does this prove that children entered a new cognitive stage at age 7?
No. The result is consistent with relatively rapid developmental change, but it does not by itself prove a distinct stage. The apparent jump could depend on the task, measurement intervals, practice, schooling or other developmental changes. Researchers need evidence that thinking has changed qualitatively rather than simply improved quantitatively.
From ages 5 to 10, Maya becomes steadily faster and more accurate at solving increasingly difficult memory problems. Another student says, “She must have moved through several cognitive stages.” Is that conclusion justified?
Not necessarily. Gradual increases in speed, accuracy or capacity are more consistent with continuous quantitative development. Evidence for a stage would require a qualitative change in the organisation or type of thinking, not simply better performance.
A younger child believes a taller glass contains more water. Later, the child understands that the amount remains the same despite the change in appearance.
Piaget interpreted this as evidence of qualitative cognitive change associated with movement toward concrete operational thinking. However, researchers must still test whether the change is genuinely stage-like or whether performance develops more gradually than the task suggests.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Stage theories propose relatively distinct qualitative changes in development.
  2. Continuous models propose more gradual quantitative or incremental change.
  3. Piaget is an important example of a stage theory because he proposed changes in the organisation of children’s thinking.
  4. Stage vs continuous development concerns the PATTERN of developmental change.
  5. Critical/sensitive periods concern the TIMING and developmental importance of experience — they are not the same debate.
  6. Different psychological abilities may show different developmental patterns.
  7. Whether development appears stage-like can partly depend on how psychologists measure it.

Piaget’s Theory of Cognitive Development

How children actively construct knowledge through stages of thinking.

Children are active constructors of knowledge, building understanding through interaction with the world.

  • Schema: mental framework organising information
    Example: a toddler’s “dog” schema includes four legs, fur, tail, barking.
  • Assimilation: fitting new info into existing schemas
    Example: seeing a Great Dane and calling it a “big dog” fits the existing dog schema.
  • Accommodation: modifying schemas when info does not fit
    Example: being told a cat is NOT a dog forces creation of a separate cat category.
  • Disequilibrium → Equilibration: discomfort resolved by adjusting schemas upward

Mechanism: experience → disequilibrium → adjustment → richer schema → equilibrium at a higher level.

How Piaget gathered evidence

Mainly through observation and clinical interviews, including detailed daily observation of his own three infants (Laurent, Lucienne and Jacqueline) — e.g., tracking when each first searched for a hidden object. Rich and insightful, but small, unrepresentative samples with interpretations made by the same researcher who designed the tasks.

  1. Sensorimotor (0–2): knowing through action; object permanence develops
  2. Preoperational (2–7): symbolic thought limited by egocentrism; no conservation
  3. Concrete operational (7–11): logical thinking about concrete situations; conservation, classification, seriation
  4. Formal operational (11+): abstract, hypothetical, systematic reasoning

Focus on the psychological LOGIC of each stage — ages are approximate guides.

Strength: showed children think QUALITATIVELY differently from adults — transformed education worldwide.

Limitation: underestimated young children (later research, next lessons).

Limitation: stage boundaries blur; progression varies by culture and domain.

How does equilibration drive cognitive growth?
When experience cannot be assimilated, disequilibrium motivates accommodation — schemas rebuild until the world makes sense again, at a higher level of understanding. Learning is driven by this tension-resolution cycle.
A toddler calls every four-legged animal “doggie” until corrected at a farm. Name the processes in order.
Assimilation (horse → dog schema), disequilibrium (correction conflicts), accommodation (new “farm animals” distinctions), equilibration at a higher level.
Why might Piaget’s own-children observations over- OR under-state abilities?
Over: privileged, verbally rich infants may advance early. Under: unfamiliar testers/tasks suppress performance. Either way, n=3 limits universality — which later research exploited.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Children construct knowledge actively via schemas: assimilation vs accommodation resolves disequilibrium.
  2. Four stages: sensorimotor → preoperational → concrete → formal; logic matters more than dates.
  3. Evidence base: observations/clinical interviews incl. his own three infants — rich but unrepresentative.
  4. Legacy: qualitative difference confirmed; timing and universality challenged.

Assimilation: interpret something new THROUGH existing understanding (dog schema absorbs a Great Dane).

Accommodation: new experience will not fit, so the SCHEMA changes (a separate “cat” schema forms).

Equilibration: the drive resolving that tension, upgrading thinking overall.

Scenario 1: A child who has only seen small dogs meets a Great Dane and calls it a “big dog.”
Assimilation. The Great Dane fits the existing dog schema — nothing about the schema needed to change, only scaled up.
Scenario 2: The child sees a cat, is told “that is NOT a dog,” and builds a new cat category.
Accommodation. The old schema failed; a new schema had to be constructed — genuine structural change.

Baillargeon (1987) — the drawbridge study

Aim: Test object permanence in infants too young to search manually.

Who: 24 infants aged 5–6 months.

Procedure: Infants were first habituated to a drawbridge rotating 180° like a wall. Then a box was placed behind its path. On test trials the bridge either stopped where a solid box should block it (possible) or rotated straight through it (impossible). Looking time was recorded.

Findings — what researchers observed: Infants looked significantly LONGER at the impossible event — an event that appeared physically impossible or inconsistent with the continued existence of the hidden object.

What researchers inferred: the longer looking suggests that infants had an expectation about how the hidden object should behave. Longer looking does not directly “prove” that infants possess a complete adult-like understanding of object permanence.

Conclusion: Baillargeon’s findings suggest that some expectations about objects may develop earlier than Piaget’s traditional search tasks indicated (by ~5 months, with follow-ups suggesting ~3.5 months).

Why this challenges Piaget (a measurement issue): Piaget often required infants or children to demonstrate understanding through an active behavioural response. Violation-of-expectation methods reduce some of these motor and task demands by measuring looking behaviour. This raises an important measurement question: did Piaget underestimate children’s knowledge because his tasks required abilities beyond the concept being tested?

Evaluation: Strengths — removes the searching/motor confound entirely; tight experimental control; replicated across variants. Limitations — longer looking must still be INTERPRETED (alternative: perceptual novelty rather than physical reasoning); artificial laboratory display differs from natural object play.

Commit first: WHY does measuring LOOKING TIME solve Piaget’s original testing problem with young infants?
Piaget required infants to SEARCH — but infants lack coordinated reaching and grasping, so failure may reflect motor immaturity, not ignorance. Looking needs no motor skill, which separates knowledge from performance. This is exactly a measurement point: task demands can hide competence.
A critic says infants stared because the bridge stopping looked odd, not because they understood objects. How could researchers answer this?
Control conditions: the same stop looks equally odd when NO box is present yet does not produce extra looking in variants where reasoning is impossible — plus replication with different stimuli and measures strengthens the object-permanence reading.
An infant looks longer when an object appears to pass through a solid barrier. A researcher concludes, “The infant fully understands the physical laws governing solid objects.” What is wrong with this conclusion?
Looking time is the measured behaviour; understanding is the psychological construct being inferred. Longer looking suggests that the event violated an expectation, but it does not tell researchers exactly how sophisticated that expectation is. Researchers must avoid claiming more about cognition than the measure can demonstrate.

Meltzoff (1988) — deferred imitation

14-month-olds reproduced a sequence of novel actions they had watched 24 hours earlier. Findings such as deferred imitation suggest that some representational and memory abilities may appear earlier than Piaget’s original stage account predicted — they do not discredit his broader theory.

Dasen (1994) — cross-cultural review

Reviewing conservation and spatial-task studies across cultures: non-Western children acquired concrete operations later or along different profiles (spatial tasks relatively stronger than quantity tasks) — suggesting schooling and cultural practice shape stage timing, challenging universality.

Precise conclusion: cross-cultural research suggests that the timing and expression of cognitive abilities can vary with schooling, experience and cultural practices. This challenges rigid claims that every child progresses through cognitive development in exactly the same way and at exactly the same age — it does not mean that biological maturation is irrelevant.

“While Baillargeon’s drawbridge study shows core competencies emerge far earlier than Piaget claimed, Dasen’s cross-cultural review shows their TIMING varies with environment — supporting Piaget’s qualitative insight while rejecting strict universal timetables.”

Why was Baillargeon’s finding significant for Piaget’s theory?
It demonstrated object permanence months earlier using a method immune to infant motor limitations — challenging Piaget’s TIMING while confirming his deeper claim that infants actively represent objects.

  4. Cross-cultural evidence challenges rigid universal timing.
  5. Evidence challenging one prediction does not invalidate an entire theory.

Vygotsky’s Sociocultural Theory

How social interaction and culture shape cognitive development.

Core claim: Vygotsky argued that cognitive development is fundamentally shaped by social interaction and culture. Children learn culturally developed ways of thinking through interaction with more knowledgeable people and gradually internalise these tools.

  • Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD): the range between what a learner can currently accomplish independently and what they can accomplish with appropriate guidance or collaboration
    Example: a child cannot build a tower alone but CAN with a parent pointing out base pieces first.
  • Scaffolding: temporary, adjustable support withdrawn as competence grows
    Example: parent does puzzle WITH child, then just points, then only encourages — support fades.
  • MKO: an MKO is someone who has greater knowledge or skill relevant to the particular task — for example a teacher, parent, skilled peer or coach (not necessarily an adult)
    Example: a peer who has already mastered the skill can be the MKO.
  • Cultural tools: language, symbols, practices mediating thought
    Example: counting systems, writing, maps — each changes what children can think about.
  • Language: private speech becomes inner speech — the engine of reasoning
    Example: children narrate tasks aloud before thinking silently.

CAN DO ALONE → CAN DO WITH SUPPORT = ZPD → CANNOT YET DO EVEN WITH SUPPORT

Effective teaching targets the ZPD rather than simply giving the learner answers.

Terminology check: Vygotsky developed the concept of the ZPD. The term “scaffolding” was introduced later by Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976) to describe temporary support that helps a learner complete a task that would initially be difficult independently. The two terms are strongly compatible but not identical: the ZPD is the range of potential performance with assistance; scaffolding is the support provided to help the learner operate successfully within that range.

How scaffolding works:

  1. Identify what the learner can currently do.
  2. Provide enough assistance for the learner to progress.
  3. Adjust support according to the learner’s performance.
  4. Gradually reduce support as competence increases.
  5. The learner eventually performs more independently.

Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976) — introducing the term “scaffolding”

Aim: To investigate how tutors support young children attempting a difficult construction task.

Who: Young children approximately 3–5 years old (30 children aged 3, 4 and 5, each working with one tutor).

Task: Children attempted to construct a complex three-dimensional structure (a wooden pyramid) from interlocking pieces while an adult tutor provided assistance.

Procedure: Researchers analysed how effective tutoring changed according to the child’s needs. Tutors used a level-based pattern of intervention — from general encouragement through specific instruction to direct demonstration — choosing the least intrusive level that kept the child engaged and progressing (“contingent instruction”).

Functions of effective tutoring: the influential analysis identified several functions of scaffolding, including: recruiting the learner’s interest; reducing the complexity or degrees of freedom of the task; maintaining direction toward the goal; highlighting important features; controlling frustration; demonstrating or modelling possible solutions.

Findings: Wood et al. showed that effective tutoring is responsive rather than simply giving constant amounts of help — assistance can be adjusted to what the child currently needs, and successful tutors progressively handed control back as competence grew.

Conclusion / connection: this provides a useful operational example of the type of guided learning later commonly linked with Vygotsky’s ZPD. The study itself did not set out to test Vygotsky’s theory, so it should not be described as proving it.

Evaluation: Strengths — direct observation of responsive tutoring; a systematic intervention framework transferable to classrooms. Limits — a single construction task; one-to-one adult tutoring differs from classroom ratios; individual differences in responsiveness were noted.

Match-to-need is the heart of scaffolding. What would happen if a tutor always intervened at the most direct level (doing the task for the child) with a capable 5-year-old?
Disengagement and a lost learning opportunity: the child never exercises the target skill. Contingency cuts both ways — too little support frustrates, too much removes the challenge the learner is ready for.

Social interaction → guided participation with an MKO → internalisation of cultural tools → independent competence. With repetition the scaffold disappears because the STRATEGY has moved inside.

Example: a teacher initially talks a child through the steps of solving a problem; later the child begins quietly talking themselves through the same steps; eventually the strategy becomes internal thought.

SOCIAL SPEECH → PRIVATE SPEECH → INTERNALISED THINKING

This illustrates Vygotsky’s claim that cognitive tools can move from the social level to the individual psychological level.

Strength — application: clear educational applications, because teaching can be adjusted to learners’ current abilities.

Strength — culture: explicitly recognises that development occurs within cultural and social contexts.

Limitation — measurement: the ZPD can be difficult to measure precisely because performance depends on the type and quality of assistance provided.

Limitation — individual development: gives less precise age-based predictions than Piaget, making some developmental claims harder to test.

That last point is a measurement and precision issue — it is not grounds for dismissing the theory as unscientific.

How does Vygotsky’s ZPD differ from Piaget’s view of the learner?
Piaget: the lone explorer whose development LEADS what they can learn (discovery after readiness). Vygotsky: learning WITH guidance LEADS development — the ZPD means assistance pulls cognition forward before maturation alone would allow it.

ZPD: paired problem-solving with teacher hints fading (scaffolding within the zone). Piaget: self-discovery stations where children reconstruct rules through their own actions without adult steering.
Leo cannot solve a maths problem alone. His teacher gives him the answer and then moves to the next question. Has the teacher necessarily used effective scaffolding?
No. Scaffolding involves temporary, responsive support that enables the learner to perform increasingly independently. Simply supplying the answer may complete the task without developing the learner’s ability. Effective support would provide prompts, modelling or guidance and then reduce assistance as Leo becomes more capable.
Mia can solve Level 3 problems independently. With hints from a skilled classmate she can solve Level 5 problems, but even extensive help does not allow her to solve Level 8 problems. Where is her ZPD?
Her ZPD lies approximately between the tasks she can already perform independently and the more difficult tasks she can successfully complete with appropriate assistance — in this example, around Levels 4–5. Level 8 currently lies beyond her demonstrated ZPD.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Vygotsky emphasised social and cultural influences on cognitive development.
  2. Cultural tools — especially language — shape thinking.
  3. ZPD = what a learner can accomplish with assistance beyond current independent performance.
  4. An MKO possesses task-relevant greater knowledge or skill.
  5. Scaffolding is temporary, adjustable support and was introduced as a term by Wood, Bruner & Ross, not Vygotsky.
  6. Socially supported strategies can become internalised.
  7. Wood et al. illustrates responsive tutoring but should not be described as direct proof of Vygotsky’s theory.

Sociocultural Factors in Development

How culture, language and social context shape development — through mechanisms, not just labels.

  • Caregiving: responsive interaction and co-regulation can help children gradually develop emotional and behavioural regulation
    Example: a caregiver calmly naming a child’s feelings while helping them soothe models how to label and re-regulate emotion.
  • Language & cultural tools: language and culturally transmitted systems such as counting, writing and symbols provide tools that children can internalise and use in thinking
    Example: finger counting and written numerals are cultural tools that support arithmetic before strategies become internalised.
  • Social norms: children learn culturally expected behaviours through modelling, reinforcement, correction and participation in social practices
    Example: greetings, turn-taking and politeness conventions are learned through everyday participation rather than being simply told.
  • Peers: peers provide opportunities for modelling, social comparison, cooperation, conflict resolution and identity development
    Example: negotiating rules during group games requires coordinating with others and practising conflict resolution.
  • Education: instruction can provide structured guidance, cultural knowledge and opportunities to practise cognitive strategies
    Example: schooling teaches literacy, abstract reasoning and formal testing conventions.

A mechanism explains HOW a factor influences development. Naming a sociocultural factor without its mechanism — “the environment matters” — explains nothing at HL.

Development emerges through interaction between biological characteristics and sociocultural experience.

Children with broadly similar developmental capacities may develop differently when they experience different languages, caregiving practices, educational opportunities, peer groups and cultural expectations.

Biological maturation creates developmental possibilities and constraints, while sociocultural experiences influence how abilities are learned, expressed and developed.

A toddler becomes better at calming down after months of a caregiver consistently naming the child’s emotions, modelling calm responses and helping the child regulate distress. Saying “the environment caused emotional development” is too vague. What mechanisms might explain the change?
The caregiver provides co-regulation, language for emotional states, modelling and repeated guided practice. Over time, the child may internalise some of these strategies and become increasingly able to regulate emotion independently. This explains HOW social experience may contribute to development rather than merely stating that the environment matters.
Two children grow up in cultures with different expectations about independence. One is encouraged to make individual choices early; the other is expected to consult family members. Does this show that biology is irrelevant to development?
No. Both children develop through biological maturation, but cultural practices can influence how abilities and behaviours are encouraged, interpreted and expressed. Development is better understood as interaction between biological and sociocultural influences rather than an either/or choice.

Strength — mechanisms: explains WHY developmental outcomes vary across contexts through concrete mechanisms rather than vague claims about “the environment”.

Strength — interaction: recognises that abilities are learned, expressed and developed within a culture, not in isolation from it.

Limitation — measurement: individual sociocultural factors are hard to isolate; several influences usually operate together.

Limitation — determinism: the same context does not produce identical outcomes for every child — individual differences and agency remain relevant.

How does this connect to the IB concept of Perspective?
Every developmental “normal” reflects a perspective: biological, cognitive and sociocultural accounts each illuminate part of the process — and cultural perspectives determine which outcomes societies value and cultivate.

  3. Culture can influence how psychological abilities are expressed and developed.
  4. Biological and sociocultural influences interact.

What Is Theory of Mind?

The ability to understand that others have mental states different from your own.

Theory of Mind (ToM) is the ability to represent and reason about mental states — such as beliefs, desires, intentions, knowledge and perspectives — in oneself and other people.

A crucial part of ToM is understanding that another person’s mental state can differ from reality and from your own.

Simple example: you know that a toy has been moved from a drawer to a cupboard. Your friend did not see it being moved. What does ToM allow you to understand?
That your friend can hold a false belief and still think the toy is in the drawer — so your friend is likely to look there. This is exactly the kind of understanding tested by false-belief tasks.

Theory of Mind: “What does another person think, know, believe or want?”

Empathy: “How does another person feel, and how do I understand or respond to that emotion?”

They can interact, but they are not the same psychological construct. A person can understand someone’s belief without necessarily sharing their emotion.

  • ∼2 years: sensitivity to others’ DESIRES and goals — an earlier ToM-related ability, distinct from success on standard explicit false-belief tasks
  • ∼3 years: some understanding that others have knowledge and beliefs, but typically still failing standard explicit false-belief tasks
  • 4–5 years (typically): performance on explicit false-belief tasks typically improves substantially, although performance varies with language ability, executive demands, task design and individual experience
    Example: Sally-Anne — saying Sally will look where she LEFT the marble.
  • Later: sarcasm, irony, second-order beliefs (“she thinks that he thinks…”)
    Example: understanding sarcasm requires modelling someone ELSE’S interpretation of reality.

Ages above describe typical patterns, not rigid deadlines. Earlier ToM-related abilities are not the same as passing a standard explicit false-belief task, and later abilities (sarcasm, second-order beliefs) are not measured by it either.

  • Predicting other people’s behaviour often requires representing what they know, want or believe
  • Understanding that others can be WRONG (false beliefs)
  • Understanding communication that depends on others’ perspectives (sarcasm, irony)

ToM contributes to many forms of social understanding because predicting behaviour often requires representing what another person knows, wants or believes.

Understanding another person’s beliefs can make strategic deception possible, but successful deception also requires other cognitive and social abilities.

Apply it: a friend hides your phone as a prank and rings it. You look under the cushion where YOU left it — wrong place. What just happened?
Your friend represented YOUR false belief about location in order to predict your search — their model of your belief differed from reality. This shows how understanding beliefs can make strategic deception possible; the prank works precisely because your belief, not reality, guided your behaviour.
Ben understands that his friend falsely believes a test is on Friday when it is actually on Thursday. Ben does not feel concerned about his friend. Does this mean Ben lacks Theory of Mind?
Not necessarily. Ben correctly represents his friend’s false belief, which demonstrates a ToM-related ability. His lack of emotional concern concerns empathy or motivation rather than necessarily showing an inability to represent another person’s mental state.
A 3-year-old fails a false-belief task. Can we conclude that the child has no understanding whatsoever of other minds?
No. Failure on one explicit task does not establish the complete absence of Theory of Mind. Performance may depend on language comprehension, memory, inhibition, attention and the specific demands of the task. Theory of Mind also contains multiple aspects that develop over time.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Theory of Mind involves representing mental states.
  2. People can hold beliefs that differ from reality and from our own beliefs.
  3. Explicit false-belief performance typically improves during early childhood.
  4. ToM is related to, but distinct from, empathy.
  5. ToM contributes to social understanding but does not single-handedly determine social behaviour.
  6. Performance on one task should not automatically be treated as a complete measure of a person’s social understanding.

False-Belief Tasks & Theory of Mind Research

How psychologists test whether children understand that others can be wrong.

False-belief tasks operationalise one aspect of Theory of Mind by testing whether a participant can predict behaviour based on another person’s belief rather than on reality.

REALITY: Where is the object actually?

BELIEF: Where does the character believe the object is?

PREDICTION: Where will the character act / search based on that belief?

Passing the task requires using the BELIEF to predict behaviour, not the child’s own knowledge of reality.

Study 1: Wimmer & Perner (1983) — the Maxi task

Aim: Test whether young children understand that others can hold beliefs contradicting reality.

Who: Children aged 4–6 years, tested individually in Austria.

Procedure: Each child heard a picture-story: Maxi puts chocolate in the green cupboard and leaves. While away, his mother moves it to the blue cupboard. Child asked: where will Maxi LOOK?

Findings: Most 4-year-olds said the BLUE cupboard (reality response); most 5–6-year-olds correctly said GREEN (belief response).

Conclusion (cautious): older preschool children were generally more successful than younger children at predicting behaviour based on a character’s false belief, suggesting substantial developmental change in explicit false-belief understanding during early childhood. This does not imply that a single birthday suddenly produces Theory of Mind: performance develops across childhood and can be influenced by task demands.

Evaluation: Strengths — replicated worldwide; clean manipulation of belief vs reality. Limits — story format demands narrative comprehension; single binary question.

Study 2: Baron-Cohen, Leslie & Frith (1985) — Sally–Anne

Aim: Apply the false-belief paradigm to investigate Theory of Mind in children with autism.

Who: 27 typically developing children (mean ~4;5), 14 with Down syndrome (mean ~10;11), and 20 autistic children (mean ~11;11).

Procedure: Sally places a marble in her basket and leaves. Anne moves the marble to a box while Sally is away. Sally returns. The child is asked where Sally will LOOK. Control questions (naming and reality) check the child understood the story.

The correct response is the original location — because Sally did not witness the move and therefore holds a false belief about the object’s location. The response is psychologically important because it shows the child predicted behaviour from Sally’s BELIEF rather than from reality.

Why the comparison groups: the comparison groups helped researchers investigate whether false-belief performance could simply be explained by general developmental/intellectual differences. The comparison helped address general intellectual/developmental ability as an alternative explanation, although no comparison group perfectly isolates a single psychological variable.

Findings: a substantially larger proportion of the autistic group failed the Sally–Anne false-belief question than the comparison groups: roughly 85% of typical and 86% of Down syndrome children pointed to the BASKET, but only about 20% of autistic children did — despite most passing the reality control.

Original (historical) interpretation: the researchers interpreted this pattern as evidence that autism may involve a relatively specific difficulty in representing other people’s beliefs — an influential account that became the “mind-blindness” hypothesis.

Evaluation: Strengths — an elegant three-group design investigating alternative explanations; highly influential and widely replicated. Limits — binary scoring may miss partial understanding; small subgroups; later work shows more variation in performance across autistic children and task versions.

  • Egocentrism (Piaget): spatial perspective-taking difficulty
  • False belief (ToM): understanding beliefs contradicting reality

Related but distinct: the Sally–Anne task is THE central ToM paradigm; the Three Mountains task belongs in Piaget evaluation.

Why do children under 4 typically FAIL these tasks?
They struggle to inhibit their OWN knowledge (an executive demand), track the character’s unseen relocation mentally, and process the narrative — several loads combine before belief-reasoning can express itself. Failure is therefore ambiguous evidence.

The additional comparison helped researchers examine whether poorer false-belief performance could simply reflect broader developmental or intellectual differences. Because many children in the Down syndrome group succeeded, the original researchers argued that the autistic group’s difficulty was relatively specific. However, comparison groups cannot perfectly isolate Theory of Mind from every other difference between participants.
A child knows where the object really is but answers that Sally will search there too. Can researchers conclude with certainty that the child cannot represent other people’s beliefs?
No. That is one possible interpretation, but false-belief performance also depends on language comprehension, memory, inhibition and task understanding. Researchers therefore treat the response as an operational measure of false-belief reasoning rather than a complete measurement of the child’s entire Theory of Mind.

Evaluating Theory of Mind

Strengths, limitations, and what the research actually tells us.

ToM-related abilities develop gradually rather than suddenly appearing at age 4.

Different ToM-related abilities emerge across development, and performance can vary substantially according to the demands of the task.

Easier tasks may reveal earlier competence, but that does not mean the same fully developed ability seen in harder tasks is present earlier.

  • Mental-state vocabulary predicts ToM progress
  • Callaghan et al. (2005): broadly similar developmental improvement in false-belief understanding across several cultural groups (Canada, Peru, Samoa and Thailand), while performance and timing were also influenced by cultural and communicative experience
    This suggests that some developmental patterns may be widespread while their expression remains shaped by sociocultural context.
  • Deaf children who experience delayed access to an accessible language may also show delayed performance on some explicit Theory of Mind tasks
    The relevant developmental factor is access to language and social communication, not deafness itself.
  • Tasks capture only ONE aspect of ToM (false belief)
  • Executive function and language load can confound scores
  • Cultural differences may reflect communication styles rather than ToM ability itself

Researchers must distinguish the construct they want to measure — Theory of Mind — from the particular behaviour used to operationalise it, such as answering a false-belief question.

A child fails Sally-Anne but explains perfectly why a hidden birthday gift stays surprising. Contradiction?
No — it exposes the measurement problem: single binary tasks can underestimate partial competence. Richer batteries reveal understanding that standard tasks miss.
  • Passing age varies across cultures — a genuine difference OR a communication-style / task-familiarity difference (Measurement)
  • Early research found group differences in explicit false-belief performance between autistic and comparison groups. However, autistic development is highly heterogeneous, and false-belief performance can also be influenced by language ability, executive functioning, development, experience and task demands.
  • One false-belief task should not be interpreted as a complete measure of an autistic person’s understanding of other minds.
Two cultures show different average ages for passing a standard false-belief task. Does this prove that children in one culture develop Theory of Mind later?
Not necessarily. The difference could reflect genuine developmental variation, but language, familiarity with the task, communication norms, schooling and testing expectations may also affect performance. Researchers must evaluate whether the measure functions similarly across cultures before concluding that the underlying psychological ability develops at different ages.
How does ToM connect to IB concepts Measurement and Perspective?
Measurement: whether ToM exists depends partly on HOW we test it (task demands, language load). Perspective: ToM IS perspective-taking — and studying it across cultures requires adopting participants’ perspectives too.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Theory of Mind develops across childhood rather than appearing as a single sudden switch.
  2. Language access and social experience can contribute to ToM development.
  3. Cross-cultural similarities suggest some widespread developmental patterns, but culture can influence their expression and measurement.
  4. False-belief tasks measure only one component of Theory of Mind.
  5. Language, executive functioning and task demands can affect performance.
  6. Autism is highly heterogeneous; early group findings should not be turned into universal claims about autistic people.
  7. Measurement matters: task performance is evidence about a construct, not the construct itself.

What Is the Self?

Introducing self-concept and how it develops.

  • Self-concept: beliefs and knowledge about who you are, including characteristics, abilities, roles and values
    Example: “I am hardworking, curious, a good friend, and I want to study psychology.”
  • Identity: an organised sense of who you are that can include personal characteristics, roles and group memberships
    Example: “I am a student, a swimmer, British, and a big sister.”
  • Self-esteem: the evaluative component of the self — how positively or negatively someone evaluates themselves
    Example: feeling proud after scoring well OR inadequate after social rejection.
  • Social identity: the part of self-concept connected to membership of social groups
    Example: identifying strongly as “a member of the debate team” shapes behaviour and self-view.

Self-concept develops through interaction between individual characteristics and experience. Relationships, culture, social feedback and personal reflection all contribute to how people understand themselves.

Biological characteristics and temperament can influence developmental experiences, but the content of self-concept develops substantially through social and cultural interaction.

  • Attachment → emotional security, relationship expectations
  • Enculturation → norms, values, cultural identity
  • Peers → comparison, belonging, exploration
  • Childhood experiences → early events shaping self-understanding

These are important influences explored in the following pages, not forces that completely determine the self. Their effects are shaped by individual characteristics, other experiences and the surrounding context.

Why is self-esteem only ONE component of the self?
Self-esteem evaluates worth; self-CONCEPT describes who you believe you are (roles, traits, identities). “I am good at mathematics” is a self-concept belief. “I feel proud of being good at mathematics” is the evaluative / self-esteem component. You can hold accurate self-knowledge with low esteem, or high esteem built on distorted self-knowledge — concept and evaluation are separable.
A teenager moves to a new country and begins changing how they speak, dress and describe themselves while also maintaining important aspects of their original cultural identity. Which developmental processes could be influencing the self?
Several processes may interact. Earlier enculturation shaped the teenager’s existing cultural knowledge and identity, while acculturation involves adapting to contact with a new cultural environment. Peer feedback, family relationships, language, social comparison and group belonging may also influence self-concept. We should not assume that one factor automatically “dominates.”
A student receives a poor exam result and says, “I failed one test, so I am an unintelligent person.” What distinction about the self is useful here?
One experience can influence self-evaluation, but it does not automatically define the person’s entire self-concept or identity. Self-concept contains multiple beliefs, roles and characteristics, and self-esteem concerns evaluation of worth. The example also shows how experiences can influence self-perception without completely determining it.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Self-concept = beliefs and knowledge about oneself.
  2. Identity includes personal and social aspects of who someone understands themselves to be.
  3. Self-esteem = evaluation of self-worth.
  4. Social identity concerns self-definition through group membership.
  5. The self develops through interaction among individual characteristics, relationships, culture and experience.
  6. Attachment, enculturation/acculturation, peers and childhood experiences can influence self-development without individually determining it.

Attachment & Development of Self

How early caregiving relationships MAY contribute to the developing self.

Attachment is an enduring emotional bond between an infant/child and caregiver, reflected in behaviours such as proximity seeking and using the caregiver as a source of security.

Repeated experiences of responsive and predictable caregiving may contribute to expectations that caregivers are available and that seeking support is effective.

Inconsistent or unresponsive caregiving may contribute to different expectations about relationships.

These are probabilistic developmental influences, not fixed outcomes.

A caregiver who functions as a secure base may support exploration. Exploration gives children opportunities to encounter challenges, develop skills and learn about their own abilities.

SECURITY → EXPLORATION → EXPERIENCE/MASTERY → INFORMATION ABOUT SELF

Present this as a possible developmental pathway, not a guaranteed outcome: exploration also depends on temperament, opportunity and encouragement from others beyond the caregiver.

Children also receive social feedback from caregivers. Repeated feedback about their behaviour, abilities and value can contribute to developing self-perceptions.

Caregiver feedback does not completely create self-worth: peers, teachers, culture and later experience also contribute to how children come to see themselves.

Bowlby later described these developing expectations as an internal working model — a mental representation of the self, other people and relationships.

Internal working models may influence later expectations and behaviour, but they can also change through later relationships and experiences.

A toddler falls, looks toward a caregiver, receives reassurance and then returns to exploring. What can we reasonably infer?

A child experiences inconsistent caregiving in early childhood. A psychologist predicts that the child will definitely become insecure, distrustful and attention-seeking as a teenager. What is wrong with this prediction?

Attachment and later self-esteem may be related, but other factors such as caregiver behaviour, temperament, family environment, peer relationships and socioeconomic conditions could influence both. Strong causal claims require designs that address alternative explanations.

Change: attachment-related expectations can show continuity, but they are not necessarily permanent. Later supportive or adverse relationships can modify expectations about self and others.

Ainsworth’s Strange Situation

A standardised observation used to classify patterns of attachment behaviour.

Ainsworth’s Strange Situation is a standardised observational procedure designed to assess patterns of infant–caregiver attachment behaviour, particularly during separation and reunion.

The procedure does NOT directly observe an invisible “attachment type.” Researchers observe behaviour and use that behaviour to classify attachment patterns.

This is an important measurement distinction: observed behaviour is evidence used to infer an underlying pattern, not the construct itself.

The classic Strange Situation has eight episodes. Most last approximately 3 minutes each, although episodes could be shortened if the infant became highly distressed.

  1. Experimenter introduces caregiver and infant to the room, then leaves.
  2. Caregiver and infant are alone; the infant can explore.
  3. Stranger enters, talks with the caregiver, then approaches the infant.
  4. Caregiver leaves; the infant remains with the stranger.
  5. First reunion: caregiver returns and the stranger leaves.
  6. Caregiver leaves again; the infant is alone.
  7. Stranger returns and interacts with the infant.
  8. Second reunion: caregiver returns and the stranger leaves.

Behaviours coded include exploration from a secure base, distress on separation, stranger anxiety and, above all, the reunion response.

Ainsworth’s Baltimore research involved extensive naturalistic home observations of caregiver–infant interaction. The Strange Situation was later used as a standardised laboratory assessment of attachment behaviour.

HOME OBSERVATION: caregiver sensitivity in everyday interaction

STRANGE SITUATION: standardised attachment-related behaviour under mild stress

  • Secure (B): uses the caregiver as a secure base; may show separation distress; seeks and reaccepts contact at reunion and can return to exploration.
  • Insecure-avoidant (A): relatively little overt distress; tends to avoid or ignore the caregiver during reunion.
  • Insecure-resistant/ambivalent (C): strong distress and difficulty being comforted; may simultaneously seek and resist contact.
  • Disorganised (D): contradictory, disoriented or apprehensive behaviour; identified as a later additional classification and not part of Ainsworth’s original three-category system.

No single behaviour is sufficient by itself — classifications are inferred from a pattern of behaviour across the procedure.

Patterns across separation, exploration and especially reunion behaviour are used together. Reunion responses are particularly informative because they show how the infant uses the caregiver after stress.

Strengths: the standardised episodes allow systematic comparison across studies, and can improve reliability and replication.

Limitation — artificiality and validity: the procedure is a brief, unfamiliar laboratory situation; behaviour there may not fully capture an enduring relationship.

US distributions, not universal norms: in Ainsworth’s original US samples, secure attachment was the most common classification, with smaller proportions classified as avoidant or resistant. These percentages should not be taught as universal human attachment frequencies.

Temperament: temperament provides a plausible alternative or contributing explanation because infants differ biologically in emotional reactivity and behavioural inhibition. However, associations between caregiver sensitivity and Strange Situation classifications support the view that attachment behaviour also reflects characteristics of the caregiver–infant relationship. Modern developmental explanations can consider temperament and caregiving as interacting rather than mutually exclusive explanations.

Which episode carries the most diagnostic weight, and why?
The reunion episodes. Separation distress varies for many reasons, but how a child responds to the returning caregiver — comfort-seeking vs avoidance vs ambivalence — best discriminates the attachment pattern.
Why did Ainsworth carry out naturalistic home observations as well as the laboratory procedure?
To develop measures of caregiver sensitivity in everyday interaction. Home observation and the Strange Situation measure related but different things, and associations between them supported the idea that the laboratory procedure captured something genuine about the caregiver–infant relationship rather than a one-off reaction to the artificial setting.
Could an infant’s temperament explain a resistant-looking classification?
Temperament provides a plausible alternative or contributing explanation because infants differ biologically in emotional reactivity and behavioural inhibition. However, associations between caregiver sensitivity and Strange Situation classifications support the view that attachment behaviour also reflects characteristics of the caregiver–infant relationship. Modern accounts treat temperament and caregiving as interacting rather than mutually exclusive explanations.
A researcher observes that an infant shows little distress when the caregiver leaves. Can the researcher immediately classify the infant as avoidant?
No. Attachment classification is based on a pattern of behaviour across the procedure, with reunion behaviour especially important. Low separation distress alone could reflect temperament, familiarity with separation or other factors.
The Strange Situation lasts only a short time in an unfamiliar laboratory. Why might this create a construct-validity problem?
Researchers want to measure the quality of an enduring attachment relationship, but they operationalise it using behaviour during a brief artificial situation. If laboratory behaviour is strongly influenced by temperament, unfamiliarity or cultural expectations about separation, the procedure may not measure attachment in exactly the same way for every infant.

The Strange Situation was developed within a particular cultural context. The meaning of separation, stranger interaction and independence can differ across cultures.

What Should I Remember?

  1. The Strange Situation is a standardised observation of attachment-related behaviour.
  2. Researchers infer attachment classifications from observed behaviour.
  3. The eight episodes involve exploration, stranger contact, separation and reunion.
  4. Reunion behaviour is particularly informative.
  5. The original classifications were secure, avoidant and resistant/ambivalent; disorganised was added later.
  6. Standardisation improves reliability and replication.
  7. Artificiality, temperament and culture raise validity questions.
  8. Behavioural measures should not automatically be equated with the underlying psychological construct.

Attachment & Research Evidence

Evaluating key studies that support, challenge and extend attachment theory.

Aim

Test whether attachment forms around feeding (drive-reduction learning) or contact comfort.

Procedure

  • 8 newborn rhesus monkeys raised with two surrogate “mothers”: wire mesh (dispensing milk) vs soft cloth (no milk).
  • Time spent with each surrogate measured daily; a frightening mechanical toy introduced to test refuge preference.
  • Monkeys followed into adolescence to assess later social functioning.

Key findings

  • The monkeys spent far more time clinging to the cloth surrogate (~17–18 hours/day vs under 1 hour at the wire mother), even though only the wire provided milk.
  • When frightened they ran to the cloth surrogate, then ventured out and returned (secure-base-like behaviour).

Conclusion

Harlow’s findings challenged simple drive-reduction explanations in which attachment was assumed to develop primarily because caregivers provide food. Contact comfort strongly influenced the monkeys’ surrogate preference and refuge behaviour.

The findings are consistent with the broader idea that attachment cannot be reduced to feeding alone and that proximity/comfort have important functions. Because the research involved rhesus monkeys raised under highly artificial conditions, generalisation to human attachment requires caution.

Later outcomes & ethics

Monkeys raised under severe social deprivation later showed serious social and behavioural difficulties, suggesting that contact with an inanimate surrogate was not sufficient for typical social development. However, the extreme deprivation procedure makes it difficult to isolate which missing social experiences produced particular later outcomes.

The procedures caused severe and lasting distress and would raise major contemporary animal-welfare concerns. Evaluation should consider whether the scientific knowledge gained justified the level of harm.

Lorenz’s observations of imprinting in greylag geese demonstrated biologically prepared following behaviour that develops rapidly during an early sensitive period.

Imprinting is not equivalent to human attachment. It provides comparative evidence that some social behaviours can be biologically prepared and sensitive to developmental timing.

Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg (1988) — cross-cultural meta-analysis

Aim: Compare attachment-classification distributions across cultural samples using the Strange Situation.

Who: 32 studies from 8 countries, pooling approximately 2,000 infant classifications.

Procedure: Meta-analysis of published Strange Situation results; distributions compared between countries and within countries.

Findings: Secure attachment was the most common classification across the cultural samples included in the meta-analysis, but within-country variation was substantial. Some samples showed cultural differences in the insecure classifications — for example, some German samples showed relatively more avoidant classifications, and some Japanese samples showed relatively more resistant classifications.

Interpretation: One interpretation is that culturally different caregiving practices and experiences with separation affect behaviour during the Strange Situation. However, these cultural practices have been proposed as possible explanations, and the meta-analysis itself cannot establish exactly why the distributions differ.

Measurement question: does the Strange Situation have the same meaning and validity in every cultural context?

Harlow’s monkeys spent far more time with the cloth surrogate even when the wire surrogate provided milk. What conclusion is justified?
The finding strongly challenges a simple drive-reduction account in which attachment preference is explained primarily by feeding. Contact comfort clearly influenced surrogate preference. However, the study does not establish that feeding has no role in natural caregiver–infant attachment.
Harlow used an experiment, Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg used meta-analysis, and Bowlby used retrospective correlational evidence. Which is the “best” method?
There is no universally best method. Harlow’s experimental control strengthens causal inference about the manipulated conditions but has major ethical and generalisability limitations. Meta-analysis can identify patterns across many studies but inherits weaknesses and cultural biases from the included research. Bowlby’s retrospective evidence provides information about real developmental histories but cannot establish causation. Method quality depends on the research question and the conclusions being drawn.
Some German samples in the meta-analysis showed relatively more avoidant classifications. Does this mean German parents are coldly rejecting?
No. One proposed interpretation is that caregiving practices in some German samples value independence, and differences in separation experience and familiarity with the procedure could also affect behaviour. These are possible explanations rather than established causes — the meta-analysis itself cannot establish exactly why the distributions differ.
Japanese infants in some samples show more resistant classifications than US infants. A researcher concludes that Japanese infants have poorer-quality attachments. What is wrong with this conclusion?
The Strange Situation was developed within a US cultural context, and separation may be less familiar or more stressful for infants from some caregiving environments. Different behaviour in the procedure may therefore partly reflect cultural experience and measurement validity rather than poorer attachment quality. The result requires interpretation rather than assuming one cultural pattern is the standard.

Harlow: what functions besides feeding may contribute to attachment behaviour?

Lorenz: can some early social behaviours show biological preparedness and sensitivity to timing?

Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg: how similar are Strange Situation classifications across cultural samples, and does the same measure mean the same thing everywhere?

Together these studies provide different kinds of evidence. None alone proves a complete theory of human attachment.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Harlow challenged simple feeding-based explanations of attachment using rhesus monkeys.
  2. Contact comfort strongly influenced surrogate preference and refuge behaviour.
  3. Severe social deprivation produced later difficulties, but animal findings require cautious generalisation to humans.
  4. Lorenz demonstrated early imprinting in birds, providing comparative evidence for biological preparedness and developmental timing.
  5. Imprinting is NOT equivalent to human attachment.
  6. Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg found secure attachment was the most common classification across the samples they analysed.
  7. Within-country variation was substantial, challenging simplistic assumptions that a nation has one attachment style.
  8. Cultural differences may partly reflect caregiving practices AND how the Strange Situation functions as a measure.
  9. Different research methods answer different questions and have different strengths and limitations.
  10. Strong HL evaluation asks: “What exactly does this evidence allow us to conclude?”

How culture shapes the developing self.

Enculturation is the process through which individuals learn and participate in the values, norms, behaviours, practices and meanings of their cultural environment.

What is learned:

  • Language — available concepts shape how people think about themselves
  • Social norms — rules about behaviour, roles and expectations
  • Rituals and traditions — shared practices reinforcing cultural identity
  • Values — what matters: individual achievement, collective harmony, family responsibility and more

How it is learned: modelling, reinforcement, instruction, language, observation, participation in cultural practices, and social feedback.

In some cultural settings, direct eye contact with adults may be encouraged as a sign of confidence or engagement, while in others reduced eye contact with elders may communicate respect. Children learn the locally appropriate meaning through repeated social interaction.

Cross-cultural research has often found higher average individualism in some societies such as the United States and Australia and higher average collectivism/interdependence in some East Asian societies. Individualism and collectivism are dimensions rather than two mutually exclusive types. Individuals and communities can show elements of both, and patterns can vary by context, generation, region and social group. Nationality alone does not label an individual person.

Markus & Kitayama (1991) distinguished two self-construals:

  • Independent self-construal: greater emphasis on internal attributes, personal goals and uniqueness.
  • Interdependent self-construal: greater emphasis on relationships, social roles and adjustment to others.

Independent/interdependent self-construals are related to broader ideas about individualism and collectivism, but the terms should not be treated as perfectly interchangeable.

How might enculturation affect self-concept differently across cultures?
In some cultural settings children may learn to describe themselves through personal traits and achievements; in others, through roles and relationships. A child describes themselves through roles and relationships (“a daughter, a team member”); another through personal traits and qualities (“funny, athletic”). What does this illustrate?
Two self-construal patterns consistent with Markus & Kitayama’s framework: more interdependent (relationship-based) and more independent (trait-based) self-descriptions. These are theoretical patterns, not fixed personality types or nationality labels.

It treats a population-level cultural tendency as though it determines an individual person. Cultural frameworks describe average patterns and possible influences, but individuals vary according to family, region, generation, education, personal experience and other cultural influences. Nationality alone cannot determine someone’s self-construal.
A child begins helping serve food to older relatives at every family gathering. Adults praise this behaviour and explain that caring for family members is important. How could enculturation influence the child’s developing self?
Through repeated participation, modelling, explicit teaching and reinforcement, the child learns that family responsibility is culturally valued. Over time, this value may become incorporated into self-concept — for example, seeing oneself as a responsible family member.

Peer Influence on Development

How peers shape identity, behaviour and self-concept.

  • Social comparison: peers provide standards against which adolescents may evaluate abilities, appearance, interests and behaviour
  • Belonging: acceptance and rejection can influence behaviour and self-evaluation
  • Identity exploration: peer contexts provide opportunities to experiment with interests, roles and styles
  • Social learning: peers can model and reinforce behaviours
  • Social skills: peer interaction provides practice in cooperation, negotiation and conflict resolution

Peer influence can be prosocial or antisocial.

Real-life example: a teenager who codes at home may only identify as “a coder” after joining a coding club where the skill earns peer recognition.

Festinger (1954) — social comparison theory: proposes that people often evaluate their abilities and opinions partly by comparing themselves with others. Comparison is common, but it does not automatically change self-concept — the effect depends on who the comparison is made with and how the result is interpreted.

Asch (1951) — conformity: Asch’s conformity experiments demonstrate that perceived group consensus can influence individual judgments. This provides evidence for a social-influence mechanism that may also operate in peer groups.

Sherif (1936) — norm formation: Sherif’s research illustrates informational social influence: when a situation is ambiguous, people may use others’ judgments as information and gradually develop shared norms. This provides another possible mechanism through which peer groups can influence behaviour.

Asch’s participants sometimes conformed to an obviously incorrect majority. Can we conclude that adolescents will show even stronger conformity to their friends?
No. Asch demonstrates that group pressure can influence judgment under particular laboratory conditions. Applying the mechanism to adolescent friendships is theoretically reasonable, but the study itself did not compare adolescents with adults or measure real-life peer pressure.

Adolescents who take more risks tend to have friends who also take more risks. There are at least two explanations:

  • Peer influence: friends’ behaviour changes the adolescent’s behaviour through modelling, reinforcement, norms or pressure.
  • Peer selection: adolescents choose friends who already resemble them.
  • Bidirectional process: selection and influence can occur together.

This is an excellent HL causality problem: similarity between friends does not by itself reveal which process produced it.

A study finds that teenagers with friends who regularly skip school are themselves more likely to skip school. The researcher concludes, “Friends cause teenagers to skip school.” What is the problem?
The association could reflect peer influence, but it could also reflect peer selection: teenagers who already have similar attitudes may choose one another as friends. Shared school, family or neighbourhood factors could also influence both. Longitudinal evidence can help examine whether friendship predicts later behavioural change, but simple correlation cannot establish causation.

Peers do not simply act on adolescents. Adolescents also choose, shape and respond to their peer environments.

Resilience refers to relatively positive adaptation despite significant adversity or risk.

  • Developing social/problem-solving skills
  • Experiences of mastery and self-efficacy

Why is the concept of resilience important for understanding development?
It prevents fatalistic readings of adversity research: outcomes depend on the interaction between risk and protective factors, so supportive experiences and skill-building can genuinely change trajectories.
Researchers find that children whose parents use an authoritative parenting style have higher average self-esteem. Can we conclude that authoritative parenting CAUSED the higher self-esteem?

The relationship provides social support, while repeated successful performances provide mastery experiences and opportunities to develop self-efficacy. Two children experience similar levels of childhood adversity. One later shows substantial psychological difficulties while the other functions relatively well. Does this mean the adversity was unimportant for the second child?
No. Similar risks can lead to different outcomes because development depends on interacting protective factors, individual characteristics and later experiences. Resilience means relatively positive adaptation despite adversity, not that the adversity had no effect.

Culture & the Self

How cultural context may shape self-understanding.

The framework developed: Markus & Kitayama (1991)

A landmark theoretical review synthesising cross-cultural psychology, linguistics and anthropology into two self-construals:

  • Independent self-construal: greater emphasis on internal attributes, personal goals, autonomy and uniqueness.
  • Interdependent self-construal: greater emphasis on relationships, social roles, connectedness and adjustment to social context.

These are theoretical cultural patterns — cultural self-systems, not fixed personality types. Individuals may show elements of both, and self-construal can vary across situations.

Supporting evidence: Heine et al. (1999)

Heine and colleagues reviewed evidence suggesting that forms of self-enhancement commonly found in North American samples were substantially less evident in many East Asian samples, where self-criticism and self-improvement motives were often more prominent.

This is consistent with the possibility that cultural context influences how people evaluate and present the self. These are group-level tendencies, not universal individual characteristics.

Looking-glass self extension: others’ evaluations can shape self-concept — but what counts as praiseworthy may vary culturally, so the “mirror” itself is culturally framed.

The same experience may be interpreted differently depending partly on culturally learned values, expectations and self-construals.

PARAGRAPH 3: evaluate the evidence and integrate the explanations — attachment and culture are not competing forces; caregiving itself occurs within cultural contexts, while later cultural and social experiences may reinforce or modify developmental expectations. CONCLUSION: the self develops through interacting biological, cognitive and sociocultural influences rather than one isolated cause.
Two adolescents had similar early attachment experiences but now have very different self-concepts. Does this disprove the idea that attachment can influence development?
No. Attachment is one possible developmental influence rather than a complete explanation of the self. Later peer relationships, cultural experiences, mastery experiences, adversity, individual characteristics and other influences can produce different developmental trajectories.
Why is asking “Does attachment OR culture shape the self?” potentially the wrong question?
Because the influences interact. Caregiving occurs within cultural expectations, culture influences how relationships and behaviour are interpreted, peers provide additional feedback, and later experiences can reinforce or modify earlier expectations. Development is better understood as an interacting system than as a competition between isolated causes.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Attachment, enculturation, peers and childhood experiences can all contribute to self-development.
  2. None alone determines the outcome.
  3. The influences interact rather than operating independently.
  4. Their relative importance can vary between people and across development.
  5. Early developmental pathways can be modified by later experiences.
  6. Strong psychological explanations integrate evidence rather than searching for one universal cause.

IB Concepts in Human Development

Six theoretical tools for analysing evidence and claims.

Bias refers to systematic influences that may distort psychological research, interpretation or conclusions.

  • The Strange Situation may carry cultural assumptions because it was developed in a particular cultural context.
  • Bowlby’s theoretical expectations may have influenced interpretation in retrospective research.
  • Cultural stereotypes can bias interpretation of group differences.
  • Sampling from narrow populations can limit generalisability.
A researcher assumes that the US Strange Situation pattern is the ideal standard and describes every cultural difference from it as deficient. Which concept is most relevant?
Bias. The researcher is treating one cultural pattern as the universal standard and interpreting differences through that assumption.

Causality concerns whether evidence demonstrates that one variable actually produces change in another.

  • Early attachment correlated with later outcomes does NOT automatically mean attachment caused them.
  • Parenting style correlated with self-esteem does NOT establish parenting → self-esteem.
  • Peer similarity may reflect peer influence, peer selection or both.
  • Experiments provide stronger causal inference about manipulated variables, but conclusions remain limited to what was actually manipulated and measured.
Children receiving highly responsive caregiving later show higher average social competence. Can we conclude that responsive caregiving caused the difference?
Not from a correlation alone. Child temperament, family circumstances or other variables could influence both caregiving and later social competence. The evidence may support an association without establishing causation.

Change concerns how psychological characteristics and behaviour develop, remain stable or alter over time.

  • Neuroplasticity
  • Synaptogenesis and pruning
  • Cognitive development
  • Changing theory-of-mind performance
  • Resilience
  • Internal working models that can be modified by later relationships
  • Developmental trajectories altered by protective experiences

Development does not mean that early experience fixes the future. Psychology investigates both continuity AND change.

A child experiences severe early adversity but later develops strong relationships and positive self-esteem. Which concept helps us analyse this?
Change. Early experiences can influence developmental trajectories without permanently fixing them. Later protective relationships, mastery experiences and changing environments can contribute to different outcomes.

Measurement concerns how psychologists operationalise and assess psychological constructs.

THEORY OF MIND → false-belief tasks

ATTACHMENT → Strange Situation behaviour

OBJECT PERMANENCE → search behaviour vs looking-time paradigms

SELF-ESTEEM → questionnaire scales

Psychologists cannot usually observe constructs such as attachment, self-esteem or theory of mind directly. They operationalise them using observable behaviour or responses.

This creates the HL question: does the operationalisation validly represent the underlying construct?

Looking-time paradigms infer infants’ expectations from patterns of attention, whereas Piaget’s search tasks require infants to produce coordinated behavioural responses. Different operationalisations can therefore lead researchers to different conclusions about when an ability appears.

A baby looks longer at an apparently impossible event. Does this directly prove the baby possesses an adult-like understanding of object permanence?
No. Looking time is an observable behaviour from which researchers infer expectations. The interpretation depends on whether the measure validly captures the psychological ability being claimed.

Perspective concerns how psychological phenomena can be understood through different theoretical, cultural or disciplinary viewpoints.

  • Piaget → emphasis on active cognitive construction
  • Vygotsky → greater emphasis on social interaction and cultural tools
  • Bowlby → evolutionary/attachment perspective
  • Cultural psychology → cultural context and self-construal

Different perspectives may emphasise different mechanisms without one necessarily explaining the whole of development.

Piaget and Vygotsky explain cognitive development differently. Does one perspective automatically make the other useless?
No. Piaget highlights active cognitive construction, while Vygotsky gives greater emphasis to social interaction and cultural tools. Comparing perspectives can reveal different mechanisms and limitations.

Responsibility concerns the obligations psychologists have when conducting research, interpreting evidence and communicating conclusions.

Research ethics:

  • Harlow → severe harm to animals
  • Strange Situation → deliberately produces mild infant distress
  • Research with children → consent from guardians, assent where appropriate, protection from harm and special care with vulnerable participants
  • Autism / deprivation / attachment → avoid deterministic, stigmatising or culturally biased interpretations

Communicating evidence: researchers and psychologists have a responsibility not to present correlational findings as causal facts or group averages as descriptions of every individual.

A headline says, “Insecure attachment permanently damages children’s future relationships.” Even if based on a real correlational study, what responsibility problem exists?
The claim overstates the evidence and presents a probabilistic association as developmental destiny. Responsible communication should acknowledge uncertainty, alternative explanations and the possibility of later developmental change.

Use the concept or concepts that genuinely help analyse the evidence or question. Do not force every study through every concept.

A cross-cultural study finds that infants in Country A are more often classified as resistant than infants in Country B using the Strange Situation. A researcher concludes: “Parenting in Country A produces poorer attachment.” Which IB concepts could help evaluate this claim?
Several concepts are relevant. MEASUREMENT asks whether the Strange Situation has equivalent meaning across both cultures. BIAS asks whether one cultural pattern is being treated as the universal standard. CAUSALITY asks whether the study can show that parenting caused the classification difference. PERSPECTIVE asks how cultural caregiving practices might change interpretation of the behaviour.   2. CAUSALITY — does the evidence establish cause?
  3. CHANGE — how and why might development remain stable or change?
  4. MEASUREMENT — how was the psychological construct operationalised?
  5. PERSPECTIVE — how might another theoretical or cultural viewpoint understand it?
  6. RESPONSIBILITY — what obligations do psychologists have in conducting, interpreting and communicating research?

These are thinking tools, not vocabulary words to memorise.

Scenario: Two adolescents describe themselves differently. One emphasises personal achievements and individual goals. The other emphasises family relationships and responsibilities.

How could cultural psychology help explain this difference?
Markus & Kitayama’s framework suggests that culturally learned self-construals may influence how people understand and describe themselves. Greater emphasis on personal traits and goals is consistent with an independent self-construal, while greater emphasis on relationships and social roles is consistent with an interdependent self-construal. However, the descriptions alone do not tell us that culture caused the difference, and nationality should not be used to assume an individual’s self-construal.

• avoids cultural stereotyping

Scenario: Two children begin at a new school. One quickly explores the classroom and approaches classmates. The other initially remains withdrawn and cautious.

Could attachment theory help explain the difference?
Bowlby proposed that repeated attachment experiences may contribute to internal working models — expectations about the self and relationships. Different expectations could therefore be one possible influence on how children approach unfamiliar social situations. However, the behaviour in this scenario cannot establish the children’s attachment classifications or show that attachment caused the difference. Temperament, previous school experiences, language, peers and other factors could also contribute.

IMPORTANT:

Do NOT write: “secure attachment causes confidence” or “insecure attachment causes withdrawal.”

Instead: “attachment may contribute to…”

Scenario: Lena watches Amir place a toy in the blue box. Amir leaves the room. While he is away, another child moves the toy into the red box. Lena is asked where Amir will look for the toy and answers, “the red box.”

How could Theory of Mind research help explain Lena’s answer?
A false-belief task tests whether a child can represent another person’s belief when that belief differs from reality and from the child’s own knowledge. Amir believes the toy is still in the blue box, while Lena knows it is now in the red box. Predicting that Amir will search the red box may therefore indicate difficulty representing his false belief. However, performance on one task should not be treated as proof that Lena completely lacks Theory of Mind because language, memory, executive demands and task understanding can also affect performance.

WHAT WAS ACTUALLY OBSERVED?

Lena predicted the red box.

WHAT IS INFERRED?

Possible difficulty representing Amir’s false belief.

This distinction is extremely important.

Scenario: A longitudinal study finds that children who experienced more responsive caregiving in early childhood later report higher average self-esteem.

A student concludes: “Responsive caregiving causes high self-esteem.” Evaluate the conclusion.
The association is consistent with caregiving being one developmental influence, but the finding alone does not establish causation. Child temperament, family environment, socioeconomic conditions and later experiences could influence both caregiving and self-esteem. The direction may also be bidirectional because children’s characteristics can influence caregiver behaviour.

OBSERVED: responsive caregiving is associated with later self-esteem.

NOT ESTABLISHED: responsive caregiving alone caused the difference.

Scenario: Researchers use the Strange Situation in two cultural groups. One group shows substantially more resistant classifications.

Can the researchers conclude that this cultural group has poorer attachment?
Not automatically. The Strange Situation operationalises attachment through behaviour during separation and reunion. If separation has different meanings or familiarity across cultural contexts, the same procedure may not measure attachment equivalently in both groups. Researchers should consider cultural validity before interpreting the classification difference as poorer attachment.

MEASUREMENT + BIAS + PERSPECTIVE

Measurement asks whether the instrument works equivalently, Bias asks whether one behavioural pattern is being treated as a universal standard, and Perspective asks how caregiving practices may differ between cultures.

Scenario: A child experiences substantial adversity early in life but later develops supportive friendships, succeeds in school and reports increasingly positive self-beliefs.

How does this scenario illustrate development rather than contradict early-experience research?
Early adversity can increase developmental risk without permanently determining an outcome. Supportive relationships, mastery experiences and changing environments may act as protective factors and alter developmental trajectories. This illustrates resilience and the IB concept of Change.

When applying psychology to an unseen situation:

  1. Start with the scenario, not the researcher.
  2. Select psychology that genuinely explains something in it.
  3. Name the mechanism.
  4. Connect the mechanism to specific scenario details.
  5. Use evidence/theory where useful.
  6. Separate observation from inference.
  7. Calibrate causal language.

Language guide

STRONG: “may contribute to” · “is consistent with” · “is associated with” · “could help explain” · “the evidence suggests”

USE ONLY WHEN JUSTIFIED: “caused” · “proved” · “determines” · “will lead to”

What Should I Remember?

  1. Application means USING psychology to explain specific scenario details.
  2. Naming a theory or researcher alone is not enough.
  3. Explain the mechanism.
  4. Separate what was observed from what psychology allows you to infer.
  5. Consider alternative explanations.
  6. Correlation does not automatically establish causation.
  7. Psychological explanations are often probabilistic.
  8. Use evidence to support reasoning rather than simply dropping study names.
  9. Strong HL answers integrate knowledge with concepts such as causality, measurement, change, bias, perspective and responsibility.
