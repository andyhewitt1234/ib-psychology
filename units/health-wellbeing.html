Health & Well-being: What You Will Learn

Two health problems will guide this unit: Major Depressive Disorder and problematic social-media use. We will investigate why they develop, how psychologists study them, and what can be done to help.

Two People. Two Health Problems. Many Questions.

Maya — Major Depressive Disorder

Maya has been diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). She has experienced persistent low mood, loss of interest in activities she previously enjoyed, difficulty sleeping and problems concentrating. But knowing that Maya has MDD does not explain why she developed it.

What could be contributing to Maya's depression?

Leo — Problematic Social-Media Use

Leo spends increasing amounts of time checking social media, including when he intends to study or sleep. He often reaches for his phone when stressed and finds it difficult to reduce his use even when it interferes with other parts of his life. But describing Leo's behavior does not explain why it developed.

What could be contributing to Leo's behavior?

Our Two Focus Areas

Throughout this unit, our selected mental health disorder is Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). Our selected health problem is problematic social-media use. The IB guide uses social media addiction as one possible health problem. In this course, we will generally use the more cautious term problematic social-media use rather than assuming that every pattern of excessive use should automatically be classified as an addiction.

Why Might Maya Develop MDD?

Biological

Some people may have greater biological vulnerability to depression. Psychologists can investigate factors such as genetic vulnerability, brain function and neurotransmission. But finding a biological process associated with depression does not automatically prove that it caused Maya's MDD.

Cognitive

How Maya interprets experiences may also matter. For example, repeatedly interpreting setbacks as evidence that 'nothing will ever improve' could contribute to patterns of thinking associated with depression. Cognitive models such as Beck's attempt to explain this through schemas and automatic thoughts.

Environmental

Experiences can also contribute to vulnerability. Persistent stress, major negative life events or difficult social circumstances may increase the risk of depression. However, experiencing adversity does not mean that a person will inevitably develop MDD.

The key idea

Maya's depression does not need to have one single cause. Biological vulnerability, patterns of thinking and environmental experiences may interact.

Why Might Leo's Social-Media Use Become Problematic?

Social Learning

Leo sees friends and influencers repeatedly checking, posting and receiving attention online. Observing other people being rewarded can make behaviors more likely to be copied, while likes, replies and other responses may reinforce Leo's own behavior. Social Learning Theory examines exactly this process: learning by observing rewarded behavior.

Stress

Leo often checks social media when he feels stressed. If scrolling temporarily reduces unpleasant feelings, using social media may become a coping response that is repeated. This creates an important question: does stress contribute to problematic use, can problematic use contribute to stress, or can both occur?

Social & Environmental Context

Leo's behavior also occurs within a social environment. Peer expectations, constant access to technology and patterns of online interaction may influence when and how often he uses social media. The behavior therefore cannot be understood only by looking at Leo as an isolated individual.

The key idea

Problematic social-media use can also have multiple influences. Social learning, stress and the wider social environment may interact.

Their problems are different, but psychologists can ask similar questions.

For both Maya and Leo, we can ask:

  • What exactly is the health problem?
  • How is it being measured?
  • What factors could contribute to it?
  • How might different factors interact?
  • Does the evidence show association or causation?
  • What could be changed to prevent or treat the problem?

This is why Health & Well-being is not simply a list of disorders, theories and treatments.

Psychologists are trying to explain health outcomes using evidence.

Psychologists are also interested in how common health problems are.

But prevalence becomes psychologically interesting when we ask why rates change or differ.

For example:

What might explain an increase in recorded problematic social-media use over time?

Why might recorded rates of depression differ between populations?

Possible explanations could involve genuine differences in risk or exposure, but psychologists must also consider measurement, diagnosis, access to healthcare, reporting and cultural context.

A prevalence difference is therefore something to explain — not an explanation by itself.

If psychologists understand processes contributing to a health problem, they can ask whether changing those processes may help.

For MDD, we will examine both biological treatment and psychological treatment.

For problematic social-media use, we will examine strategies designed to reduce problematic behavior.

But an intervention sounding reasonable does not prove that it works.

We must ask:

  • What is the intervention trying to change?
  • How should improvement be measured?
  • Compared with what?
  • How large and consistent is the improvement?
  • What other explanations could account for the result?

This is why the unit examines both the explanation and the effectiveness of treatment and prevention.

Understanding Major Depressive Disorder

What MDD is, why it matters, and why it is more than ordinary sadness.

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a clinical condition characterised by persistent low mood and/or loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia) that interferes with daily functioning.

Common symptoms can include:

  1. Depressed mood
  2. Loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia)
  3. Changes in sleep
  4. Changes in appetite or weight
  5. Fatigue or low energy
  6. Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
  7. Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt
  8. Unusually slowed or restless movement
  9. Recurrent thoughts about death or suicide

A diagnosis requires five or more symptoms during the same two-week period, including either depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure, and the symptoms must cause significant distress or difficulty in everyday functioning.

  • Duration: MDD persists rather than being a brief emotional response.
  • Intensity: Symptoms can be severe.
  • Functional impact: Symptoms can interfere with school, work, relationships, self-care, and everyday life.
  • Symptom cluster: MDD involves multiple symptoms rather than sadness alone.

Sadness is a normal human emotion, whereas MDD is a disorder involving a persistent and impairing pattern of symptoms.

Imagine “River”

River has not seemed like themselves for the past three weeks. They do not necessarily describe themselves as sad. Instead, they say they feel “numb” and that things they used to enjoy—playing basketball, gaming with friends and going out on weekends—do not feel enjoyable anymore. They have stopped joining their friends after school and spend much more time alone.

River is also sleeping badly, feels tired almost every day, has difficulty concentrating in class, and has begun saying that they feel worthless and that they “can’t do anything right.” Their schoolwork is falling behind and they are struggling to manage their normal daily routine.

River is showing several symptoms associated with MDD, including loss of interest or pleasure, sleep disturbance, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, feelings of worthlessness, and clear impairment in everyday functioning. The symptoms have also persisted for more than two weeks and are interfering with daily life.

The important point: depression does not always look like obvious sadness. For some people, a major feature is anhedonia—a loss of interest or pleasure in things that previously mattered to them—which may be experienced as emptiness or emotional numbness.

People diagnosed with the same disorder may show very different symptom profiles. Recognizing symptoms helps us identify MDD, but psychologists also want to understand the mechanisms that may explain why depression develops and persists.

Why is it important that depression is not just ordinary sadness?
MDD involves a persistent cluster of symptoms that can significantly interfere with everyday functioning. Ordinary sadness is usually temporary and does not necessarily involve the same range, duration, or level of impairment.

Measuring & Diagnosing Depression

How depression is identified and measured — and the challenges psychologists face when turning symptoms into data and diagnoses.

How can psychologists measure something they cannot directly see?

Depression cannot be measured directly like height or temperature. Psychologists and clinicians therefore measure indicators of depression, such as reported symptoms, how frequently they occur, their severity, and their effect on everyday functioning.

IB Connection — Measurement

Measurement is the process of turning a psychological construct into something that can be recorded or quantified.

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is identified through clinical assessment. There is currently no single biological test that can diagnose MDD.

Clinical interviews

A clinician talks with the individual about their symptoms, how long they have lasted, their severity, their impact on everyday functioning, previous episodes, and other relevant information.

Clinicians use standardized diagnostic systems to guide their assessment:

  • Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR) — published by the American Psychiatric Association
  • International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11) — published by the World Health Organization

These systems provide standardized criteria that clinicians can use when assessing whether a person's symptoms fit a recognized disorder such as MDD.

Self-report measures

Self-report measures ask people standardized questions about their own symptoms. Their responses can then be recorded and scored. Two widely used measures are the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II).

Other clinical information

Clinicians also consider:

  • Symptom duration
  • Symptom severity
  • Impact on everyday functioning
  • Previous episodes and history
  • Other possible explanations for symptoms
  • Other relevant clinical information

MEASUREMENT ≠ DIAGNOSIS

A questionnaire can measure reported depressive symptoms and contribute evidence to an assessment. A questionnaire score by itself is not the same thing as a clinical diagnosis.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

  • The Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) contains 9 items relating to depressive symptoms.
  • It asks how frequently symptoms have occurred during the previous two weeks.
  • Responses use a four-point scale:
    • 0 — Not at all
    • 1 — Several days
    • 2 — More than half the days
    • 3 — Nearly every day
  • Responses can be converted into numerical data and summed.
  • Higher scores indicate greater reported depressive symptom severity.
  • The PHQ-9 can be useful for screening and monitoring symptoms.
  • A PHQ-9 score alone does not establish a clinical diagnosis of MDD.

Explore the PHQ-9 as a Measurement Tool

The PHQ-9 is a real psychological questionnaire. Explore how its questions and response scale turn reported experiences into numerical data. The purpose here is to understand psychological measurement, not to diagnose yourself or anyone else.

Think about what you just did

What did the questionnaire actually measure?
The frequency of symptoms associated with depression during the previous two weeks.
Why is this an example of psychological measurement?
Because experiences that cannot be directly observed, such as low mood or loss of interest, have been operationalized as standardized responses that can be recorded numerically.
What is one advantage of measuring depression this way?
Everyone responds to the same questions using the same response scale, which helps standardize measurement and makes responses easier to compare.
What is one limitation?
It relies on self-report. Responses may be affected by memory, interpretation of questions, willingness to report symptoms, or response bias.
Does the score alone prove that someone has Major Depressive Disorder?
NO. The PHQ-9 measures reported symptoms and can support screening or monitoring. Diagnosing MDD requires broader clinical assessment and consideration of diagnostic criteria.
  • The Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) contains 21 self-report items.
  • It measures the severity of depressive symptoms.
  • Items contain statements representing increasing levels of symptom severity.
  • Responses are converted into scores and combined into a total.
  • Higher scores indicate greater reported depressive symptom severity.

Explore the BDI-II as a Measurement Tool

The BDI-II uses a different approach from the PHQ-9. Instead of asking about frequency, each item presents four statements representing increasing levels of symptom severity. Select the statement that best describes how you have been feeling during the last two weeks, including today.

Compare the PHQ-9 and BDI-II

If the PHQ-9 and BDI-II both measure depressive symptoms but use different questions, could they potentially produce different results?
Yes. Different measures operationalize depression differently. This raises important questions about whether psychological measurement is consistent and whether it accurately measures the intended construct.

IB Connection — Measurement & Individual Differences

The same measurement output can result from different combinations of reported symptoms. Measurement records an indirect indicator, and individual differences mean identical scores do not necessarily imply identical symptom profiles, causes or treatment needs.

Reliability = consistency

Reliability asks whether psychological measurement produces reasonably consistent results.

Consider this question: If the same person were assessed again, or if different clinicians assessed the same person, would the result be similar?

Regier et al. (2013)

Researchers wanted to see whether people would receive the same MDD diagnosis when assessed again.

MDD received a kappa score of about 0.28, which is considered low.

In simple terms: the diagnoses were not very consistent. A person might receive an MDD diagnosis in one assessment but not receive the same diagnosis when assessed again.

This suggests that diagnosing MDD may not always be very reliable.

Important: A kappa of 0.28 does not mean that clinicians agreed only 28% of the time.

For now, remember:

Low kappa score = low consistency = lower reliability.

This provides evidence that standardized diagnostic criteria do not guarantee perfect agreement between assessments. Reliability can also depend on the assessment procedure, sample, clinicians and context.

What does this show?
Even when clinicians use standardized diagnostic criteria, they may not always reach the same diagnosis.

IB Connection — Reliability

Reliability concerns the consistency of psychological measurement or diagnosis.

Validity — “accuracy” is a helpful starting point

Validity asks whether the interpretation made from a psychological measure or diagnosis is justified — particularly whether it adequately represents the construct it is intended to assess. Keeping “accuracy” in mind is fine as a simple memory aid, but HL answers need this fuller definition.

A questionnaire may reliably produce a score, but researchers must still ask whether that score validly represents the depressive symptoms or construct they want to measure.

Imagine someone reports sleep problems, exhaustion, difficulty concentrating, and appetite changes. A depression questionnaire produces a high symptom score. Does this automatically prove that depression is causing those symptoms?

Does a high questionnaire score automatically prove that depression is causing the symptoms?
NO. Some symptoms can have several possible explanations. A high questionnaire score indicates reported depressive symptoms, but interpreting those symptoms requires additional information.

Reliability

Is the measurement reasonably consistent?

Validity

Does the evidence justify the interpretation we are making from the measurement?

Standardized criteria and questionnaires can reduce subjectivity, but they cannot automatically eliminate bias.

Cultural factors

People from different cultural backgrounds may describe, interpret, or express psychological distress differently. This can influence whether symptoms are recognized as depression.

Gender expectations

Social expectations surrounding gender may influence how symptoms are reported or interpreted.

Clinician expectations

A clinician's assumptions or expectations can potentially influence how information is interpreted.

Standardization can reduce subjectivity, but symptoms still have to be reported and interpreted by people. Psychological measurement and diagnosis can therefore still be influenced by context and bias.

IB Connection — Bias

Bias refers to systematic distortion that can affect how research is conducted, interpreted, or applied.

Check Your Understanding

What is the difference between measuring depressive symptoms and diagnosing MDD?
Measurement records indicators such as symptom frequency or severity. Diagnosis involves interpreting evidence alongside diagnostic criteria and broader clinical information.
A questionnaire produces similar results when repeated. Is this mainly evidence of reliability or validity?
Reliability — because the measurement is producing consistent results.
Could a psychological measure be reliable but invalid?
Yes. A measure could consistently produce similar results without accurately measuring the psychological construct it claims to measure.
Why can bias still matter even when standardized diagnostic criteria are used?
Because symptoms still have to be reported and interpreted, and this can be influenced by individual, cultural, and clinician factors.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Depression is measured indirectly through indicators such as reported symptoms.
  2. Measures such as the PHQ-9 and BDI-II operationalize depressive symptoms by converting responses into numerical data.
  3. Measurement is not the same thing as diagnosis.
  4. Reliability, validity, and bias can affect psychological measurement and diagnosis.

Biological Explanations of Depression

How genes, brain processes, and chemical messengers may increase vulnerability to depression.

Why do some people develop depression while others do not?

Biological explanations examine how genetic inheritance, brain processes and chemical messengers may contribute to vulnerability to Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). Importantly, biological factors may increase risk or vulnerability — they do not mean that depression is biologically predetermined. Keep this distinction in mind as you work through the evidence.

Depression can run in families, which suggests that genetic differences may contribute to a person’s vulnerability to developing MDD.

This does not mean that there is a single ‘depression gene.’ Depression is polygenic: many genes may each make a small contribution to vulnerability. A person may therefore inherit a greater or lower susceptibility to depression rather than inheriting depression itself.

Genes also do not act independently of experience. Two people with similar genetic vulnerability can have different outcomes because their environments, experiences and other biological factors differ.

A Real-Life Example

Imagine that depression has occurred several times in Noah’s family. His mother and grandfather have both experienced MDD.

This family history could mean that Noah has inherited some genetic vulnerability. But it does not mean that Noah will inevitably develop depression.

Noah’s outcome may also depend on factors such as stressful life events, relationships, social support and other biological differences. Genetic vulnerability changes probability; it does not determine a person’s future.

Why “Polygenic” Matters

Because depression is polygenic, psychologists should not expect to find one gene that explains whether someone develops MDD.

Instead, many genetic differences may contribute small amounts of vulnerability. Genetic explanations therefore describe differences in risk, not a simple gene-to-disorder pathway.

McGuffin et al. (1996): Do Genes Matter?

McGuffin and colleagues compared depression concordance in identical (monozygotic or MZ) twins and non-identical (dizygotic or DZ) twins.

MZ twins share almost all of their genes, while DZ twins share roughly half of their segregating genes on average.

The researchers reported probandwise concordance of approximately 46% for MZ twins and 20% for DZ twins.

The higher concordance among MZ twins is consistent with genetic differences contributing to vulnerability to depression.

What Does This Actually Tell Us?

The most important comparison is that concordance was higher for MZ twins than DZ twins.

Because MZ twins are more genetically similar, this pattern is consistent with a genetic contribution to depression.

But notice something equally important: MZ concordance was about 46%, not close to 100%.

If genes completely determined whether someone developed depression, we would expect identical twins to show much greater similarity. The fact that many identical twin pairs differed shows that genes are not sufficient by themselves.

If genes completely determined whether someone developed MDD, what would you expect the concordance rate for identical twins to be?
Close to 100%. Identical twins are extremely genetically similar, so a completely genetically determined disorder should appear in both twins almost every time. The actual MZ concordance was much lower, which supports genetic influence but not genetic determinism.

One Important Limitation

Twin studies cannot perfectly separate genes from environment. Identical twins may experience more similar environments than non-identical twins, so greater MZ concordance cannot automatically be attributed entirely to genetics.

The evidence therefore supports genetic influence, but it does not tell us that genes alone caused the difference.

Researchers have found differences in the activity and functioning of several brain regions in people experiencing depression.

Two useful examples are the prefrontal cortex and the amygdala.

These findings can help psychologists understand biological processes associated with depressive symptoms. However, finding a brain difference does not automatically tell us whether that difference caused the depression, resulted from it, or developed through an interaction with other factors.

Two brain areas commonly discussed in research on depression: the prefrontal cortex and amygdala.

The Prefrontal Cortex

The prefrontal cortex is involved in processes such as planning, decision-making, attention and regulating thoughts and emotions.

Differences in prefrontal functioning have been associated with depression. This could help explain why some people with MDD experience difficulties such as persistent negative thinking, poor concentration or difficulty regulating emotional responses.

For example, imagine someone receives one piece of criticism at work and then spends the entire evening repeatedly thinking, ‘I’m useless. I always get everything wrong.’ Difficulties in the systems involved in regulating attention and thought may contribute to why negative information becomes difficult to disengage from.

This does not mean that the prefrontal cortex contains a ‘depression center.’ It is one part of a much larger network.

The Amygdala

The amygdala is involved in detecting and processing emotionally important information, particularly information related to threat and negative emotion.

Research has associated depression with differences in amygdala responses to emotional information. This may help explain why some people with depression show increased sensitivity to negative emotional cues.

For example, someone with depression might receive nine positive comments and one critical comment but find that the criticism captures their attention and produces a particularly strong emotional response.

The amygdala does not simply ‘create negative emotion,’ and depression cannot be reduced to an overactive amygdala. Its activity is part of interacting brain systems.

These Regions Do Not Work Alone

The prefrontal cortex and amygdala interact with wider brain networks.

It can therefore be useful to think about depression in terms of systems involved in processing emotional information and regulating thought and attention, rather than imagining that one brain region causes the disorder.

Brain findings provide evidence about biological processes associated with depression, but they need to be interpreted alongside cognitive, environmental and genetic evidence.

A student with depression receives mostly positive feedback on an assignment but becomes intensely focused on the one negative comment and cannot stop thinking about it. How could the prefrontal cortex and amygdala help us understand this pattern?
The amygdala is involved in processing emotionally important information and may contribute to a strong response to the negative comment. Prefrontal systems are involved in regulating attention, thought and emotion, so differences in these systems may contribute to difficulty disengaging from the criticism. This is a possible biological explanation of the pattern, not proof that these brain differences caused the student’s depression.

What are neurotransmitters?

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that neurons use to communicate. When a neuron fires, it releases neurotransmitter molecules into the small gap between neurons, called the synapse. The neurotransmitter crosses the synapse and binds to receptors on another neuron, influencing its activity. Some neurotransmitter molecules are then taken back into the neuron that released them — a process called reuptake.

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT)

One neurotransmitter that has been extensively investigated in relation to depression is serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT). Serotonin signaling is involved in several functions relevant to Major Depressive Disorder (MDD), including mood-related processing, sleep, and appetite — all commonly disrupted in depression.

Serotonin affects brain areas involved in emotion. Increased serotonin signaling can reduce the amygdala’s response to negative or threatening information, reducing negative emotional reactions. For example, if a student receives criticism from a teacher, they may experience a less intense emotional reaction rather than immediately feeling extremely upset or threatened.

Serotonin also influences activity in the prefrontal cortex, a region involved in regulating thoughts and emotional responses. For example, after receiving that criticism, the student may be better able to control their emotional response, think about the feedback calmly and shift their attention back to their work rather than continuing to focus on the negative comment.

The monoamine hypothesis

Researchers proposed that reduced activity in monoamine neurotransmitter systems, particularly serotonin, might contribute to depressive symptoms. Monoamines are a group of neurotransmitters that includes serotonin, noradrenaline (norepinephrine), and dopamine. This idea developed into the monoamine hypothesis of depression, one of the most influential biological explanations for MDD.

The “chemical imbalance” oversimplification

The monoamine hypothesis was later simplified in popular accounts into the idea that depression is caused by a “chemical imbalance” — specifically, having “too little serotonin.” However, depression cannot accurately be explained as simply having low serotonin. Current evidence suggests MDD involves much more complex interactions among neurotransmitter systems, neural circuits, genetics, cognition, and environmental experiences.

A conceptual chain

A useful way to hold this together:

Neurotransmitter system → communication between neurons → functioning of neural networks → psychological processes and behavior → possible contribution to depressive symptoms

This is a proposed contribution, not a proven single causal pathway. An association does not establish causation, and no single neurotransmitter explains MDD on its own.

So why do antidepressants that affect serotonin help some people if depression is not simply caused by low serotonin?
Neurotransmission involves release, binding and reuptake; Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) affect serotonin signaling within that process; and the research evidence challenges simple chemical-imbalance explanations.

Reductionism

Explaining MDD entirely through genes, brain activity, or neurotransmitters would be reductionist — reducing a complex disorder to simpler processes while overlooking other important influences. Because MDD is influenced by interacting biological, cognitive, social, cultural and environmental factors, a purely biological explanation may identify important mechanisms but neglect other levels of explanation.

Evaluating the biological evidence

Genetic evidence: McGuffin et al. (1996) supports genetic vulnerability because MZ concordance was higher than DZ concordance. However, MZ concordance was only about 46%, not 100%, showing that genetic similarity alone cannot determine who develops MDD.

Brain-imaging evidence: Research can identify brain differences associated with MDD, but cannot by itself establish whether those differences caused depression or resulted from it.

Neurotransmitter evidence: Neurotransmitter systems are biologically relevant, but the simple claim that MDD is caused by a single chemical imbalance is inadequate.

Evidence from beyond biology: Stressful life experiences, cognitive processes, and cultural and environmental differences provide additional evidence that a complete explanation cannot rely on biology alone. The effectiveness of psychological treatments such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) in reducing depressive symptoms also supports explanations beyond a purely biological account.

Does having a biological vulnerability mean you will develop depression?

Yes or No?
No. Biological vulnerability increases probability, not certainty. A person may have genetic or biological risk factors without developing MDD, while environmental experiences and psychological factors can influence whether that vulnerability develops into a disorder.

This raises the next question: what happens when a vulnerability interacts with stressful life experiences?

Vulnerability to depression can interact with stressful environmental experiences — the Diathesis–Stress Model explains how.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Genetic evidence supports biological vulnerability, but MZ concordance below 100% shows that genes are not deterministic.
  2. MDD is polygenic: many genetic variants contribute small amounts of risk rather than there being one “depression gene.”
  3. Brain and neurotransmitter differences can support biological involvement in MDD, but association does not automatically establish causation.
  4. Biological explanations provide important evidence but can become reductionist if they ignore interacting cognitive, social, cultural and environmental influences.

Diathesis–Stress Model

How vulnerability and environment interact to influence mental health.

Why can two people experience the same stressful event, but only one develops depression?

Imagine two students who both fail an important examination — the same stressful event. One is disappointed for a few days, talks it through with friends, and starts planning a resit. The other cannot stop thinking about the result, stops attending classes, and describes feeling worthless for weeks afterwards.

Notice first what this example does not show: responding differently to an event is not the same as developing Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) or not developing it. A strong emotional reaction to failing an exam is an ordinary response, not a diagnosis. But people do differ in how low they become and for how long, and this slide introduces the Diathesis–Stress Model as one explanation of why the probability of developing MDD can differ between people: it combines pre-existing vulnerabilities, life circumstances, coping resources, and the support available to each person.

The Core Model

The Diathesis–Stress Model proposes that a person's underlying vulnerability interacts with stressful experiences to influence their probability of developing a psychological disorder such as MDD. Neither ingredient alone tells the whole story — the model's central claim is about how they combine.

Diathesis (vulnerability). A pre-existing susceptibility that may increase the likelihood of developing depression. It is present before the stressful event arrives, and it can take several forms:

Biological vulnerability

Example: a student whose mother and grandmother have both experienced depression.

Inherited susceptibility exists in the person before any current stressor occurs, so it is genuinely pre-existing — though a family history is a risk marker, not proof that this individual inherited a specific vulnerability.

Cognitive vulnerability

Example: a student who habitually interprets a friend's short reply as “they must be annoyed with me” and replays the thought for days.

Negative thinking habits are stable patterns that exist before the stressful event and shape how it is interpreted — the same ambiguous event is processed differently by different people.

Developmental vulnerability

Example: someone whose early experiences included prolonged insecurity or loss.

Earlier experiences can contribute to later vulnerability, for example by shaping how safe, capable, or supported a person expects the world to be when new stress arrives.

Stress. Difficult experiences or environmental demands that may increase psychological strain. Examples include bereavement, relationship breakdown, prolonged unemployment, financial insecurity, and social isolation. What counts as stressful depends partly on the person and their circumstances — the same demand is not experienced identically by everyone.

Interaction. The effect of stress may depend partly on a person's vulnerability, and the influence of vulnerability may depend partly on their experiences. For example, one demanding term of revision pressure may be manageable for a student with few vulnerabilities, while the same term overwhelms a classmate whose negative thinking turns every small setback into proof of failure. The stress is similar; its effect is not.

Probability. The model describes changes in risk, not certain outcomes. Vulnerability and stress shift the likelihood of developing MDD; they do not guarantee it or rule it out.

Common confusion. A family history of depression is one possible source of vulnerability — but having a family history does not guarantee a high diathesis, and having no family history does not mean having no vulnerability. Cognitive and developmental factors can create vulnerability in anyone.

Cognitive Vulnerability: Rumination

Rumination is repetitive thinking about distressing experiences, emotions, or problems, often without moving toward effective problem-solving.

A student receives a disappointing examination result. That evening they repeatedly think — and think again — about the result, what it says about them, and what might go wrong next. The examination is finished, but their attention keeps returning to it, and no plan for preparing differently ever forms. This is why rumination can act as a cognitive vulnerability: it keeps attention on negative information, makes it harder to disengage from distressing thoughts, and can maintain low mood — particularly when combined with stressful experiences.

Rumination

“Why am I always such a failure? What if everything goes wrong?”

Constructive reflection

“Which questions did I struggle with, and how can I prepare differently?”

Both involve thinking about a negative event, so they can look similar from the outside. They differ in focus and direction. The ruminating thought is repetitive, evaluative and self-focused — it returns to the same conclusion about the person rather than moving anywhere. The reflective thought is specific and future-oriented — it identifies something concrete to do next. Repeating the first kind of thought for an evening typically maintains distress; the second kind can reduce it by converting the setback into a plan.

Research has reported associations between rumination and activity in the prefrontal cortex, the amygdala, and the default mode network (DMN) — regions involved in attention control, emotional processing, and internally focused thought respectively. Some researchers propose that rumination persists because of interactions between these systems: difficulty disengaging attention from negative material, and repetitive self-focused thought. These are proposed explanations supported by observed associations, not settled proof of one mechanism. The details matter less for this model than the core idea: thinking patterns are one way vulnerability can be cognitive rather than genetic.

Case Study: Applying the Model

Case

Sarah is 19 years old. Her mother and aunt have both been diagnosed with depression. Sarah has always tended to worry excessively and interpret ambiguous events negatively. Last month, her close friendship group dissolved after a conflict, and she has been spending most of her time alone. She has also been sleeping poorly and finding it difficult to concentrate on her university work. Sarah plays football twice a week.

1. Identify Sarah's possible vulnerabilities.

Two kinds. First, a family history of depression (her mother and aunt) — a biological risk marker. Note the distinction: family history is not direct evidence that Sarah personally inherited a specific genetic vulnerability; it shows elevated family risk, which can reflect shared genes or shared environment. Second, a cognitive vulnerability: a long-standing tendency to worry excessively and interpret ambiguous events negatively. Both were present before the recent events, which is what makes them vulnerabilities rather than reactions.

2. Identify her environmental stressors.

The main stressor is the dissolution of her close friendship group after a conflict — a significant relationship loss. Its consequence, spending most of her time alone, is social isolation, which can itself act as an ongoing stressor. Her poor sleep and difficulty concentrating are better described as signs of strain than as separate stressors, though they can make coping with the original stressor harder.

3. Explain how these factors might interact.

Her cognitive vulnerability shapes how the stressor is processed: a tendency to interpret ambiguous events negatively can turn a friendship conflict into evidence that she is unlikeable, deepening the loss. The isolation then removes social contact that might otherwise buffer the stress — leaving her alone with repetitive negative interpretations. The interaction prediction is that this combination carries a higher probability of a depressive episode than the same stressor would for someone without these vulnerabilities — a prediction about probability, not a conclusion that Sarah has, or will develop, MDD.

4. Identify an existing protective factor.

Sarah plays football twice a week. That routine provides social contact, physical activity, and structure — all of which can lessen the impact of the friendship loss by keeping other relationships and a regular schedule in place. It is a real protective factor even though her vulnerability and the stressor remain.

5. Explain why the case does not establish that Sarah has MDD.

A scenario describes risk factors, not a diagnosis. The Diathesis–Stress Model predicts probabilities — it cannot tell you that a particular person has a disorder. Some details in the case (poor sleep, poor concentration) overlap with depression symptoms, but a diagnosis requires a clinical assessment against criteria such as symptom number, duration, and impairment — none of which a paragraph can establish. The case is useful for identifying vulnerabilities, stressors, interactions, and protective factors; it is not evidence about Sarah's diagnostic status.

Protective Factors

Protective factors are influences that may reduce vulnerability, lessen the impact of stress, or support recovery and coping. The model is not only about two risk factors stacking up — what a person has around and inside them also shapes the outcome:

Social support

Example: a student experiencing bereavement has trusted friends and family members who provide emotional and practical assistance.

Support can lessen the impact of the stressor itself — grief is shared rather than carried alone, practical burdens are reduced, and someone is present to notice if the person is struggling.

Coping strategies

Example: a student facing examination pressure uses realistic planning, problem-solving, and support rather than repeatedly avoiding the problem.

Coping changes the person's response to the same demand: a problem broken into planned steps usually strains a person less than the same problem avoided, which lets stress accumulate.

Access to support

Example: someone experiencing prolonged difficulties can obtain appropriate professional or community support.

Access turns potential help into actual help. Without it, even strong support networks and effective services cannot act — knowing help exists and being able to reach it are different things.

No guarantee. Protective factors reduce risk rather than guaranteeing protection against depression — someone can have strong support and still develop MDD, just as low vulnerability does not guarantee safety.

In the case of Sarah, what protective factor does she already have, and how might adding another change her risk?

Sarah plays football twice a week, which provides social contact, physical activity, and routine — these are protective. If she also had a close friend she could confide in, or accessed university counselling, additional protective factors could reduce her risk by providing social support and coping resources, even though her vulnerability and the friendship stressor remain.

Research Evidence

What would evidence for this model look like? Ideally: a measure of vulnerability, a measure of stress, and a measure of depression outcomes, showing that their combination predicts outcomes better than either alone. Two studies are often discussed — for different reasons.

McGuffin et al. (1996) — twin evidence on vulnerability

Aim and sample: to estimate the contribution of genes to liability to DSM-IV major depression. The researchers studied 177 people with major depressive disorder (probands) from the Maudsley Hospital Twin Register in London, together with their same-sex co-twins. Assessments of the co-twins were carried out by researchers blind to zygosity and to the proband's history.

Findings: probandwise concordance for major depression was 46% in monozygotic (MZ) twins and 20% in dizygotic (DZ) twins — a large difference, since MZ twins share essentially all their genes and DZ twins about half. But concordance was far from 100% even in MZ pairs.

Interpretation: the pattern supports a genetic contribution to depression without genetic determinism — identical genes produced the disorder in both twins less than half the time. This is consistent with a probabilistic, multifactorial account.

Limitations: the study was not a direct test of the Diathesis–Stress Model — it measured genetic similarity and depression, not how vulnerability and stress combine, so it cannot show that a particular vulnerability–stress interaction occurred or that stress is necessary. The hospital-based sample may not represent depression in the wider population, and zygosity was established before routine DNA testing.

Caspi et al. (2003) — a direct test of a gene×stress interaction

Aim: to test why stressful experiences lead to depression in some people but not others — specifically, whether a variant of the serotonin transporter gene (5-HTTLPR) moderates the effect of stressful life events on depression.

Participants and design: the Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study — a representative birth cohort of 1,037 children born in Dunedin, New Zealand, in 1972–73, assessed repeatedly from age 3, with 96% of the cohort still participating at age 26. This prospective design means stress was measured before the outcomes, not recalled afterwards.

Genetic measure: 847 cohort members (all of European ancestry, to avoid confounds from population stratification) were genotyped for the 5-HTTLPR polymorphism: 17% carried two short alleles, 51% one short and one long, 31% two long alleles.

Stress measure: stressful life events between the 21st and 26th birthdays were recorded with a life-history calendar covering 14 kinds of event (employment, financial, housing, health, and relationship stressors). 30% reported no such events, 25% one, 20% two, 11% three, and 15% four or more — with no significant difference between genotype groups, so carriers were not simply attracting more stress. Childhood maltreatment was also measured.

Outcome measure: past-year depression at age 26 assessed with the Diagnostic Interview Schedule, yielding both symptom scores and DSM-IV major depressive episode diagnoses (17% of the sample met criteria), plus measures of suicidal ideation and informant reports from people who knew each study member well.

Reported findings: among carriers of a short allele, depression symptoms and diagnoses increased with the number of life events; among those with two long alleles, they did not. For example, among cohort members experiencing four or more stressful life events, about 33% of short-allele carriers were diagnosed with a major depressive episode compared with about 17% of those with two long alleles. The authors concluded this was evidence of a gene-by-environment interaction — and they themselves noted that the finding awaited replication.

Limitations: the study is observational, so it cannot prove causation; it tested a single candidate gene chosen in advance; and the genotyped sample was one ancestry group within one national cohort, limiting generalizability. There was no main effect of the gene alone — risk appeared only in combination with stress, which is exactly why the finding attracted so much attention.

Replication status — read this before citing Caspi et al. The specific 5-HTTLPR×stress finding has not been consistently replicated, and it must not be treated as an established fact. Risch et al. (2009) re-analysed data from 14 studies (over 14,000 participants) and found no interaction, while confirming that stressful life events themselves are strongly associated with depression risk. Culverhouse et al. (2018) combined 31 datasets (over 38,000 participants of European ancestry) and found no support for the interaction in any subgroup examined. Other meta-analyses have reported support for the interaction, but they have been criticized for combining studies with very different measures and statistical models. The overall picture: the original result remains contested, and the consistent finding across all sides is that stress matters and the gene alone does not.

The broader Diathesis–Stress framework does not depend on this one genetic candidate. Independent lines of evidence — twin studies showing genetic contributions without determinism, the strong association between stressful life events and depression, and research linking cognitive styles such as rumination to depression risk — all support the general idea that vulnerability and stress jointly shape risk, whatever the specific biological markers turn out to be.

Evaluation

Strength 1 — Integrative explanation. The model combines biological, cognitive, developmental, and environmental influences rather than reducing depression to a single cause. Each restricted account leaves evidence out: a purely genetic account struggles with the ~46% MZ concordance (genes alone do not decide), while a purely environmental account struggles with why people exposed to the same events differ. The Diathesis–Stress framework can accommodate both at once — which is exactly what McGuffin et al.'s non-deterministic concordance and the strong life-events effect both require.

Strength 2 — Individual differences. The model explains why people exposed to similar stressors can have different outcomes. In Example A, the same breakup carried different probabilities for two students; in Caspi et al., carriers of the same allele were not uniformly depressed — risk depended on the stress they experienced. Single-factor models have no natural way to express this.

Strength 3 — Practical applications. If vulnerability, stress, and protective factors can be identified, prevention and early intervention can be targeted: monitoring students with family histories during high-stress periods, teaching coping strategies, and strengthening access to support. Identifying risk factors does not guarantee that interventions will work — but it gives them a place to start.

Limitation 1 — Measurement. Vulnerability and stress are difficult to operationalize. Consider two people who are both “unemployed”: one has savings, a supportive family, and expects to find work soon; the other faces financial insecurity, has no one to turn to, and has been searching for a year. Which is experiencing “the same stress”? Depending on how researchers measure it — events counted, severity rated, or impact self-reported — studies can reach different conclusions about the same lives.

Limitation 2 — Causality. Finding that vulnerable people under stress experience more depression does not prove the interaction caused the depression. Stress exposure may itself relate to vulnerability (for example, some genes may influence the situations people enter, a possibility Caspi et al. themselves acknowledged), and depression can generate stressors as well as result from them. Prospective designs such as Dunedin's — measuring stress before outcomes — strengthen the inference, and yet the key specific finding still failed to replicate, a reminder that even strong designs do not settle questions on their own.

Limitation 3 — Prediction. The model cannot precisely predict which individual will develop MDD. In Caspi et al., among short-allele carriers with four or more stressful life events — the highest-risk profile reported — about two-thirds were not diagnosed. Elevated risk is a statement about probabilities in groups, not about a named person's future, which is also why the simulator's bar must not be read as a personal risk calculator.

Limitation 4 — Oversimplification. A two-variable diagram hides real complexity: people carry multiple vulnerabilities at once, stress comes in different forms and durations, and resilience and protective factors interact with both. Some people with high vulnerability and high stress never develop MDD — the four-box illustration cannot capture why without adding more factors than a simple diagram can hold.

Can the model predict exactly who will develop MDD?

No. The model is probabilistic: individual vulnerabilities and stressors are difficult to measure precisely, people exposed to apparently similar combinations can still have different outcomes, and even the highest-risk profiles in research (such as Caspi et al.'s short-allele carriers with four or more life events) included many people who were not diagnosed.

How Does Serotonin Communication Work?

Serotonin is a neurotransmitter, meaning it is one of the chemical messengers neurons use to communicate. Serotonin signaling is involved in a wide range of functions, including mood, sleep, appetite and aspects of emotional processing. This is one reason psychologists have been interested in its possible relationship with depression.

When a neuron releases serotonin, it enters the tiny gap between neurons called the synapse. Some of the serotonin binds to receptors on the receiving neuron, influencing its activity. Serotonin does not simply remain in the synapse indefinitely: some is taken back into the neuron that released it through a process called reuptake.

This gives us the basic process we need to understand the debate about depression. If serotonin is involved in functions that are often disrupted in MDD, such as mood, sleep and appetite, could differences in serotonin signaling contribute to depression?

That is a reasonable hypothesis. But there is an important difference between saying that serotonin may be involved in depression and saying that depression is caused by having too little serotonin. The second claim is much stronger and requires much stronger evidence.

The simple serotonin hypothesis proposed that depression results from abnormally low serotonin activity. In other words, low serotonin was not merely associated with depression; it was proposed as part of its cause.

That gives psychologists something they can test. If low serotonin were a general cause of depression, we would expect people with depression to consistently show evidence of reduced serotonin functioning. We might also expect experimentally reducing serotonin availability to increase the likelihood of depressive symptoms.

Researchers can therefore ask whether these predictions are actually supported by the evidence. This is important because showing that serotonin plays a role in mood does not, by itself, show that insufficient serotonin causes MDD.

This question becomes particularly interesting when we consider selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), a widely used class of antidepressants.

SSRIs reduce the reuptake of serotonin after it has been released. This leaves serotonin available in the synapse for longer and changes serotonin signaling. For some people with depression, treatment with an SSRI reduces their symptoms.

It is tempting to reason backwards from this and say: if increasing serotonin helps depression, depression must have been caused by too little serotonin.

But that conclusion does not follow. A treatment can change a biological system and improve a condition without identifying what originally caused the condition. A painkiller can reduce a headache, for example, but that does not mean the headache was caused by a deficiency of painkillers.

SSRIs therefore provide evidence that altering serotonin signaling can influence depressive symptoms. They do not, by themselves, prove that a serotonin deficiency caused those symptoms in the first place.

There is another reason the simple explanation becomes difficult.

SSRIs begin affecting serotonin reuptake relatively quickly, whereas improvement in depressive symptoms, when it occurs, generally develops more slowly. If simply increasing the amount of serotonin available in the synapse directly corrected the underlying problem, we might expect improvement to follow that change much more closely.

The delay suggests that antidepressant effects involve processes occurring after the initial change in serotonin signaling. We do not need to conclude from this that serotonin is unimportant. Rather, it tells us that the explanation “low serotonin caused the depression, and the SSRI simply corrected the shortage” is probably too simple.

This brings us to the evidence for the serotonin hypothesis itself.

In 2022, Moncrieff and colleagues published a major umbrella review examining several areas of research that had been used to investigate the relationship between serotonin and depression. Instead of relying on one experiment, they considered different types of evidence that should help us judge whether the serotonin-deficiency explanation holds up.

For example, researchers have investigated whether people with depression consistently show lower serotonin activity, whether differences in serotonin receptors or transporters are consistently associated with depression, and what happens when serotonin availability is experimentally reduced.

Across these areas, Moncrieff and colleagues concluded that the evidence did not consistently support the idea that depression is caused by abnormally low serotonin.

That finding needs to be interpreted carefully. It does not demonstrate that serotonin has nothing to do with depression. Nor does it demonstrate that SSRIs cannot be effective. Instead, it challenges a much more specific claim: that depression can be explained as a straightforward serotonin deficiency.

This leaves us with a more nuanced biological explanation. Serotonin signaling may be involved in depression and in how antidepressant treatments influence symptoms, but the evidence does not support reducing MDD to a simple chemical shortage.

Jamie's conclusion confuses a treatment effect with evidence about the original cause of the disorder. An SSRI can alter serotonin signaling and reduce symptoms without proving that the depression was caused by a serotonin deficiency. Treatment response therefore does not establish the original cause.

Beck's Cognitive Model of Depression

Beck’s cognitive model proposes that the way a person interprets experiences can contribute to depression.

Previous experiences may contribute to underlying negative schemas — organized beliefs about ourselves and the world. These schemas can influence how new situations are interpreted and can make negative automatic thoughts more likely.

Imagine Sofia receives a lower grade than she expected on an important test.

Sofia already has a negative schema about herself: she believes that she is not intelligent enough.

Because of this underlying belief, the disappointing grade may trigger an automatic thought such as:

‘I knew it. I’m a failure.’

Another student receiving exactly the same grade might think:

‘I didn’t prepare well enough this time. I need to change how I study.’

The event is the same, but the interpretation is different. Beck’s model focuses on how underlying beliefs can influence the meaning a person gives to experiences.

Beck proposed that depression is often associated with negative thinking in three related areas:

The self: negative beliefs about who I am.

For Sofia:

‘I’m a failure.’

The world: negative beliefs about experiences or the environment around me.

For Sofia:

‘Nothing ever works out for me.’

The future: negative expectations about what will happen next.

For Sofia:

‘I’m never going to succeed.’

These three patterns form the negative cognitive triad.

The important idea is not simply that someone has an occasional negative thought. The model proposes that persistent patterns of negative interpretation can contribute to and maintain depressive symptoms.

If Sofia repeatedly interprets setbacks as evidence that she is worthless, that the world is against her and that her future is hopeless, these interpretations may contribute to persistent low mood, hopelessness and withdrawal.

Withdrawal can also reduce opportunities for positive experiences. For example, if Sofia stops studying, seeing friends or participating in activities because she expects failure, she may experience fewer events that challenge her negative beliefs.

This helps explain how patterns of thinking and behavior may reinforce one another over time.

Beck’s model is valuable because it provides a clear psychological explanation for how patterns of interpretation may contribute to depression.

It can help explain why two people experiencing a similar event may respond differently. The event itself does not automatically determine the emotional outcome; existing beliefs can influence how the event is interpreted.

The model also has practical value. If negative interpretations contribute to depressive symptoms, psychologists can try to identify and change these patterns. This idea helped provide the theoretical basis for cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT).

Research has also found associations between negative thinking patterns and depression, providing support for the idea that cognition is relevant to the disorder.

One limitation is that negative thinking and depression can influence each other.

For example, negative schemas and automatic thoughts may contribute to the development or maintenance of depressive symptoms.

But experiencing depression may also make negative thoughts more frequent.

This makes causality difficult to establish. If negative thinking and depression occur together, we cannot automatically conclude that the thinking caused the disorder.

Beck’s model is therefore useful for understanding an important psychological process involved in depression, but it is not a complete explanation of every case. Biological vulnerability, stressful experiences and other factors may also contribute.

Gotlib and Joormann (2010) reviewed extensive evidence that people with depression show biased attention towards negative stimuli, biased memory for negative events, and difficulty disengaging from negative material, supporting Beck’s claim that biased processing maintains depression.

Rueger and colleagues (2011) found that negative cognitive styles predicted depression in adolescents, supporting the idea that schemas can develop early and predispose individuals to depression when activated by stress.

Sofia and Daniel both receive the same disappointing test result. Sofia thinks, ‘I’m useless and I’ll never succeed,’ while Daniel thinks, ‘I need to prepare differently next time.’ How would Beck’s model explain why the same event might affect them differently?
Beck’s model proposes that underlying schemas influence how people interpret events. Sofia may have an existing negative schema about herself, so the disappointing grade activates automatic negative thoughts about herself and her future. Daniel interprets the same event as a specific problem that can be changed. The different interpretations could therefore contribute to different emotional responses even though the external event was the same.

Environmental Factors & Depression

Why do some people develop depression after difficult experiences while others do not?

The diathesis-stress model proposes that vulnerability and environmental stress interact to influence depression risk. Even individuals with similar genetic vulnerabilities may experience different outcomes depending on their environments.

Why might losing a job, experiencing financial hardship or becoming socially isolated increase someone's risk of depression? And why do people facing similar circumstances sometimes have very different outcomes?

Environmental explanations examine how life experiences and social circumstances may contribute to Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). Rather than locating the explanation entirely within the individual, they consider the conditions in which people live.

These conditions can increase depression risk, but they do not determine who develops the disorder. To understand their influence, psychologists examine stressful experiences, the processes through which they affect people, and the research evidence connecting them to depression.

Psychologists distinguish between two broad types of stressor.

An acute stressor is a relatively sudden or time-limited event, such as bereavement, relationship breakdown or losing a job.

A chronic stressor is an ongoing difficulty, such as financial insecurity, persistent family conflict or long-term social isolation.

These categories often overlap. A single event can produce consequences that continue long after the event itself. For example, losing a job may initially be an acute stressor. However, financial pressure, uncertainty, disruption to daily routine and reduced contact with colleagues may continue for months.

The psychological impact of stress depends partly on its severity, duration and accumulation. Several overlapping difficulties can place greater demands on a person's coping resources than one isolated event.

Someone loses their job and subsequently experiences six months of financial difficulties and social isolation. Identify the acute stressor and two chronic stressors.
Losing the job is the acute stressor. Continuing financial insecurity and social isolation are chronic stressors. The original event has produced ongoing difficulties that may increase psychological strain.

Identifying a stressful experience is only the beginning of an explanation. Psychologists also want to understand the processes through which that experience might contribute to depressive symptoms.

Unemployment and Job Insecurity

Employment provides more than income. It can offer structure, social relationships, rewarding activity, a sense of purpose and some control over everyday life.

Unemployment may disrupt several of these simultaneously. Financial worries can become persistent, daily routines may disappear, and reduced contact with colleagues can increase isolation.

These changes may contribute to low mood, hopelessness and withdrawal, particularly when unemployment continues for a prolonged period.

Poverty and Financial Insecurity

Financial insecurity can expose people to persistent stress concerning housing, food, bills and other essential needs.

It may also restrict access to healthcare, leisure activities and supportive resources. Someone experiencing prolonged financial pressure may have fewer opportunities for rest, recovery and positive experiences.

The relationship is therefore not simply that having less money produces depression. Economic hardship can create several overlapping conditions that increase psychological vulnerability.

Social Isolation and Lack of Support

Social relationships provide emotional reassurance, practical assistance and a sense of belonging.

When these relationships are absent or disrupted, people may have fewer opportunities to share difficulties, receive encouragement or participate in rewarding activities.

Isolation can therefore contribute to depressive symptoms through loneliness, reduced support and fewer positive experiences.

Chronic Conflict and Adversity

Ongoing family conflict, discrimination, unstable housing and other prolonged difficulties can expose individuals to repeated psychological strain.

Unlike a temporary stressful event, chronic adversity may offer little opportunity for recovery. It can also reduce a person's sense of control over their circumstances.

Over time, this sustained pressure may increase vulnerability to depression.

Connecting the Explanations

Environmental influences frequently occur together. Consider someone who loses their job. The resulting financial difficulties may create relationship conflict, while the loss of workplace relationships increases isolation. The person is now dealing with several connected pressures rather than one independent stressor.

Environmental explanations therefore examine both individual experiences and the wider circumstances that can maintain psychological strain.

Explain two different ways unemployment might contribute to depression. Do not simply identify the stressors.
Unemployment may create prolonged financial insecurity, producing continuing stress and uncertainty about the future. It may also disrupt daily routines and social relationships, reducing opportunities for support, rewarding activity and a sense of purpose. These processes can increase vulnerability to depressive symptoms.

Environmental stress does not affect everyone equally. Two people might experience the same job loss yet differ in their financial resources, social relationships, coping strategies and underlying vulnerabilities.

Protective factors are conditions or resources that may reduce the impact of stressful experiences. Examples include:

  • Supportive relationships and practical assistance.
  • Financial security and stable living conditions.
  • Effective coping strategies.
  • Regular routines and rewarding activities.
  • Access to healthcare and psychological support.

Social support can act as a stress buffer. For example, a person who loses their job may still experience considerable stress. However, supportive friends may provide reassurance, while family members may offer practical assistance. These resources can reduce the psychological burden even though the unemployment itself remains unresolved.

Protective factors reduce risk rather than guarantee protection.

Two people lose their jobs. One has savings, supportive relationships and a stable daily routine. The other has little financial security and limited social contact. Explain why their risk of depression might differ.
The first person has several protective factors that may buffer the effects of unemployment. Financial security reduces immediate economic pressure, social relationships provide emotional and practical support, and a stable routine maintains structure and rewarding activity. The second person faces greater ongoing strain with fewer resources to manage it.

George Brown and Harris (1978) investigated the relationship between stressful life experiences, social circumstances and depression among women in London.

The Investigation

Their research included a community sample of 458 women from Camberwell, South London, alongside women experiencing a recent onset of depression.

The researchers used detailed interviews to investigate severe life events and ongoing difficulties. Events were assessed in the context of each woman's circumstances rather than relying only on her emotional reaction to them.

This allowed the researchers to examine whether stressful experiences were more common before the onset of depression.

Findings

Women who developed depression were more likely to have experienced severe life events or major ongoing difficulties beforehand.

Brown and Harris referred to these experiences as provoking agents.

They also identified vulnerability factors that appeared to increase risk when provoking agents occurred. These included lacking a close, confiding relationship, losing one's mother before age 11, having three or more young children at home, and not working outside the home.

These findings suggested that environmental adversity and personal circumstances could interact in influencing depression risk.

Not every woman exposed to severe adversity developed depression.

Interpreting the Findings

The research provided evidence that stressful life experiences and social circumstances are associated with the onset of depression.

It supports environmental explanations while also suggesting that individual vulnerability affects responses to adversity.

However, the findings do not establish that stressful events independently caused depression.

What does Brown and Harris's research suggest about the relationship between stressful life experiences and depression?
Severe life events and major ongoing difficulties were more common before the onset of depression among the women studied. This supports an association between environmental stress and depression and suggests that vulnerability factors may influence responses to adversity.

Suppose researchers discover that unemployed people report higher levels of depression than employed people. Several explanations are possible.

Environmental contribution: Unemployment may increase depression risk through financial pressure, reduced social contact and disrupted routines.

Reverse causation: Existing depressive symptoms may make it harder to find or maintain employment.

Third variables: Other influences, such as chronic illness or earlier adversity, may increase both unemployment risk and depression risk.

These possibilities illustrate why correlation does not automatically establish causation.

Brown and Harris strengthened the environmental argument by examining whether stressful experiences occurred before depression began. Establishing this temporal order makes an environmental contribution more plausible.

However, their research was observational and relied partly on retrospective accounts. It could not eliminate alternative explanations, including third variables and possible recall bias.

Their sample also limits generalization because it focused on women from a particular social and historical context.

The justified conclusion is that stressful life experiences are associated with increased depression risk, rather than that the study conclusively proved a single causal pathway.

A student claims, "Brown and Harris proved that stressful life events cause depression." Explain why this conclusion is too strong.
Brown and Harris found an association between severe life events and the onset of depression, with stressful experiences often occurring beforehand. However, the study was observational and partly retrospective. Other factors may have influenced both stressful experiences and depression, and the research could not eliminate every alternative explanation.

Environmental explanations become more complete when considered alongside biological and cognitive influences.

The Diathesis–Stress Model proposes that environmental stress interacts with an underlying vulnerability, or diathesis, to influence the probability of developing depression.

Vulnerability may be biological, such as inherited susceptibility, or cognitive, such as persistent negative schemas and a tendency to interpret setbacks negatively.

Consider two people experiencing prolonged unemployment. One has a family history of depression and tends to interpret setbacks as evidence of personal failure. The other has fewer identifiable vulnerabilities and more effective coping resources.

The same environmental experience may therefore have different psychological consequences.

The model does not suggest that either person is certain to develop or avoid depression. Instead, it explains why environmental stress may have different effects depending on individual vulnerability and available protective factors.

Why is the Diathesis–Stress Model more comprehensive than an explanation based only on stressful life events?
It considers how environmental stress interacts with pre-existing biological or cognitive vulnerability. Stressful experiences may increase risk, but the effects can differ depending on the individual's susceptibility and protective resources.

Environmental explanations identify circumstances and experiences associated with increased depression risk. They also have practical value because some risks may be reduced through social support, improved living conditions and access to services.

However, several limitations must be considered.

Causality

Observational research cannot always distinguish whether adversity contributes to depression, depression contributes to adversity, or other variables influence both.

Measurement

Stress is difficult to measure objectively. The same event may have different meanings and consequences depending on an individual's circumstances. Brown and Harris attempted to address this by assessing events in their social context.

Individual Differences

Environmental explanations alone cannot fully account for why people exposed to similar adversity experience different outcomes. Biological susceptibility, cognitive processes, coping resources and protective factors also matter.

A comprehensive understanding of MDD therefore considers interactions between environmental experiences, biological vulnerability and cognitive processes.

Culture & Depression

Why might depression appear more common in some countries than others?

Depression may be communicated through emotional or physical symptoms. Cultural expectations can influence which symptoms people report first, affecting clinical recognition and diagnosis.

The Puzzle: Do Depression Rates Really Differ Across Cultures?

Imagine two countries carry out research into depression.

  • Country A reports that 12% of people experience depression.
  • Country B reports that 4% experience depression.

Does this mean people in Country A are three times as likely to experience depression? Not necessarily. To understand why, it helps to separate three quantities that are often confused.

  • Actual prevalence — the true proportion of people in a population who meet the criteria for depression. This is what researchers really want to know, but it can never be observed directly.
  • Recorded (diagnosed) prevalence — the proportion of people who have been formally diagnosed and entered into health-service records. This depends not only on who is depressed, but on who seeks help, who is recognized, and who ends up in the records.
  • Survey-estimated prevalence — the proportion who score above a cut-off on a screening questionnaire in a population survey. This depends on whether the questionnaire measures depressive symptoms comparably across the people being surveyed.

These three numbers can differ even within one population — and they can differ for very different reasons between populations.

All figures in these examples are hypothetical and used for teaching. They are not real reported statistics.

The recorded prevalence of depression is lower in Country B than in Country A. Why can this alone NOT establish that Country B has less depression?
Recorded prevalence counts only people who were diagnosed and entered into records. That depends on help-seeking, clinical recognition and reporting practices, not just on who is actually depressed. Country B might have fewer recorded cases because of greater stigma, poorer access to services, or a measure that under-recognizes certain presentations — even if actual depression is equally common. Comparing countries requires evidence that depression was measured comparably, not just a comparison of recorded numbers.

It is tempting to treat any reported figure as an accurate measure of “how much depression there is”. But each way of counting depression captures a slightly different group of people.

A Worked Example (Hypothetical)

Suppose careful clinical studies showed that about 5% of adults in both Country A and Country B actually meet the criteria for depression. Now suppose:

  • Country A has well-funded mental-health services, relatively low stigma and routine screening, so most cases are diagnosed → recorded prevalence is around 4%.
  • Country B has limited services, greater stigma and little screening, so many cases are never seen → recorded prevalence is around 1%.
  • A population survey that focuses on emotional symptoms returns 12% in Country A.
  • The same survey, translated and used in Country B, returns 4%.

Nothing about the underlying depression changed between the countries. The recorded and surveyed numbers differed because of help-seeking, recognition, stigma and measurement. This is why a difference in recorded or surveyed rates is not the same thing as a difference in actual depression.

Key idea: before asking “which country has more depression?”, ask “which number are we comparing, and was it produced the same way in both places?”

Depression does not always look like someone saying, “I feel sad.” A somatic symptom is a physical symptom — for example persistent tiredness, headaches, changes in sleep or appetite, or aches and pains — that a person experiences in the body. Somatic symptoms are common in depression and are part of its standard symptom picture.

Culture can influence several things:

  • Which symptoms receive attention: people learn, within their culture, which experiences are treated as a “real” illness and worth reporting.
  • How distress is communicated: in some contexts it may be more acceptable to describe bodily suffering than emotional suffering, so distress is expressed through the body.
  • What a person raises first: the presenting complaint is the problem a person chooses to mention to a clinician first. It is not necessarily their complete symptom profile.

Two Students, Two Descriptions

Imagine two university students experiencing similar difficulties.

Student A tells a doctor: “I feel hopeless, and nothing makes me happy anymore.” Student B tells a doctor: “I’m exhausted all the time. I can’t sleep properly, and I keep getting headaches.”

Both students might be experiencing depression, but they described their difficulties differently. It would be a mistake to conclude that Student B only has physical symptoms. Reporting physical symptoms does not mean someone lacks emotional symptoms — those symptoms may be present but not mentioned, not noticed, or not yet put into words. Equally, physical symptoms can have many non-psychiatric causes, such as anemia or a thyroid problem, so a clinician must investigate rather than assume depression.

If a clinician relies only on the presenting complaint, or uses a questionnaire focused on emotional wording, Student B’s depression may be less likely to be recognized — even though their difficulties are just as real. Recognizing depression therefore means assessing the full range of symptoms, not just the one a person mentions first.

An important caution: it would be wrong to claim that “Western people express depression emotionally, while Asian people express it physically.” People within every culture vary, group-level tendencies are not rules for individuals, and patterns change over time as societies change.

A patient mentions only chronic headaches and tiredness, and does not mention feeling low. Why should a clinician not conclude that the patient has no emotional symptoms?
The headache and tiredness may be the patient’s presenting complaint rather than their full symptom profile. Emotional symptoms such as low mood or loss of interest may be present but not raised, not noticed, or considered less acceptable to discuss. At the same time, physical symptoms can have non-psychiatric causes. The clinician should assess the full range of symptoms and investigate possible physical causes rather than assume either that depression is absent or that it is definitely present.

Stigma refers to negative attitudes, judgments or social consequences attached to a characteristic or condition. Saying that stigma “affects reporting” is too vague; it is more useful to describe the mechanism:

Fear of judgment → reluctance to disclose symptoms or seek professional help → fewer recorded diagnoses.

The same mechanism can affect different kinds of data in different ways:

  • Clinical diagnosis statistics: stigma can reduce them directly, because people who fear judgment may never attend a service or may not be recorded as depressed.
  • Anonymous population surveys: anonymity removes some barriers, so surveys may capture more people than clinical records. But it does not remove all bias — people may still under-report symptoms, or answer in ways they believe are more acceptable.
  • Willingness to disclose: even when a person recognizes their symptoms, stigma can change what they are willing to say to a clinician, a researcher or their own family.

Key point: anonymous surveys can reduce some reporting barriers without eliminating reporting bias. Lower numbers are not automatically more accurate, and higher numbers are not automatically more truthful.

A Fictional Example

Ravi lives in a community where seeking help for mental-health difficulties is seen as a sign of weakness. He recognizes that he feels persistently low and has lost interest in things he used to enjoy, but he tells no one. When he finally visits a doctor for tiredness, he mentions only the physical symptoms and says nothing about his mood. A survey conducted door-to-door would also find him reluctant to admit to depressive symptoms, though he might answer more honestly on a fully anonymous form.

Ravi’s depression is real either way. Whether it appears in a dataset depends on how, and in what context, he is asked.

Why might a country’s anonymous survey estimate of depression be higher than its recorded diagnosis rate, and why is that not proof of anything by itself?
Recorded diagnoses depend on people seeking help and being recognized, which stigma and limited access can reduce. An anonymous survey removes some of those barriers, so more people may report symptoms. But this does not prove which figure is “correct”: the survey may also capture people who would not meet full diagnostic criteria, and reporting bias is reduced but not eliminated. The two figures are produced by different processes and measure slightly different things.

Psychologists often identify depression using questionnaires and diagnostic criteria. But a questionnaire developed in one cultural setting does not automatically work the same way elsewhere. Using the same questionnaire in two countries does not, by itself, mean it measures depression equivalently in both.

Several things can differ even when the words look identical:

  • Translation: translating words accurately may not preserve their psychological meaning. “Hopeless”, “guilt” or “depressed” may not carry the same connotations in another language.
  • Interpretation: two people may understand the same question differently because of cultural expectations or familiar ways of describing emotions.
  • Response styles: people differ in their willingness to endorse particular response categories — for instance, some groups are more reluctant to select extreme options regardless of their symptoms.
  • Construct validity: the deeper question is whether the scores actually represent the intended construct of depressive symptoms in that group.
  • Cross-cultural comparability: researchers need evidence that scores have comparable meanings across populations before comparing them.

Measurement Invariance (In Plain Terms)

Researchers call this idea measurement invariance: the concept that a measure operates comparably across groups. In simple terms, it asks whether the same score means the same thing in each group — whether people with the same underlying level of depressive symptoms would tend to receive similar scores, and whether the questions relate to each other in the same way. You do not need the statistics behind it; the key idea is that comparability has to be demonstrated, not assumed.

A Worked Questionnaire Example

Imagine a questionnaire asks people to rate, from 0 to 3, statements such as “I feel sad” and “I feel worthless”. In Country C, participants are cautious about endorsing strong emotional statements and rarely choose the highest options, so the average score is low. In Country D, participants use the full range of options, so the average score is higher.

It would be easy to conclude that Country D has more depression. But the difference may come from a response style (how people use the scale) rather than from more severe symptoms. The scores are not measuring the underlying construct in exactly the same way in both groups.

Distinguishing two possibilities: a difference in scores can reflect (a) measurement bias — the instrument behaves differently across groups — or (b) a genuine difference in symptom severity. Good research tries to rule out (a) before claiming (b).

Two countries use the identical translated questionnaire. Country D scores higher on average. Why can we not immediately conclude that people in Country D have more severe depression?
Using the same questionnaire does not guarantee that it measures depression equivalently in both groups. Differences in translation meaning, interpretation, response styles or construct validity can all raise or lower scores without any change in underlying depression. This is why researchers test measurement invariance — gathering evidence that scores mean the same thing in each group — before treating a score difference as a real difference in symptom severity.

Two separate 2001 publications by Gordon Parker and colleagues are often confused, so it is important to keep them apart.

Parker, Gladstone & Chee (2001): A Review

This paper, “Depression in the planet’s largest ethnic group: the Chinese” (published in the American Journal of Psychiatry), is a review. It brings together and interprets existing studies and reviews on emotional distress, depression, neurasthenia and somatisation in Chinese subjects. A review does not collect new participants; it evaluates and synthesises what other researchers have already found.

The review examined the claim that Chinese people tend to deny depression or express it somatically, and asked what might explain such a tendency. It highlighted that the evidence is hard to interpret because “the Chinese” are a highly heterogeneous group, and because many factors affect how data are collected: how illness is defined, how samples are chosen, how cases are found, differences in help-seeking, the idiomatic expression of distress, and the stigma of mental illness. The authors concluded that the available evidence does support a tendency to deny or somatise depression, but noted that Western influences on Chinese society had likely changed how depression is expressed since the early 1980s.

Parker, Cheah & Roy (2001): An Empirical Study

This separate paper, “Do the Chinese somatize depression? A cross-cultural study” (published in Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology), is original empirical research: the researchers collected their own data. They studied two sets of depressed outpatients — one group of Malaysian Chinese and one group of Australian Caucasian patients — matched by age and sex.

They asked each patient to nominate the prime symptom they presented with when they first sought help, and had them complete an inventory of both somatic and cognitive symptoms, ranking the three items that best captured their distress.

What Did They Find?

About 60% of the Malaysian Chinese patients nominated a somatic symptom as their main presenting complaint, compared with about 13% of the Australian patients. The Australian patients were more likely to nominate depressed mood, cognitive and anxiety items.

Crucially, the inventory revealed a more detailed picture: the Chinese patients scored only somewhat higher on somatic items, but were much less likely to affirm cognitive items of depression, producing significantly lower total inventory scores. Both groups reported a high rate of somatic and related items (such as insomnia).

These percentages describe the particular clinical samples, not entire national populations.

What Does This Tell Us — and Not Tell Us?

The study shows that cultural context can influence what patients present with and how they respond to symptom questions. It does not show that the groups had different underlying biology, different rates of depression, or a distinctly “Chinese” way of experiencing symptoms — indeed, the authors questioned a culture-specific somatic explanation, suggesting instead that depressed patients across cultures tend to experience and report bodily symptoms.

Evaluating the Evidence

A strength is that it is a direct cross-cultural comparison with matched groups, giving concrete evidence that symptom presentation can differ between clinical samples.

Limitation — Samples: the participants were outpatients who had already sought help, so they may not represent people with depression in the wider population, and those who never attend services are absent.

Limitation — Groups: Malaysian Chinese patients cannot stand in for all Chinese people, and Australian patients for all Western populations; each group is internally varied.

Limitation — Interpretation: the study measured reported symptoms and presenting complaints. It cannot establish that one group experiences depression more often, or with different biological mechanisms.

Limitation — Causality: a difference between two cultural groups does not prove that culture alone produced it, because many other factors (socioeconomic circumstances, migration, service context) also differed.

Parker, Cheah and Roy found that far more Malaysian Chinese patients than Australian patients presented with a somatic complaint. Does this prove that depression is more common, or biologically different, in one group?
No. The study measured presenting complaints and responses to a symptom inventory in two clinical samples. It shows a difference in how patients presented and reported their distress, not a difference in how many people were depressed or in the underlying biology. The samples were outpatients who had sought help, so they do not represent whole populations, and a group difference does not establish that culture alone caused it.

Not every cross-cultural difference is a measurement artifact. Populations really can differ in their exposure to the environmental risks and protective factors such as unemployment, poverty, isolation, chronic adversity and social support. Culture and social context help shape which of these a person meets.

  • Economic insecurity and poverty: stable income, housing and food are not equally available everywhere, and chronic financial strain is a documented stressor.
  • Social support: the strength of family, friendship and community networks varies, and support can buffer the effects of stress.
  • Discrimination and chronic social stress: prejudice and exclusion are themselves ongoing stressors that can raise risk.
  • Conflict and displacement: war, migration and forced relocation expose people to loss, uncertainty and trauma.
  • Access to protective resources: healthcare, counselling and community services are unevenly distributed and can reduce or fail to reduce risk.

These are the same pathways — unemployment, poverty, isolation and chronic adversity — through which environments shape risk. Applying them here shows how context, not culture as an abstract force, can genuinely change the probability of depression.

Why Interaction, Not Cultural Determinism

Diathesis–stress reminds us that risk depends on an interaction between stressors and pre-existing vulnerability. A population with more exposure to stressors may show higher average risk, but culture does not determine any individual’s outcome, and two people in the same community can have very different outcomes.

Key idea: differences in measured rates can combine genuine differences in risk with differences in reporting and measurement — often both are present at once.

Culture can influence not only how depression is expressed, but how people understand it and what help they consider appropriate. Beliefs about what causes distress — a medical condition, a spiritual problem, a family or social matter — can shape decisions about:

  • Seeking professional help: whether to approach a doctor, psychologist or psychiatrist at all.
  • Speaking to family or community members: whether distress is handled privately within the family or shared more widely.
  • Considering psychological therapy: whether talking-based treatment is seen as appropriate or useful.
  • Considering medication: attitudes towards medication for mental health vary.
  • Involving family in treatment decisions: some people expect family involvement; others prefer to decide privately.

Maya and Lina

Imagine two students, Maya and Lina, both experiencing persistent low mood, loss of interest, sleep problems and difficulty concentrating.

Maya has grown up where mental health is discussed openly. She visits a doctor, describes her emotional symptoms and is comfortable considering CBT or medication. Lina has grown up where emotional difficulties are usually discussed privately within the family. She initially speaks to her parents and would prefer family involvement when considering professional support.

Neither response is automatically better or worse. These are illustrations of how beliefs and expectations can influence help-seeking, not rigid categories. People from the same cultural background often make different choices, and individual preference matters.

For psychologists, this means assessing depression in a culturally responsive way: considering beliefs, preferences and circumstances without assuming that everyone from a particular background shares them.

Two people with similar symptoms make different treatment decisions: one seeks individual therapy, the other asks to involve family. Using the IB concept of Perspective, explain this difference.
Perspective asks us to consider how experiences and evidence can be understood differently depending on context. The two people may hold different beliefs about the causes of distress and about what kind of help is appropriate — for example, whether depression is primarily an individual medical matter or a family and social one. These beliefs shape what they consider helpful and who should be involved. Perspective does not mean culture determines the choice: people with similar backgrounds differ, and individual preferences are important. A culturally responsive assessment recognizes these beliefs rather than treating one route as superior.

Measurement: Can depression be assessed comparably across cultural settings? A questionnaire or diagnostic interview is only comparable if there is evidence that it works the same way in each group. Translation, interpretation, response styles and construct validity all affect whether a score means the same thing for everyone. Measurement invariance is the evidence researchers look for.

Bias: Could diagnostic assumptions, questionnaire design or reporting practices systematically distort results? If a measure under-recognizes culturally shaped presentations of distress, if stigma suppresses disclosure, or if clinicians apply one cultural template, then results may be skewed in a consistent direction rather than being random.

Perspective: How might beliefs about mental health influence symptom interpretation and help-seeking? Different cultural frameworks lead people to notice, explain and respond to distress differently, which affects what they report and what help they seek. Recognizing Perspective supports culturally responsive assessment rather than a single “correct” view.

Causality: Finding a difference between cultural groups does not establish that culture itself caused that difference. Group differences may reflect measurement, reporting, socioeconomic conditions or other factors that differ alongside culture. Claiming culture as the cause requires evidence that other explanations are ruled out.

A strong evaluation separates distinct claims rather than listing generic limitations.

What the Evidence Supports

Culture can influence symptom presentation. Studies such as Parker, Cheah and Roy (2001) show that patients from different cultural groups may present with and report symptoms differently — for example, nominating somatic rather than emotional complaints.

Culture can influence reporting and help-seeking. Stigma, beliefs about the causes of distress and the acceptability of help all shape whether people disclose symptoms and seek services, which affects recorded and surveyed rates.

Where the Problems Lie

Genuine differences in risk may also exist. Populations can differ in exposure to economic insecurity, social support, discrimination, conflict and access to protective resources. So a difference between groups is not automatically an artifact.

Comparable measurement is hard to establish. Without evidence of measurement invariance, a score difference may reflect the instrument rather than the underlying construct.

Generalizability is limited. Many studies use clinical samples (people who have already sought help), which do not represent whole populations; those who never attend services are missing.

Cultural groups are not uniform. Treating “Western” or “Chinese” people as internally identical hides large variation and risks stereotype.

Attributing differences to culture is difficult. Culture tends to travel alongside socioeconomic status, migration history and service context, so isolating culture as the cause of a group difference is rarely straightforward.

Because these explanations can operate together, and because we usually lack evidence that depression was measured comparably, the numbers alone cannot tell us which population has better mental health.

Evidence Check — interpreting cultural data

What Should I Remember?

  1. Actual prevalence, recorded (diagnosed) prevalence and survey-estimated prevalence are different quantities that can disagree even within one population.
  2. Differences in recorded rates can reflect genuine differences in risk, differences in expression, stigma and help-seeking, or differences in measurement.
  3. Somatic symptoms are common in depression; a person’s presenting complaint is not their complete symptom profile, and physical symptoms can have non-psychiatric causes.
  4. Stigma works through a mechanism: fear of judgment → reluctance to disclose or seek help → fewer recorded diagnoses; anonymous surveys reduce some, but not all, reporting bias.
  5. Using the same questionnaire across cultures does not guarantee comparability. Translation, interpretation, response styles and construct validity all matter, which is why researchers test measurement invariance.
  6. Parker, Gladstone and Chee (2001) is a review of research on depression in Chinese populations. Parker, Cheah and Roy (2001) is a separate empirical comparison of Malaysian Chinese and Australian depressed outpatients, in which 60% versus 13% nominated a somatic presenting complaint.
  7. Population-level differences in risk can be genuine, shaped by exposure to stressors and access to protective factors — culture does not determine any individual’s outcome.
  8. Cultural beliefs influence help-seeking and treatment preferences; assessment should be culturally responsive while respecting individual differences.
  9. Measurement, Bias, Perspective and Causality are the key IB concepts for cultural explanations of depression.

When Does Social-Media Use Become Problematic?

Two students can spend exactly three hours a day on social media and end up in very different places.

So when does social-media use actually become a problem?

This lesson asks three questions: what makes use problematic rather than merely frequent, how researchers measure it, and how strong the resulting evidence is.

Frequent Use, Problematic Use, and Addiction

Frequent use describes how much time someone spends online. For many people that time is neutral or even welcome — contact with friends, content-based interests, support that might otherwise be unavailable.

Problematic use describes patterns of use that cause real difficulty: impaired control, distress, or repeated harm to schoolwork, sleep and relationships. The question is not how long someone is online, but whether they can stop when they choose to and what the use costs them.

Addiction remains a contested label. Classification systems do recognize some behavioral addictions — ICD-11 includes Gaming Disorder, and DSM-5-TR lists Internet Gaming Disorder among the conditions for further study — but there is no equivalent entry for social media. Researchers therefore describe problematic use as a pattern that can be measured and investigated, not as a disorder a clinician can diagnose.

The Six Components

The six components come from an addiction-components framework that Andreassen et al. (2012) applied to social-media behavior. They are not diagnostic criteria for a social-media addiction. Researchers use them to describe patterns worth investigating, and displaying several components does not by itself establish a recognized disorder. The components were adapted from substance and gambling research, so their fit to social media remains an open question rather than an established fact.

Salience

The platform occupies thinking and planning even when the person is offline — planning the next post during a lesson, replaying comments while trying to work. What matters is not enjoying the platform but having it persistently displace other responsibilities.

Tolerance

More time or more stimulating content is needed to produce the same satisfaction: an evening check that once felt satisfying now feels insufficient unless it stretches for hours. Amount can also rise for ordinary reasons, such as a new interest or a project group moving online, so increasing use on its own is not decisive.

Mood modification

Use escalates specifically to change or escape a mood, so scrolling begins whenever an unpleasant feeling appears rather than for a particular activity. Coping with stress through a pleasant activity is common; the concern is that use becomes the main or only strategy.

Relapse

After reducing use, the person returns to the previous level, often despite deciding to stop — deleting the app before exams and reinstalling it within days. Reinstalling an app is not automatically relapse; it becomes relevant when it repeats.

Withdrawal

Unpleasant feelings such as restlessness or irritability appear when the platform cannot be accessed. Brief frustration at disconnection is common, so a mild reaction on its own is not evidence of anything.

Conflict

Use repeatedly interferes with everyday life in meaningful ways and continues anyway: losing sleep, missing deadlines, damaging friendships. Consequences that persist despite awareness are among the strongest markers of problematic use, and a single late night does not count.

Which two components most clearly separate genuinely problematic use from enthusiastic heavy use? Explain your reasoning.
Continued use despite harm (conflict) and repeated failure of attempts to cut down (relapse) best separate problematic from heavy use. Both concern control and consequences rather than the amount of time online: a heavy but controlled user can stop when needed and is not continuing despite meaningful harm. This is why frequency alone is a poor measure.

Research Evidence: Andreassen et al. (2012)

Aim and sample

Andreassen and colleagues set out to develop and evaluate the Bergen Facebook Addiction Scale (BFAS), a short self-report measure of Facebook addiction-related behavior built on the six components. They surveyed 423 college students (227 women; mean age 22.0) at the University of Bergen, Bergen College and the Royal Norwegian Naval Academy in Norway.

Method

The scale began as 18 items, three for each component, each rated from 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often). For every component the researchers retained the single item with the highest corrected item-total correlation, producing a final six-item scale. Participants completed the BFAS alongside measures of Facebook attitudes, online sociability, personality and sleep.

Reliability and validity

The scale showed a good one-factor structure in a confirmatory factor analysis, with internal consistency of .83 (Cronbach’s alpha) and a test-retest correlation of .82 over three weeks for a subsample of 153 students. Scores converged with other measures of Facebook activity, which supports their validity. Higher scores were associated with neuroticism and extraversion and lower scores with conscientiousness, and high scorers reported later bedtimes and rising times.

What the findings do and do not show

What the study establishes is that this behavior can be operationalized and measured with a standardized scale that produces consistent scores related to other variables. What it does not establish is that a high score indicates a clinical disorder. The sample was young Norwegian students, the data were self-reported and cross-sectional, and the items were written for Facebook rather than social media in general. Because the design is cross-sectional, the links with personality and sleep are associations rather than evidence of causation.

Explain how Andreassen et al. checked the reliability of their scale.
They checked reliability in two ways. Internal consistency: the six items produced a Cronbach’s alpha of .83, showing that they largely measured the same construct. Stability over time: when a subsample of 153 students completed the same items three weeks later, the test-retest correlation was .82. Together these show that the scale produces consistent scores, though consistency is not proof that Facebook addiction is a distinct clinical disorder.
A questionnaire produces consistent scores when repeated. Does this mean it can accurately identify someone as having a social-media addiction? Explain.
No. Consistent scores provide evidence of reliability, but reliability does not establish validity. Researchers must also determine whether the questionnaire accurately measures problematic behavior rather than simply frequent use. Furthermore, a questionnaire score alone cannot establish a clinical diagnosis, particularly when social-media addiction is not a formally recognized disorder in the DSM-5-TR or ICD-11.

How Measurement Shapes Prevalence

Measuring a construct such as problematic use is a sequence of decisions: define the construct, write items that stand for it, check that scores are reliable and valid, set a threshold for what counts as problematic, and then count who passes it. The threshold is the decisive step.

If 100 students complete a six-item scale scored 1–5, a strict rule might classify 5 of them as problematic (5%), while a looser rule applied to the same responses might classify 18 (18%).

The students and the questionnaire are identical; the prevalence changes because the cut-off changed. These 5% and 18% figures are hypothetical — they illustrate how a threshold changes the count, and they are not published prevalence estimates.

Andreassen et al. reported psychometric properties rather than a single fixed cut-off, and later researchers have applied different threshold rules, so published prevalence figures also reflect measurement decisions rather than behavior alone.

Two studies use the same questionnaire on the same population but report very different prevalence figures. Explain two measurement reasons why this could happen.
First, the studies may have used different thresholds: one may count people who answer “often” on four or more items, while another counts those who answer “sometimes” on three or more, so different people are classified as problematic from the same responses. Second, they may have operationalized the construct differently: for example, different reference periods (past month rather than past year), different instructions, or slightly different item wording, which can measure subtly different things. Because the construct is a continuum, the cut-off and the operational definition largely determine who is counted.

Prevalence: Why Does It Differ?

Why recorded rates of problematic social-media use change over time — and how to tell real change from measurement change.

When researchers report that problematic social-media use has “increased,” two very different things could have happened. Either more young people genuinely developed problematic patterns, or the recorded number changed because the way researchers measured, counted or sampled changed. Telling these explanations apart is the main skill on this page.

1. Understanding Prevalence

Imagine two schools investigating problematic social-media use. At one school, teachers are concerned that students are increasingly checking their phones during lessons, staying online late at night, and struggling to concentrate. At another school, teachers have noticed similar behavior but believe it affects relatively few students.

How could we find out whether the problem is actually more common at one school than the other?

Reveal Explanation

Researchers could survey students at both schools using the same questionnaire and criteria for problematic use. They would then calculate the proportion of students who meet those criteria. This is called prevalence.

Suppose 400 students complete the questionnaire at one school, and 40 meet the criteria.

Prevalence (%) = (Number meeting the criteria ÷ Number assessed) × 100

40 ÷ 400 × 100 = 10%

This tells us that 10% of the students surveyed met the research criteria for problematic social-media use. It does not mean that 10% have received a clinical diagnosis, or that everyone who spends several hours online has a problem.

There is also an important distinction between how common a problem is and how many people develop it. Prevalence tells us how many people meet the criteria during a specified period. Incidence tells us how many new cases emerge during that period.

A school could have a relatively high prevalence because students who developed difficulties last year are still experiencing them, even if relatively few new cases appear this year.

Importantly, the percentage also depends on how researchers define problematic use. If they change the criteria or the threshold students must meet, the recorded prevalence could change even if students’ actual behavior stays exactly the same.

2. Why Might Prevalence Increase?

Greater access and availability

A teenager who once used social media for an hour on a shared family computer may now have a smartphone available throughout the day. Checking messages during breakfast, between lessons, while studying, and before sleeping becomes much easier.

Greater access creates more opportunities to use social media, including occasions when someone intended to do something else. For some individuals, repeated checking may develop into difficulty controlling use. However, access alone does not establish problematic behavior: someone can use social media frequently without experiencing significant harm or impairment.

Changing social expectations

Social media is not simply entertainment. For many teenagers, it is part of maintaining friendships and participating in school life.

A student who stops checking a class group chat might miss plans made by friends or feel excluded from conversations the next morning. The expectation of remaining available can create pressure to keep checking, even during homework or family time. Over time, this pressure may contribute to patterns of use that become difficult to regulate.

This is a plausible explanation for changing prevalence, not evidence that social expectations necessarily caused an increase.

Platform features

Many platforms are designed to encourage continued engagement through notifications, personalized recommendations, and content that refreshes continuously.

Someone might open an app to reply to a message and then remain there because another video, notification, or conversation repeatedly captures their attention. When there are few natural stopping points, ending a session may require more deliberate self-control.

These features may help explain why some users experience difficulty regulating their behavior. Nevertheless, researchers must distinguish prolonged engagement from problematic use involving impaired control and meaningful negative consequences.

Apparent changes in prevalence

Recorded prevalence can also increase without an equivalent change in people’s behavior. This happens when researchers change how they define, measure, or count problematic use.

Consider two schools surveying similar groups of students. One questionnaire classifies students as problematic users only when they report repeated loss of control and negative consequences. Another also counts frequent checking and spending longer online than intended. The second school could report a higher prevalence even if students’ underlying behavior were similar.

The same problem arises when researchers use different cut-off scores, recruit different types of participants, or ask about different time periods. A survey completed anonymously at home may also produce different responses from one completed in a classroom.

These differences matter because prevalence estimates depend partly on the research methods used to produce them.

Hypothetical figures — for illustration only, not actual research findings.

Imagine one study reports 12% recorded prevalence among teenagers in 2016, and another reports 20% in 2026.

The gap is 8 percentage points (20 − 12 = 8), which is about a 66.7% relative increase on the 2016 figure. Percentage points describe the absolute gap; relative percentage change describes the gap relative to where it started, and reporting one as the other exaggerates or understates the finding.

Two explanations fit these figures: students’ behavior may genuinely have changed, or the later study may have used a broader definition, a lower cut-off or a different sample. Researchers can only choose between them if they know whether both studies used the same operational definition, questionnaire, threshold, age range, sampling method and reference period.

3. Why Measurement Matters

Thresholds change who is counted

Suppose 500 students complete the same questionnaire. Using a strict threshold, researchers classify 25 students as problematic users, producing a prevalence of 5%. Using a more inclusive threshold, 90 students meet the criteria, producing a prevalence of 18%.

Nothing about the students has changed. The difference comes entirely from the researchers’ decision about which responses count as problematic.

Neither threshold is automatically more valid. A very strict threshold might overlook students whose social-media use is genuinely interfering with sleep, relationships, or schoolwork. A very inclusive threshold might classify ordinary frequent users as having a problem.

Researchers therefore need evidence that their chosen threshold identifies the intended psychological construct — that is, that it reliably captures impaired control and meaningful consequences — rather than simply producing a higher or lower percentage.

Sampling changes whose experiences are represented

A school surveys members of its social-media content creation club and finds that 30% meet the criteria for problematic use. It would be misleading to conclude that 30% of all students have the same problem, because the club members were selected partly through their interest in social media. That is a form of selection bias, and it limits the representativeness of the estimate for the wider school population.

A more representative sample might produce a very different estimate.

The issue is not simply how many people are surveyed, but whether the sample adequately represents the population researchers want to describe. Even a very large sample can produce a biased estimate if certain groups are systematically overrepresented.

Comparing findings over time

Suppose researchers survey adolescents in 2018 and again in 2022 using the same questionnaire, threshold, age range, and broadly comparable sampling procedures. A change in recorded prevalence would provide stronger evidence of a genuine population-level change than two studies using different methods.

However, stronger evidence of change is not the same as evidence of its cause. Researchers would still need to investigate which social, technological, or psychological factors contributed.

Using the nine-item Social Media Disorder Scale, researchers reported that problematic social-media use increased from approximately 7% in 2018 to 11% in 2022.

Because the surveys used the same measurement instrument and threshold, the comparison is more informative than comparing studies that define problematic use differently. The findings are consistent with an increase in problematic use among the populations studied.

However, the surveys did not establish why the increase occurred. Greater smartphone access, changing expectations surrounding online communication, platform features, and reporting differences are possible explanations that require further investigation.

The surveys also involved different cross-sectional samples rather than following the same individuals over time. Consistent measurement improves comparability, but it does not eliminate every possible source of bias or establish causation.

Prevalence: What Affects Rates?

Decide what conclusions are justified about problematic social-media use prevalence.

Prevalence can also differ between populations. Researchers might find different rates of problematic social-media use in different countries, age groups or social groups.

A difference in recorded prevalence can reflect genuine differences between the populations, but it can also be influenced by how the health problem was measured.

Differences in Social-Media Use

Populations may differ in access to smartphones and the internet, the platforms they use and how frequently they use them. These differences can affect how much opportunity people have to develop patterns of use that become difficult to control.

For example, if adolescents in one population use highly interactive social-media platforms much more frequently than adolescents in another population, this could contribute to a difference in problematic use. It would not, by itself, prove that greater use caused the difference.

Social and Cultural Differences

Populations can also differ in expectations surrounding online communication, peer behavior, parental monitoring and acceptable technology use.

These differences may influence how people use social media and how willing they are to describe their use as problematic. Culture should therefore be considered as one possible influence rather than treated as a simple cause.

Differences in Measurement

Researchers also need to ask whether the populations were measured in the same way.

Different studies may use different definitions of problematic use, questionnaires, cut-off scores or sampling methods. People from different populations may also interpret questions differently or differ in their willingness to report particular behaviors.

This means that different recorded prevalence rates do not automatically demonstrate an equally large difference in the underlying health problem.

Researchers report that problematic social-media use is more common among teenagers in Population A than Population B. Explain two possible reasons for this difference.
One possibility is a genuine difference in social-media behavior. The populations may differ in access, frequency of use, social expectations or other aspects of how social media is used. Another possibility is measurement. The researchers may have used different definitions, questionnaires, samples or cut-off scores, or participants may have responded differently to the measures. The difference in recorded prevalence therefore needs to be interpreted before concluding that the underlying health problem is genuinely more common to the same extent.

Social Learning & Health Behavior

How observing others and experiencing rewards can influence social-media behavior.

People can learn behaviors by observing others, while the consequences of their own actions can influence whether those behaviors are repeated. These processes may work together, but they explain different aspects of behavior.

How Do People Learn Health Behaviors?

People can learn behaviors by watching family members, friends, peers, and online influencers. For example, a teenager might notice that certain posts attract positive attention and begin copying similar behavior. Social Learning Theory helps explain how this happens.

Bandura’s Social Learning Theory

Albert Bandura argued that people do not need to experience a consequence themselves in order to learn from it. Watching what happens to someone else can be enough to change what we consider worth doing.

Imitation depends on four processes:

1. Attention

Notice the behavior being modeled.

2. Retention

Remember what the model did.

3. Reproduction

Have the ability and opportunity to perform the behavior.

4. Motivation

Have a reason to imitate the behavior.

Observing a behavior does not guarantee imitation: whether someone copies it depends on these processes and on the situation.

The four processes are related but distinct. Noticing a behavior is not the same as remembering it, and being able to reproduce it is not the same as wanting to. A student may understand exactly how a classmate gains attention online and have the skill to copy them, yet see no reason to bother. Another may want to imitate someone but lack the confidence to post. Imitation becomes more likely when the four align, which is why two people can watch the same model and respond differently.

Models and Behavior

A model is a person whose behavior is observed.

People may pay closer attention to models they admire, identify with, or consider successful. However, noticing a model’s behavior does not guarantee that it will be remembered or imitated.

Similarity and status can therefore influence attention and motivation without determining what someone does.

Vicarious Reinforcement

Vicarious reinforcement occurs when someone observes another person’s behavior being rewarded. Seeing that consequence may increase the observer’s motivation to imitate the behavior.

Emma notices that popular students receive enthusiastic comments when they post certain kinds of videos, and she begins making similar videos in the hope of a comparable response. The reward she is responding to happened to someone else; she has not received it herself.

An observed reward can raise motivation without producing imitation. The same visible reward leaves some observers unmoved, because it matters only if it is something that observer wants.

Application — Emma’s Behavior

Scenario: Emma notices that popular students receive many positive comments when they post certain types of content online. She begins posting similar content because she hopes to receive the same attention.

Who is the model?

The popular students whose posts Emma watches.

What behavior is Emma learning?

Explain how vicarious reinforcement may influence Emma.

Apply Your Understanding — Individual Differences

Scenario: Emma copies the posting behavior of a popular student, but her friend watches the same student and does not imitate them.

Explain this difference using two processes from Social Learning Theory.

Model Answer

Both students observed the same behavior, but only Emma imitated it. The difference lies in what each brought to the observation. Emma may have identified with the popular students or valued their approval, which held her attention and gave her a reason to copy them. If she also remembered the style of the posts and had the confidence to reproduce it, all four processes were in place. Her friend may have noticed the same comments but found the model irrelevant, forgotten the details, or had no interest in that kind of attention. Popularity does not transfer automatically: it supplies a reason for some observers and not others.

Aim: to investigate whether children would reproduce aggressive behaviors after observing adults — learning measured in a new situation, with the model absent.

Method: children were randomly assigned to observe adult models behaving aggressively or non-aggressively toward a Bobo doll (with male and female models, and a no-model control group). Each child was later observed playing in a different room containing the doll — with no model present.

Limitations: the main limitation is what the task can tell us. The children were young, the model was an adult in the same room, and the target was an inflatable doll that invites hitting and carries no consequences. Copying a Bobo doll is a long way from developing a social-media habit over months.

Evaluate the Evidence — What Can Bandura Establish?

Bandura’s experiment demonstrated imitation of aggressive behavior after observing an adult model. Why does this not establish that observing influencers causes problematic social-media use?

Model Answer

Bandura showed that children can reproduce specific aggressive acts shortly after watching an adult perform them, in a controlled setting where the model had just been present. That is evidence for short-term imitation of a simple, physically demonstrated behavior. Problematic social-media use is a different outcome: it develops over months, involves complex posting and checking routines, and may be shaped by reinforcement, stress, peer norms and individual differences. The study also says nothing about the content or scale of influencer culture. Observational learning is therefore a plausible contributing mechanism, not a demonstrated cause of problematic use.

Learning and reinforcement are not strictly sequential. A first attempt can be reinforced, and an observed model can affect whether someone persists; the two explanations describe different mechanisms rather than separate stages.

Why Do Some Behaviors Continue?

Psychologists do not only ask why behaviors begin. They also ask why they are repeated. When a consequence increases the likelihood that a behavior will occur again, the behavior has been reinforced.

Reinforcement is defined by that effect rather than by the consequence itself. A like, a comment or a moment of relief is a potential reinforcer; it counts as reinforcement only if the behavior becomes more likely afterwards.

Positive and Negative Reinforcement

Positive reinforcement

Something is added after a behavior, and the behavior becomes more likely to occur again. Positive comments may reinforce posting if receiving them makes someone more likely to post again.

Negative reinforcement

Something unpleasant is removed or reduced after a behavior, and the behavior becomes more likely to occur again. If checking social media temporarily reduces Leo’s anxiety, and that relief makes him more likely to check again when stressed, the behavior may be negatively reinforced.

Negative reinforcement is not punishment. Both forms of reinforcement increase behavior and differ only in whether something is added or removed. Punishment works in the opposite direction, making a behavior less likely.

Intermittent Reinforcement

Social-media rewards do not always occur predictably. Someone might receive little attention after one post but considerable attention after another. If these occasional rewards increase the likelihood of continued posting or checking, intermittent reinforcement may help explain why the behavior persists.

This is a possible mechanism rather than a complete account. It applies where unpredictable rewards can be shown to affect behavior, and many people use social media regularly without their use becoming problematic.

Application — Leo’s Behavior

Scenario: Leo posts a video of his training routine and receives several encouraging comments. He posts similar videos more often over the next few weeks. He also checks social media when he feels stressed; scrolling sometimes calms him, and he increasingly reaches for his phone when facing difficult schoolwork.

Explain how positive reinforcement and negative reinforcement could each contribute to Leo’s behavior. What evidence would show that these consequences are actually reinforcing it?

Model Answer

The encouraging comments may be acting as positive reinforcement: something was added after Leo posted, and if his posting became more frequent afterwards, the comments helped strengthen it. The relief from scrolling may be acting as negative reinforcement in a different way: an unpleasant state is removed, and if Leo checks more often when stressed as a result, that relief is reinforcing the checking.

Neither label is settled by the event itself. Feeling calmer once does not show that relief reinforces anything. The test is whether the consequence increases the behavior afterwards, so researchers would need to track how often Leo posts and checks before and after each consequence and show that the behavior rises when the consequence follows it.

Comparing Observational Learning and Reinforcement

Observational learning

Someone observes another person’s behavior and may imitate it.

Emma copies a popular student’s posting style.

Positive reinforcement

An added consequence makes a behavior more likely.

Encouraging comments increase Leo’s posting.

Negative reinforcement

Removing or reducing an unpleasant condition makes a behavior more likely.

Temporary relief increases Leo’s checking.

Vicarious reinforcement belongs with observation, because the consequence happens to someone else. In direct reinforcement the consequence follows the person’s own behavior: a pleasant consequence is added in positive reinforcement, an unpleasant one is removed in negative reinforcement.

Evaluating the Explanations

Both explanations describe testable mechanisms. Observational learning directs attention to models, to the processes that support imitation and to the consequences people see others receive. Reinforcement directs attention to what follows a behavior and whether it changes how often the behavior occurs.

Neither is sufficient on its own. They cannot show that every observed behavior will be copied, or that frequent social-media use is problematic. The same likes and comments affect people differently: one student changes what they do, another does not care about online attention, and frequency alone does not establish harm.

Stress, peer expectations, individual differences, access to technology and wider circumstances also contribute. Research on observational learning supports the mechanism in general; applying it to social-media use requires evidence from that setting.

Social Comparison, FOMO & Social Media

How social comparison and fear of missing out may influence social-media behavior and psychological well-being.

Why Do People Compare Themselves with Others?

Humans often evaluate themselves by comparing their abilities, opinions and characteristics with other people — at school, at work, among friends and online.

People may compare academic achievement, appearance, popularity, skills or lifestyle.

Example: a student may compare their grades with classmates to understand their own academic performance — the comparison supplies information that no textbook can: where do I stand?

Psychologists study these processes through Social Comparison Theory.

Social Comparison Theory

Leon Festinger proposed that people have a tendency to evaluate themselves by comparing themselves with others. These comparisons can help people understand their abilities, their social position and their progress.

However, comparisons can also influence emotions and self-evaluation — the same comparison that informs can also wound or encourage.

Example: a student comparing their performance with classmates may feel motivated, confident or discouraged depending on how they interpret the comparison. The comparison provides the information; the interpretation decides the feeling.

Different Types of Social Comparison

Upward Social Comparison

Upward comparison occurs when people compare themselves with someone who appears better than them in some way.

Examples: a student comparing grades with a top-performing classmate; someone comparing appearance with an idealised online image.

Possible effects cut both ways. Positive: inspiration, motivation — the better-off other shows what is reachable. Negative: feeling inadequate, lower self-evaluation — the gap feels like a verdict. Important: upward comparison does not always have the same effect on everyone; the same gap inspires one student and deflates another.

Downward Social Comparison

Downward comparison occurs when people compare themselves with someone who appears worse off.

Example: a student compares their academic performance with someone struggling more — and feels steadier about their own position.

Possible effects: increased confidence, reassurance. Measuring yourself against someone facing greater difficulty can stabilise self-evaluation, at least temporarily.

Why Can Social Media Increase Comparison Opportunities?

Social comparison existed long before technology. But social media changes the comparison environment by increasing the number of people we compare ourselves with, the amount of information available about them, and the visibility of popularity and success.

Social media often includes follower counts, likes, comments, views and carefully selected images — each one a ready-made yardstick. Where offline life offered a handful of comparison targets glimpsed occasionally, the feed offers hundreds, measured precisely, updated constantly.

The Highlight Reel Effect

People usually share selected parts of their lives online. They are more likely to post achievements, celebrations, attractive images and exciting experiences — and less likely to post failures, worries and ordinary moments.

This means people may compare their complete everyday life — including boring mornings, bad days and unfiltered faces — with another person’s carefully selected highlights. The comparison is rigged by selection before psychology even enters the picture.

Example: a student sees classmates posting successful achievements online and assumes everyone else is happier or more successful — mistaking a curated sample for a representative one.

Fear of Missing Out (FOMO)

Fear of Missing Out refers to anxiety that other people are experiencing enjoyable or meaningful events without you.

Social media may increase FOMO because users constantly see social events, achievements, experiences and group activities unfolding in real time — a continuous broadcast of everything happening elsewhere.

Example: a student sees pictures of classmates together at an event and worries they are being excluded. The photographs supply the event; FOMO supplies the fear that exclusion is a pattern, not a moment.

How Can Social Comparison Influence Well-being?

The psychological process runs in steps:

↓
Social comparison
↓

↓

↓
Possible behavior change

The comparison itself does not automatically produce negative outcomes. The interpretation matters.

Example: the same post of a classmate’s success produces two reactions. Student A: “I want to improve after seeing someone succeed.” Student B: “I will never be good enough compared with them.” Identical content, opposite interpretations, opposite emotional outcomes — which is why comparison predicts wellbeing only through the meanings people make.

Aim

To investigate the relationship between social media use, social comparison and self-esteem — testing whether comparison explains the link between online behavior and self-evaluation.

Method

Undergraduates completed measures of social media (Facebook) use, social comparison orientation (how much and in which direction they compare on Facebook), and self-esteem. In a follow-up experiment, different participants were randomly assigned to view fictitious Facebook profiles presenting upward or downward comparison targets, then reported their state self-esteem.

Findings

Greater Facebook use was associated with greater social comparison and lower self-esteem — and upward comparison statistically explained (mediated) the link between use and self-esteem. Experimentally, participants shown upward-comparison profiles reported lower state self-esteem than those shown downward-comparison profiles.

Conclusion

Social media may provide more opportunities for comparison processes — especially upward comparison — which in turn relate to lower self-evaluation.

Evaluation

A strength is that the study helps explain a possible psychological mechanism linking online behavior and self-evaluation — not just that use and self-esteem travel together, but how comparison carries the effect — with experimental follow-up supporting the direction. One limitation is that the core association remains correlational: it cannot prove that social media use caused lower self-esteem. Other explanations are possible — for instance, people with lower self-esteem may use social media differently, or third factors may shape both.

The aim was to test experimentally whether browsing Facebook affects young women's mood and body-image concerns, whether the effect differs from an online fashion magazine, and whether appearance-comparison tendency changes (moderates) any effect.

Participants. 112 young women, randomly assigned to three groups: own Facebook account (38), fashion-magazine website (36), appearance-neutral control website (38).

This was a true experiment: 10 minutes of browsing, with state mood and body dissatisfaction measured before and after, plus a state appearance-discrepancy measure (weight/shape items such as "flatter stomach"; face/hair/skin items such as "better complexion") collected with an unobtrusive questionnaire designed to reduce demand characteristics. Trait appearance-comparison tendency was measured one week later.

Findings — stated precisely.

  • Mood: the Facebook group reported significantly more negative mood than the control group — a main effect of condition.
  • Face, hair and skin discrepancies: no overall effect of condition. The effect appeared only in women high in appearance-comparison tendency: for them, Facebook exposure produced greater face/hair/skin discrepancies than the control website. Women low in comparison tendency were unaffected.
  • Weight/shape body dissatisfaction: no Facebook effect at all. (The magazine website, not Facebook, increased weight/shape discrepancy versus control.)
  • So: Facebook exposure worsened mood generally, but its appearance effects depended on the person — exactly the individual-differences point: the same exposure affects different people differently.

Interpretation. Random allocation means the browsing difference caused the short-term differences in mood (and, for high comparers, face/hair/skin concerns) — a short-term causal state effect. But the study cannot prove that social media causes depression, eating disorders, long-term body-image problems or problematic use: exposure lasted 10 minutes, participants were women only, and outcomes were temporary states, not clinical conditions.

Application. A student spends ten minutes viewing appearance-focused content, then feels dissatisfied with their own face and skin. This connects directly: brief exposure can shift state appearance concerns — especially in someone who habitually compares — without showing anything about lasting disorder.

(a) Because participants were randomly allocated to browsing conditions and measured immediately after, researchers can claim Facebook browsing caused a short-term increase in negative mood in this sample, and caused short-term face/hair/skin concerns specifically in women high in comparison tendency. Randomisation rules out pre-existing differences as the explanation. (b) They cannot claim Facebook causes depression, eating disorders or lasting body-image problems — exposure was 10 minutes and outcomes were temporary states. Nor can they generalize to men or to long-term use — the sample was women only and duration was untested. This is why experiments license short-term causal conclusions but never long-term clinical ones.

Evaluating Social Comparison Explanations

Strength: Explains a psychological mechanism

Social Comparison Theory explains why social media may influence wellbeing — through the process of comparing oneself with others — rather than stopping at “social media is harmful.” A mechanism can be tested, traced and interrupted; a slogan cannot.

Strength: Recognizes individual differences

The same online content may affect people differently depending on self-esteem, personality, goals and interpretation — Student A’s motivation and Student B’s despair from the identical post. The theory expects variation instead of predicting uniform harm.

Limitation: Correlation does not prove causation

People with lower wellbeing may also use social media differently — seeking reassurance, comparing more, scrolling longer. The relationship may work in multiple directions, and cross-sectional findings cannot separate them.

Limitation: Other factors influence wellbeing

Social comparison is only one possible influence among many — stress, relationships, environment and coping strategies all shape wellbeing alongside it. Any account that stops at comparison is incomplete by design.

Stress & Health

How the body responds to stress, why people react differently, and how prolonged stress can affect health.

Stress is a normal part of life. Before an important exam, you might notice your heart beating faster, feel more alert, or find it difficult to concentrate.

However, people do not always respond to the same situation in the same way. One student may see an exam as a challenge, while another sees it as a threat.

Psychologists study how we interpret stressful situations, how our bodies respond, and why prolonged stress can sometimes contribute to health problems.

Stressor → Cognitive Appraisal → Stress Response

How someone interprets a situation can influence their stress response. The same event does not affect everyone in exactly the same way.

Understanding Stress

Stressor

A situation or demand that may cause stress.

Examples include an important exam, an argument with a friend, or financial difficulties.

Cognitive appraisal

How someone interprets a situation and evaluates their ability to cope with it.

Stress response

The psychological and physical changes associated with experiencing stress. These may include worry, a faster heartbeat, muscle tension, or difficulty concentrating.

Example: Two Students, One Exam

Two students receive the same disappointing exam result.

One thinks, “I need to change how I study next time.”

The other thinks, “I'm going to fail everything.”

The first student sees the result as a problem they can address. The second sees it as a threat to their future.

Their different interpretations may help explain why they experience different levels of stress.

Cognitive Appraisal

Lazarus and Folkman (1984) emphasized the role of cognitive appraisal in stress. People evaluate what a situation means for them and whether they have the resources to cope with it.

This helps explain why the same event can produce different stress responses.

Apply Your Understanding

Two students receive the same disappointing exam result. One feels motivated to improve, while the other feels overwhelmed.

Explain how cognitive appraisal might account for their different responses.

Although both students experienced the same event, they interpreted it differently. The first student may see the result as a challenge they can overcome, while the second may see it as evidence that they cannot succeed. These different appraisals can influence how much stress they experience.

How the Body Responds: The Sympathetic Nervous System and HPA Axis

The Sympathetic Nervous System — The Fast Response

When we perceive a threat, the sympathetic nervous system helps prepare the body for action. This is often called the fight-or-flight response.

The heart beats faster, breathing may increase, and the body becomes more alert. These changes help us respond quickly to a challenge.

For example, just before giving a presentation, you might notice your heart racing and your hands becoming sweaty. This is part of the body's immediate stress response.

Once the challenge has passed, the parasympathetic nervous system helps the body return toward its resting state.

The HPA Axis — The Hormonal Response

The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis is another system involved in responding to stress. It works through a series of hormonal signals that lead to the release of cortisol.

Figure: The HPA axis. CRH stimulates the pituitary gland to release ACTH, which stimulates the adrenal cortex to release cortisol. Cortisol also helps regulate the system through negative feedback.

What Does Cortisol Do?

Cortisol helps the body respond to demands by making energy available and influencing several body systems.

Cortisol is not inherently harmful. However, prolonged stress can affect how stress-related biological systems are regulated, potentially contributing to difficulties with sleep, mood, and immune functioning.

Cortisol also helps regulate the HPA axis through negative feedback, reducing further hormonal signaling when appropriate.

Apply Your Understanding

A student notices that their heart is racing immediately before an exam. Another student experiences ongoing stress for several weeks. Explain how the sympathetic nervous system and HPA axis may contribute to their stress responses.

The sympathetic nervous system produces rapid changes such as an increased heart rate and heightened alertness. The HPA axis involves hormonal signals that lead to cortisol release. Both systems can respond to stress, but they operate through different mechanisms and timescales. Prolonged stress may involve changes in how these systems are regulated.

Acute and Chronic Stress

Acute Stress

Acute stress is a short-term response to a particular situation.

For example, you may feel nervous before speaking in front of your class. Your heart rate increases, but the response usually decreases after the presentation.

Acute stress is not always harmful. In some situations, increased alertness can help performance.

Chronic Stress

Chronic stress occurs when stressful demands continue over a long period.

Examples include ongoing family conflict, financial difficulties, or persistent academic pressure.

Prolonged stress can affect sleep, concentration, mood, and physical health. However, the effects vary between people and depend partly on coping strategies and social support.

Key Study: Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome

Hans Selye proposed that the body responds to prolonged stress through three stages, known as the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS).

Alarm

The body detects a stressor and activates its stress-response systems.

Resistance

If the stressor continues, the body attempts to adapt while maintaining a physiological response.

Exhaustion

Under prolonged or severe stress, the body's capacity to maintain its response may become compromised.

What Selye's Animal Research Found

Selye's findings came from his animal experiments. Rats were exposed to various prolonged stressors, and their bodies showed consistent physiological changes.

The changes included enlarged adrenal glands, reduced weight of immune-related organs such as the thymus and spleen, and ulcers in the stomach.

Example

Imagine a student facing several weeks of demanding examinations. They may initially feel highly alert, continue coping with the workload for some time, and eventually experience fatigue or difficulty concentrating.

Selye's findings suggest that the body responds physiologically to sustained demands. His model can help explain why prolonged stress may place strain on the body. However, the stages were developed largely from animal research and should not be used to diagnose a person's stress level from symptoms such as tiredness or catching a cold.

Apply Your Understanding

A student studies intensely for several weeks and then becomes ill. A friend claims this proves the student has reached the exhaustion stage of GAS. Explain why this conclusion is not justified.

GAS suggests that prolonged stress can place demands on the body's ability to adapt. However, becoming ill does not prove that someone has entered a particular stage. Illness can have many causes, and the three stages are part of a theoretical model rather than a diagnostic test.

Evaluate the Research

Selye's model was developed largely through animal experiments. Explain one limitation of using this research to understand human stress and one reason it remains useful.

Animal research cannot fully explain human stress because people's interpretations, relationships, and coping strategies influence their responses. However, the research helped identify physiological patterns associated with prolonged exposure to stressors and contributed to later stress research.

Key Study: Cohen et al. (1993) — Stress and Catching a Cold

Aim

To investigate whether psychological stress was associated with greater susceptibility to developing a common cold following exposure to a respiratory virus.

Method

Researchers assessed participants' psychological stress and exposed them to respiratory viruses under controlled conditions.

The study included 394 healthy volunteers. Participants completed questionnaires measuring three aspects of stress: negative life events, perceived stress, and negative affect.

They were then intentionally exposed to a common cold virus through nasal drops and monitored in quarantine for a clinically verified cold.

Findings

Participants reporting higher levels of psychological stress were more likely to develop a clinical cold after exposure.

Higher scores on each of the three stress measures were associated with greater risk.

Conclusion

The findings support an association between psychological stress and susceptibility to respiratory illness.

Stress levels were measured rather than assigned, so the study does not prove that stress directly caused the illness.

Apply the Findings

Two students are exposed to the same cold virus. One has experienced considerable psychological stress, while the other reports little stress. Based on Cohen et al. (1993), what might we predict, and what can we not conclude?

Cohen et al. found that higher psychological stress was associated with a greater likelihood of developing a clinical cold following controlled viral exposure. We might therefore predict a greater risk of illness for the more stressed student. However, we cannot predict either student's outcome with certainty or conclude that stress alone caused any difference.

Evaluate the Evidence

Why does Cohen et al. (1993) provide stronger evidence about the relationship between stress and illness than simply asking people whether they often become sick when stressed? Why can it still not establish that stress itself caused the illness?

The researchers measured psychological stress and used controlled viral exposure rather than relying only on participants' memories of becoming ill. This helped reduce some alternative explanations. However, stress levels were measured rather than experimentally assigned, so other differences between participants could still have contributed to the results.

Individual Differences

People differ in how they interpret stressful situations and the resources they have available. Previous experiences, social support, coping strategies, and individual characteristics can all influence their responses.

Stress and Behavior

Stress can influence what people do. Some people seek support, exercise, or plan how to solve a problem. Others may avoid difficult tasks or turn to activities that provide temporary relief.

These behaviors may help in the short term without necessarily solving the original problem.

The Possible Stress Cycle

One relieved check is harmless. A loop is different. Follow a realistic scenario:

  1. A student experiences academic stress.
  2. They use social media to escape unpleasant emotions.
  3. They spend longer online than intended.
  4. They sleep less.
  5. They feel more tired and stressed the next day.
  6. They use social media again to cope.

This represents a possible cycle — each turn leaves the student slightly worse placed to cope, which makes the next escape slightly more likely. Crucially, the relationship is not one-directional: stress may contribute to problematic use (escape → habit), and problematic use may contribute to stress (lost sleep, missed work, comparison, conflict → more stress). Either direction alone is an oversimplification; researchers must test both, and must avoid claiming simple causation from correlation between stress and use.

Stress is therefore a contributing factor, not a deterministic cause: most stressed students never develop problematic use, and social media sometimes provides genuine support and connection.

Why the Same Stressor Hits Differently: Appraisal

Lazarus and Folkman's transactional model splits interpretation into two judgments:

  • Primary appraisal — interpreting the event: “Is this situation threatening?”
  • Secondary appraisal — evaluating coping resources: “Do I have the resources and support to manage this?”

Appraisal is not always a fully conscious, deliberate judgment: rapid evaluations can trigger physiological responses, and cognitive and biological processes interact rather than running in a strict order.

For example, two students face the same workload. Student A thinks, “I have a plan and support,” judging the threat as manageable and their resources as adequate. Student B thinks, “I cannot handle this,” judging the threat as overwhelming and their resources as absent. The same workload produces different levels of stress and different coping choices.

This connects to individual differences — coping skills, personality, previous experience, and support networks all feed into appraisal — and to the cognitive perspective, in which thought mediates between the environment and the response.

Correlation and Causation

Stress research often finds that two things occur together: people who report more stress are also more likely to report poorer health. This is a correlation. It does not, by itself, show that stress caused the difference.

Reverse causation is possible: health problems can create stress. A third factor, such as poverty or lack of sleep, could influence both stress and health. Researchers therefore use controlled designs, longitudinal studies, and measures of biological mechanisms to narrow the range of explanations.

Cohen et al. (1993) reduced one alternative explanation by giving everyone the same viral exposure. Stress itself was still measured rather than assigned, so the study supports an association, not proof of direct causation.

Strengths and Limitations of Evidence

Stress research has several strengths.

  • Studies such as Cohen et al. (1993) use controlled exposure, which rules out the possibility that stressed people simply encounter more viruses.
  • Research on the sympathetic nervous system and HPA axis identifies specific biological mechanisms, making the link between stress and health testable.
  • Findings have practical applications: reappraisal, social support, sleep routines, and stress-management strategies can change the conditions that maintain stress.

There are also important limitations.

  • Stress is difficult to measure. Researchers may count stressful events, ask people to rate perceived stress, or measure biological responses such as cortisol. These measures do not always agree, and self-reports depend on memory and honesty.
  • Individual differences matter. Two people facing the same event may differ in coping skills, personality, social support, and previous experience. An explanation that ignores these differences will overpredict stress.
  • Much research is correlational. Brown and Harris (1978) found that severe life events and ongoing difficulties were more common before the onset of depression among the women they studied. This suggests that stressful experiences may contribute to depression risk, particularly alongside vulnerability factors. However, the observational findings do not prove that stress independently caused depression.

These strengths and limitations are complementary: mechanisms explain how stress could affect health, while cautious interpretation keeps the claims proportionate to the evidence.

A researcher finds that teenagers who report more problematic social-media use also report higher levels of anxiety.

Biological Treatment of Depression

How antidepressants work, what research shows about their effectiveness, and why treatment outcomes vary.

Antidepressants are one biological treatment for major depressive disorder (MDD). They affect chemical signaling in the brain and can reduce depressive symptoms, although people respond differently.

In this lesson, you will learn how SSRIs work and examine two major studies investigating antidepressant effectiveness.

What Is Biological Treatment?

Biological treatments aim to reduce symptoms by influencing biological processes. For depression, medication is one of the most widely used biological approaches.

Antidepressants are commonly prescribed for people with MDD. Different types of antidepressants affect neurotransmitter systems in different ways.

One frequently prescribed group is selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Examples include fluoxetine and sertraline.

How SSRIs Work

Serotonin is a neurotransmitter involved in communication between nerve cells.

When one neuron releases serotonin, some of it binds to receptors on another neuron. Normally, much of the released serotonin is taken back into the sending neuron through a process called reuptake.

SSRIs block the serotonin transporter, reducing this reuptake. This increases the availability of serotonin outside the sending neuron.

How treatment works

Serotonin released → Serotonin transporter blocked → Reduced reuptake → More serotonin remains available outside the sending neuron

However, the improvement in depressive symptoms usually develops over several weeks. The immediate change in serotonin signaling does not fully explain the longer-term therapeutic effects.

Treatment Mechanisms and Causes

When a treatment improves a condition, it tells us something about how symptoms can be changed. It does not necessarily tell us what originally caused the condition.

For example, SSRIs alter serotonin signaling and can reduce depressive symptoms. However, this does not establish that depression is caused by a simple shortage of serotonin.

Researchers therefore distinguish between a treatment's mechanism of action and the causes of the disorder being treated.

Research Evidence — Kirsch et al. (2008)

Aim

To investigate how much additional improvement antidepressants provide compared with placebo, and whether the initial severity of depression influences the difference.

Method

Kirsch and colleagues analyzed data from 35 clinical trials submitted to the US Food and Drug Administration. These trials compared four antidepressants with placebo in adults with depression.

The analysis included 5,133 patients. Improvement was measured using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS).

Findings

Across the trials, participants taking antidepressants improved more than those taking placebo. However, the average difference between the two groups was approximately 1.8 points on the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale.

The researchers also found that the difference between antidepressants and placebo tended to be larger among participants with more severe depression at the beginning of treatment.

In their analysis, the average difference reached the three-point threshold they used for clinical importance only at very high initial severity levels.

Interpretation

The study suggests that antidepressants provided an additional benefit over placebo, but the average difference was relatively small in the trials examined. It also raised questions about how initial depression severity may influence the size of that difference.

Limitation

The findings came from a particular collection of trials involving four medications. They should not be treated as proof that antidepressants are ineffective for everyone with less severe depression.

The clinical-importance threshold used by the researchers is also open to debate.

Research Evidence — Cipriani et al. (2018)

Aim

To compare the effectiveness and acceptability of 21 antidepressants used to treat adults with major depressive disorder.

Method

Researchers conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis of 522 double-blind randomized controlled trials involving more than 116,000 participants.

They compared antidepressants with placebo and, where data allowed, with one another.

Findings

All 21 antidepressants were more effective than placebo on average at producing a treatment response during acute treatment.

However, the medications differed in effectiveness and acceptability, meaning some performed better than others and some were more likely to be discontinued.

Interpretation

The findings support antidepressants as an effective treatment option for many adults with MDD.

However, the results describe average outcomes across groups. They do not establish which medication will work best for a particular individual.

Response, the outcome the study measured, refers to at least a 50% reduction in depressive symptoms. It is not the same as full remission, so the results do not show that every patient recovered.

Limitation

Most included trials examined relatively short-term treatment. The findings therefore provide less information about long-term effectiveness, relapse prevention, and outcomes in people whose circumstances differ from those of trial participants.

The studies measured effectiveness differently, so their findings are not necessarily contradictory.

Treatment Effectiveness

Antidepressants can reduce depressive symptoms, but they do not work equally well for everyone.

Treatment outcomes may depend on several factors, including the severity of symptoms, side effects, whether someone continues taking the medication, and the support available to them.

For example, someone experiencing depression alongside ongoing financial difficulties may benefit from medication while still needing support to address the circumstances contributing to their distress.

  • Symptom severity: the level and pattern of depressive symptoms may influence treatment decisions and outcomes.
  • Individual response: people can respond differently to the same medication.
  • Side effects: unwanted effects may make treatment difficult to continue.
  • Ongoing circumstances: stress, sleep, relationships, and other health problems may influence recovery.
  • Additional support: psychological therapy, follow-up appointments, and social support may help address difficulties that medication alone does not resolve.

Strengths and Limitations

Biological treatment has several strengths.

  • Antidepressants have demonstrated benefits over placebo on average in randomized controlled trials.
  • Medication may reduce symptoms sufficiently for some people to participate more fully in everyday activities and psychological treatment.
  • Different medications provide treatment options when an initial approach is ineffective or poorly tolerated.

There are also important limitations.

  • Not everyone experiences a meaningful improvement.
  • Side effects can affect well-being and treatment continuation.
  • Medication does not necessarily address ongoing psychological or environmental difficulties.
  • Symptoms may return after treatment ends, so follow-up and longer-term planning can be important.

  7. Treatment outcomes depend on symptoms, individual response, side effects, ongoing circumstances, and support. Medication may leave psychological and environmental difficulties unresolved.
  8. Biological and psychological treatments target different processes. Some people benefit from combining approaches.

Psychological Treatment of Depression

How CBT helps people change unhelpful patterns of thinking and behavior, and what research tells us about its effectiveness.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is a psychological treatment that helps people identify unhelpful patterns of thinking and change behaviors that may contribute to depression.

Example

For example, someone who believes they are a failure might withdraw from friends and stop doing activities they previously enjoyed. CBT can help them examine that belief and gradually return to meaningful activities.

In this lesson, we will examine how CBT works and what research tells us about its effectiveness.

1. What Psychological Treatment Means

A psychological treatment aims to reduce symptoms by changing psychological processes — for example, interpretations, patterns of thinking, behaviors and coping strategies. CBT is the most widely researched example, and this lesson focuses on it.

Depression involves interacting biological, psychological and environmental factors. Therapy is one entry point into that system: it targets the thoughts and behaviors that keep symptoms going, alongside whatever else contributed to them.

2. CBT: The Cognitive Component

CBT helps people recognize automatic thoughts that may contribute to negative emotions and behavior. During cognitive restructuring, a therapist helps the person examine the evidence for these thoughts and consider whether a more balanced interpretation is possible.

Example — Maya

For example, after receiving criticism, Maya might think, 'I always get everything wrong.' Rather than simply telling Maya to think positively, the therapist could help her examine whether this conclusion is supported by the evidence.

Maya might recognize that she has made some mistakes but has also succeeded in many situations. A more balanced thought could be, 'I made a mistake this time, but that does not mean I always fail.'

Cognitive restructuring therefore involves examining thoughts rather than simply replacing negative thoughts with positive ones. The goal is a more accurate interpretation, not forced optimism.

Maya sends a message to a friend but receives no reply. She immediately thinks, 'Nobody likes me.' Explain how a CBT therapist could help Maya examine this thought.
The therapist could help Maya identify the automatic negative thought and examine the evidence supporting it. They might ask whether there are other explanations for the unanswered message, such as the friend being busy. Maya could then develop a more balanced interpretation rather than automatically assuming rejection.
3. CBT: The Behavioral Component

Depression can make everyday activities feel difficult or unrewarding. A person may stop meeting friends, exercising or participating in activities they previously enjoyed.

Behavioral activation helps the person gradually increase meaningful and potentially rewarding activities, even when motivation is low.

Example — Maya

For example, Maya might begin by taking a short walk, attending one social activity or returning to a hobby for a manageable period.

The aim is not to force happiness. It is to reduce avoidance and withdrawal while creating more opportunities for positive experiences, achievement and social connection.

Maya has stopped meeting her friends because she rarely feels motivated to leave home. Explain how behavioral activation could help her.
A therapist could help Maya plan small, manageable social activities rather than waiting until she feels motivated. Gradually increasing these activities may reduce withdrawal and provide opportunities for social connection and rewarding experiences, potentially improving her mood over time.
4. The Depression Maintenance Cycle

Cognitive and behavioral patterns can feed each other and keep depression going. Consider Maya, whose symptoms have been building for several months:

Negative automatic thought — 'I am a failure.'
↓
Low mood — Maya feels hopeless.
↓
Withdrawal — Maya avoids friends and activities.
↓
Fewer rewarding experiences — Maya becomes more isolated.
↓

Each step feeds the next: thoughts influence mood, mood shapes behavior, behavior narrows experience, and the narrowed experience confirms the original thought.

CBT attempts to interrupt this cycle in two ways. Cognitive restructuring helps Maya question negative interpretations, while behavioral activation helps her reduce withdrawal and return to meaningful activities.

This is an illustrative maintenance cycle, not an inevitable sequence for everyone with depression.

Cognitive restructuring could help Maya question the belief that she is a failure, reducing the influence of negative automatic thoughts. 6. Research Evidence — Cuijpers et al. (2013)

Aim

The aim was to investigate the effectiveness of psychological treatments — CBT in particular — for adult depression, updating the evidence across the whole field rather than trusting any single trial.

Method

The researchers conducted a meta-analysis: systematic searches of PubMed, PsycINFO, Embase and the Cochrane library identified 115 studies examining CBT compared with control conditions (waiting list, usual care, placebo), other psychotherapies and pharmacotherapy.

Findings

  • Across the studies, people receiving CBT generally showed greater improvement in depressive symptoms than people in the comparison groups.
  • The researchers found evidence that the original results probably overestimated CBT's effectiveness. After accounting for publication bias, the estimated benefit became smaller.
  • Higher-quality studies tended to report smaller benefits than lower-quality studies.
  • Therapy combined with medication produced better average outcomes than medication alone.

What do these findings mean?

The findings support CBT as an effective treatment for depression, but they also show why research quality matters. If studies with positive results are more likely to be published, the overall evidence may make a treatment appear more effective than it really is.

Interpretation

The authors' conclusion was that CBT is an effective treatment for adult depression, although the effects may have been overestimated. CBT carries the greatest weight of evidence partly because it is the most studied treatment, while other approaches approach its efficacy.

Limitations

Publication bias inflates the headline figure, because studies finding little or no effect are less likely to be published. Conclusions also inherit the flaws of the included studies: the better the study, the smaller the estimated benefit. Averages describe studies in aggregate and cannot predict how one particular person will respond.

Cuijpers et al. (2013) found evidence that CBT can reduce depressive symptoms. However, the researchers also considered publication bias. Explain why publication bias matters when evaluating CBT's effectiveness.
Studies reporting positive results may be more likely to be published than studies finding little or no effect. If a meta-analysis mainly includes published positive findings, it may overestimate the average effectiveness of CBT. Publication bias therefore makes it important to interpret the overall effect cautiously.
7. Research Evidence — Elkin et al. (1989): Comparing Treatments for Depression

Aim

The study was part of the NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program (TDCRP) and aimed to compare several treatments for depression directly within one controlled study.

Participants and treatment conditions

Depressed adults were randomly assigned to one of four conditions over sixteen weeks: cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), interpersonal psychotherapy, imipramine with clinical management, or placebo with clinical management.

Method

A randomized comparison of two talking therapies, antidepressant medication and pill placebo, with clinical management provided across medication and placebo conditions. This design allowed each active treatment to be evaluated against both another therapy and supportive contact without medication.

Findings

  • All three active treatments produced substantial improvement, with no clear overall winner at the end of treatment.
  • Antidepressant medication showed an added advantage for the most severely impaired patients.
  • Even the placebo group improved substantially, showing that expectation and regular supportive contact themselves produce genuine relief.

Interpretation

The results align with Cuijpers' no-difference finding. Treatment choices depend partly on each patient's presentation — severity can guide whether medication plays the larger role — and any comparison between medication and therapy must account for the genuine effects of supportive contact and expectation.

Limitations

A sixteen-week trial cannot establish which treatment better protects against relapse afterward; confirming durability requires longer follow-ups. Findings about severely impaired patients come from subgroup results, which are less certain and need replication. A controlled efficacy trial also does not automatically transfer to every real-world clinical setting.

Elkin et al. (1989) compared different treatments for depression. Explain why the results should not be used to claim that one treatment is always best for every person with MDD.
Treatment outcomes can differ depending on factors such as symptom severity, individual circumstances and the outcome being measured. The study compared particular treatments under specific research conditions, so its findings do not establish that one treatment will be most effective for every person with depression.
8. What Do These Studies Tell Us Together?

Cuijpers et al. (2013) examined evidence across many studies of CBT, while Elkin et al. (1989) directly compared different treatments under controlled research conditions.

Together, they help us understand both whether CBT can reduce depressive symptoms and how its effectiveness compares with other approaches.

However, average research findings do not determine which treatment will work best for a particular individual.

How does a meta-analysis such as Cuijpers et al. (2013) provide different evidence from a randomized treatment comparison such as Elkin et al. (1989)?
A meta-analysis combines results from multiple studies to estimate an overall pattern of effectiveness, although the result depends on the quality and selection of those studies. A randomized treatment comparison directly tests specific interventions under controlled conditions, helping researchers compare outcomes and make stronger causal inferences about treatment effects within that study.
9. Strengths and Limitations of CBT

CBT provides practical strategies for addressing thinking and behavior. Its effectiveness has been investigated in controlled studies and meta-analyses, and the skills learned during treatment may continue to be useful afterward.

However, CBT does not work equally well for everyone. Participation can require effort, time and access to a trained therapist. Depression severity, individual circumstances and other factors may influence outcomes, and research averages cannot predict an individual patient's response.

10. The Therapeutic Relationship

The therapeutic relationship refers to the working relationship between a therapist and client. It includes trust, agreement about treatment goals and cooperation in completing therapeutic activities.

A supportive relationship may help someone feel comfortable discussing difficult experiences and participating in treatment.

However, a positive therapeutic relationship does not guarantee improvement, and it can be difficult to separate its effects from other aspects of treatment. Outcomes involve multiple interacting factors — technique, relationship, expectations and support — so no single ingredient can be credited alone.

Preventing & Treating Problematic Social-Media Use

Why social-media habits can become difficult to control, how psychologists try to change them, and what research tells us about effective interventions.

Social media can help people stay connected, find information, and enjoy entertainment. However, some people find it difficult to control their use, even when it begins to interfere with sleep, schoolwork, relationships, or well-being.

Psychologists investigate why these patterns develop and how they can be changed. In this lesson, we will examine negative reinforcement, social learning, prevention strategies, psychological treatment, and research into reducing social-media use.

1. What Is Problematic Social-Media Use?

Problematic social-media use occurs when a person's use becomes difficult to control and causes meaningful difficulties in everyday life.

Spending several hours online does not automatically mean someone has a problem. A student might use social media extensively to communicate with friends or organize school activities without experiencing significant difficulties.

The concern is greater when someone repeatedly uses social media longer than intended, struggles to stop, or continues despite problems with sleep, schoolwork, or relationships.

Example — Leo

For example, Leo often checks social media when he feels stressed about school. He plans to spend only five minutes scrolling but frequently continues for much longer. As a result, he sometimes goes to bed late and struggles to concentrate the following morning.

Problematic use is a description of a behavior pattern, not automatically a formal psychiatric diagnosis.

Two students spend three hours a day on social media. One uses it to communicate with friends and complete school projects. The other repeatedly misses deadlines and loses sleep because they struggle to stop scrolling. Explain why screen time alone is insufficient to determine whether their use is problematic.
The amount of time spent online does not tell us whether someone has difficulty controlling their behavior or experiences negative consequences. The second student shows possible signs of problematic use because scrolling interferes with schoolwork and sleep. The first student's use may be extensive without causing comparable difficulties.
2. Why Checking Can Continue: Negative Reinforcement

Negative reinforcement occurs when removing or reducing something unpleasant makes a behavior more likely to happen again.

Example — Leo

For example, Leo feels anxious about an upcoming examination. He checks social media to distract himself, and his anxiety temporarily decreases.

However, the relief may be temporary. Leo could lose study time, stay awake later, and experience more stress the following day.

This is why the definition matters. Relief alone does not establish negative reinforcement; checking has been negatively reinforced only when the consequence increases the likelihood of checking again in similar circumstances.

Leo feels less anxious after scrolling through social media. Explain what additional evidence we would need before concluding that his checking behavior has been negatively reinforced.
We would need evidence that the temporary reduction in anxiety makes Leo more likely to check social media again when he experiences similar stress. Relief alone does not establish reinforcement unless the behavior becomes more likely in the future.
3. Learning From Others: Social Learning Theory

Social Learning Theory suggests that people can learn behaviors by observing others.

Example — Leo

For example, Leo notices that his friends frequently check their phones during breaks. He also sees influencers receiving attention, likes, and positive comments for their posts.

Observing these rewards may make certain social-media behaviors appear attractive or worthwhile. Leo may be more likely to imitate people he identifies with, especially when their behavior seems rewarding.

Vicarious reinforcement occurs when observing someone else receive a reward increases an observer's likelihood of performing a similar behavior. This differs from negative reinforcement: in vicarious reinforcement the observer sees someone else rewarded, whereas in negative reinforcement the person's own relief strengthens their behavior.

However, observing a behavior does not guarantee imitation. Attention, motivation, and individual circumstances also matter.

Leo notices that his friends frequently post photographs and receive positive comments. He begins posting more often himself. Explain how Social Learning Theory could account for this change.
Leo observes his friends receiving positive social feedback for posting. This may act as vicarious reinforcement, making posting appear rewarding. If Leo identifies with his friends and expects similar attention, he may be motivated to imitate their behavior.
4. Preventing Problematic Use

Prevention aims to reduce the likelihood that problematic social-media habits will develop or become more serious.

For example, students might turn off unnecessary notifications, establish phone-free study periods, or avoid keeping their phones beside their beds.

These strategies can reduce opportunities for distraction and make it easier to control when social media is used.

However, prevention is not simply about reducing screen time. It also involves developing healthier ways to manage stress and maintain everyday responsibilities.

Reducing triggers — turn off unnecessary notifications during study periods.
Changing routines — keep the phone away from the bed at night.
Developing alternative coping strategies — take a short walk, speak with someone, or break a difficult task into manageable steps instead of automatically scrolling when stressed.
Leo checks his phone whenever he receives a notification while studying. Suggest one prevention strategy and explain which part of his behavior it is intended to change.
Leo could disable social-media notifications during study sessions. This would reduce external cues that trigger checking, making interruptions less frequent and helping him maintain concentration.
5. Psychological Treatment and Behavioral Strategies

Treatment focuses on helping someone change an existing pattern of social-media use that is causing difficulties.

A psychological approach may help the person identify what triggers their checking, what they gain from it, and how the behavior affects their daily life.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) may be used to address unhelpful beliefs and develop alternative behaviors. For example, someone who believes they must immediately respond to every message might learn to question that belief and practice delaying responses.

Behavioral strategies may also involve gradually changing routines, reducing cues, and developing healthier responses to stress.

CBT is not a guaranteed treatment for problematic social-media use, and difficult-to-control use is not the same as substance addiction. Research into these approaches is still developing.

Leo believes that if he does not check his messages immediately, his friends will become upset with him. Explain how a CBT-based approach could help him.
A therapist could help Leo examine the evidence for his belief and consider whether delayed replies actually cause the consequences he fears. Leo could then practice waiting before checking messages and observe what happens. This may help him develop a more balanced belief and reduce unnecessary checking.
6. Research Evidence — Hunt et al. (2018)

Aim

To investigate whether limiting social-media use would reduce loneliness and depressive symptoms among university students.

Method

The researchers recruited 143 undergraduate students and randomly assigned them to different conditions.

One group was instructed to limit Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat use to approximately 10 minutes per platform per day for three weeks. The comparison group continued using social media as usual.

Participants' social-media use was monitored; iPhone users verified actual use with battery-usage screenshots rather than relying only on self-report, and researchers measured psychological outcomes including loneliness and depression.

Findings

  • Students instructed to limit their social-media use showed greater reductions in loneliness and depressive symptoms than students in the comparison group.
  • The benefits were particularly noticeable among participants who initially reported higher depressive symptoms.
  • Some other measures, including anxiety and fear of missing out, improved across both groups rather than showing the same clear difference between conditions.

Interpretation

The findings suggest that limiting social-media use may improve some aspects of psychological well-being over a short period.

Because participants were randomly assigned to conditions, the study provides stronger evidence for a causal effect of the intervention than a simple correlation between screen time and mental health.

Limitations

  • The participants were university students, so the findings may not apply equally to younger adolescents or other populations.
  • The intervention lasted only three weeks, so the study does not establish whether the benefits continue over longer periods.
  • The study also does not demonstrate that limiting social media is an effective treatment for every person experiencing problematic use.
A school wants to introduce a program limiting students' social-media use because Hunt et al. (2018) found benefits for university students. Explain one reason the findings support this proposal and one reason the school should be cautious.

Hunt et al. found that limiting social-media use reduced loneliness and depressive symptoms relative to the comparison group, providing evidence that limits may benefit well-being.

However, the study involved university students and lasted only three weeks. The findings may not generalize to younger students or demonstrate long-term benefits.

7. Evaluating Prevention and Treatment

An intervention should be judged by three questions:

Does the intervention improve well-being? Reducing screen time may be useful, but it does not necessarily mean that problematic behavior or psychological well-being has improved. Researchers should also examine outcomes such as sleep, concentration, emotional well-being, and the person's ability to control their use.

Can we establish causation? If people who use social media more frequently also report greater anxiety, this does not establish that social media caused the anxiety. Experimental studies can provide stronger causal evidence when researchers manipulate an intervention and compare outcomes between randomly assigned groups.

Will the intervention work for everyone? People use social media for different reasons. For some, it provides valuable friendships, information, or emotional support. Interventions therefore need to consider individual circumstances rather than assuming that reducing social-media use will benefit everyone equally.

Psychologists use evidence to determine whether treatments and prevention strategies work. However, an effective treatment does not necessarily prove what originally caused a health problem.

For example, SSRIs can reduce depressive symptoms in some patients, but this does not prove that their depression was caused by low serotonin.

In this lesson, we will compare biological and psychological treatments for depression, examine interventions for problematic social-media use, and evaluate how psychologists determine whether an intervention is effective.

1. How Do Psychologists Evaluate Interventions?

Psychologists evaluate interventions by asking what they are intended to change, why they might work, and whether research demonstrates meaningful improvement.

An intervention may target a biological process, a pattern of thinking, or a behavior that contributes to a health problem.

However, an intervention being theoretically plausible does not establish that it is effective. Researchers must investigate whether people receiving the intervention improve and whether the improvement can reasonably be attributed to the intervention.

1. What is the intervention targeting? SSRIs affect serotonin signaling. CBT targets unhelpful patterns of thinking and behavior. Social-media interventions may target checking habits, environmental cues, or coping responses.
2. Why might it work? Psychological theories suggest how changing these processes could influence symptoms or behavior.
3. What evidence shows that it works? Researchers compare outcomes, consider alternative explanations, and examine whether improvements are meaningful and sustained.

One distinction runs through this whole lesson: a treatment can improve symptoms without proving what originally caused the disorder. CBT, for example, can reduce depressive symptoms without establishing that negative thinking was the sole cause of depression.

2. Biological vs. Psychological Treatment of MDD

Biological treatment: SSRIs. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) reduce serotonin reuptake, changing serotonin signaling in the brain. SSRIs can reduce depressive symptoms for some patients. However, treatment effectiveness varies, and side effects, patient preferences, symptom severity, and other clinical considerations may influence treatment decisions. Large reviews of antidepressant trials, including Cipriani et al. (2018) and Kirsch et al. (2008), indicate that antidepressants outperformed placebo on average, with the clearest advantages among more severely depressed patients; individual responses vary considerably.

Psychological treatment: CBT. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) helps people identify and change unhelpful thinking patterns and behaviors. Cognitive restructuring focuses on examining negative interpretations, while behavioral activation encourages engagement in meaningful or rewarding activities. CBT can reduce depressive symptoms, but effectiveness varies across individuals and circumstances.

Research Evidence — Elkin et al. (1989)

Aim. To test whether psychotherapy and medication differed in effectiveness for patients with major depressive disorder, using a design that included a placebo group.

Method. Approximately 250 patients with major depressive disorder were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: CBT; interpersonal psychotherapy; imipramine (a medication) with clinical management; or a placebo with clinical management. Findings. Patients in all three active treatment conditions improved substantially. None of the active treatments was clearly superior overall; some advantages for medication appeared mainly on some measures among more severely depressed patients.

Research Evidence — Cuijpers et al. (2013)

Aim. To summarize the evidence on how effective psychological therapies are for adults with depression.

Method. A meta-analysis of 115 controlled studies comparing psychotherapy with control conditions and, in part, with other treatments.

Findings. Psychological therapies reduced depressive symptoms relative to control conditions on average, and no therapy type showed a consistent overall advantage across the studies. In the comparisons included in the analysis, combined psychotherapy and medication outperformed medication alone.

Interpretation. The findings support the effectiveness of psychological treatment for depression and suggest that combined treatment can be worth considering. Findings should be read as averages across studies, not as guarantees for any individual patient.

Limitations. Study quality varied across the 115 studies, control conditions differed between studies, and the analysis depended on the studies available at the time. Follow-up evidence on longer-term outcomes was limited.

Combined medication and CBT showed advantages in particular meta-analyses, but this does not mean every patient must receive both. Treatment decisions depend on clinical circumstances, symptom profiles, the available evidence, patient preferences, availability, and professional guidance.

SSRIs and CBT act on different processes, and both may reduce depressive symptoms. However, changing a process through treatment does not establish that this process originally caused the disorder. Depression may involve interacting biological, cognitive, and environmental influences.

  • Practicing alternative responses to anxiety or stress.
  • Gradually changing established habits.

Not every person who uses social media frequently requires treatment. Frequent use can coexist with control, sleep, and responsibilities; the concern needs supporting evidence of loss of control or meaningful interference with everyday life.

Research Evidence — Hunt et al. (2018)

Aim. To test whether limiting social-media use would change psychological outcomes over a short period.

Method. 143 undergraduate students were randomly assigned to limit Facebook, Instagram, and Snapchat to approximately 10 minutes per platform per day for three weeks, or to continue using social media as usual. iPhone users verified actual use with battery-usage screenshots.

Findings. The limited group showed greater reductions in loneliness and depressive symptoms than the comparison group, particularly among participants who initially reported higher depressive symptoms. Some other measures, including anxiety and fear of missing out, improved in both groups.

Interpretation. The randomized design provides good support for short-term effects of limiting use in this sample. A three-week experiment with students is not evidence that a clinical treatment for problematic social-media use has been established, and the study was not a trial of CBT. Improvement in the comparison group also suggests that being monitored contributed to some changes.

Limitations. The participants were university students and the intervention lasted only three weeks, so longer-term effects in other populations remain unknown.

A school introduces phone-free study periods for all students. A psychologist separately works with a student whose social-media use repeatedly interferes with sleep and schoolwork. The school-wide approach aims to reduce distractions and help prevent problematic habits across the student population. 4. Comparing the Research Evidence

Strong evaluation answers use the same four questions, whatever the intervention:

1. Did the intervention produce meaningful improvement? A reduction in symptoms or problematic behavior may indicate improvement, but researchers must consider what was measured and whether the change matters in everyday life.

3. How reliable and generalizable is the evidence? Researchers should consider sample characteristics, study duration, consistency across studies, and whether findings apply to other populations.

4. Are there important limitations or disadvantages? An intervention may improve one outcome while producing side effects, requiring considerable time or resources, or providing limited long-term benefits.

HL Extension: Culture & Health

HL extension: how culture shapes the understanding, diagnosis, prevalence and treatment of health problems — building on Culture & Depression.

Why do recorded rates of depression differ across cultures?

Recorded rates of depression can vary between countries and cultural groups. However, these differences do not necessarily mean that people in one culture experience more depression than people in another.

Some differences may reflect genuine variation in risk factors, such as stressful life experiences or social conditions. Others may arise because people express symptoms differently, interpret questionnaire questions differently, or have unequal access to diagnosis and treatment.

Psychologists therefore need to distinguish between differences in underlying prevalence and differences in how depression is measured, reported, and diagnosed.

This distinction raises an important question: when researchers identify cultural differences in depression rates, how much of the difference reflects psychological reality, and how much reflects the methods used to investigate it?

1. Why Do Depression Rates Differ Across Cultures?

When psychologists compare cultures, four separate ideas are often folded into a single "rate". Each needs its own check:

  • Underlying prevalence — how many people in a population genuinely experience the disorder, whether or not anyone counts them.
  • Recorded prevalence — how many a country’s system counts, which depends on what is measured, who reports, and who reaches services.
  • Symptom expression — what distress looks like when it is described, since cultures differ in whether it is voiced emotionally or bodily.
  • Diagnosis and help-seeking — who reaches a diagnostician and which labels are applied once they do.

Every recorded figure sits at the end of this chain, and each earlier link can shift it without any change in underlying prevalence — which is why the research in the next section matters.

Four HL concepts run through this lesson and you will apply each one: Measurement (can the tools see the same experience in every culture?), Bias (imposed categories and clinicians' own cultural defaults), Causality (a recorded difference does not by itself show what caused it), and Perspective (the same figure can look like a rate of suffering or a rate of visibility, depending on whose viewpoint the measures encode).

Researchers compare depression rates between two countries. Country A reports higher rates of depression. Country B reports much lower rates. However: people in Country B are less likely to discuss emotional difficulties; distress is often described through physical symptoms; mental-health treatment is less commonly accessed.

Give one explanation for why Country A may have higher recorded rates, and one competing explanation for the same figure.
Country A may face genuinely higher risk factors (economic strain, conflict, weaker support). But equally, it may simply record more of what exists: accepted discussion, accessible clinics and mood-based diagnostic tools tuned to emotional expression all raise counts without any extra suffering. Either route — or both together — fits the same figure, so the rate alone cannot choose between them.
2. Genuine Differences in Risk and Experience

Culture can influence the experiences that affect health: social support (who helps in a crisis), economic conditions (security vs precarity), stress exposure (discrimination, conflict, instability), coping strategies (what counts as an acceptable response) and beliefs about health (what illness means and what should be done).

Example. A population experiencing greater economic insecurity or discrimination faces more of the stressors associated with depression — unpredictable income, unsafe housing, hostile treatment — through no biological difference at all. If recorded depression is higher there, part of the gap may be genuinely earned by harsher conditions.

Important: culture can influence risk, but it does not determine outcomes. Populations are not destinies; individuals within any culture vary enormously. Genuine differences explain risk gradients, never individual fates.

3. Differences in Symptom Expression

No. Different cultures have different ways of communicating distress. Some individuals describe depression mainly through emotional experiences:

"I feel hopeless and empty."

Others describe the same underlying distress mainly through physical symptoms: fatigue, headaches, body pain, sleep problems:

"I cannot sleep, I am exhausted and my body hurts."

The psychological distress may be similar while its communication differs — idioms of distress, not different diseases. A culture can also carry culturally specific ways of expressing symptoms: the distress is real, but its language differs. This matters because clinicians and researchers can only work with what is expressed, reported and measured: if the tools listen for "hopeless" and the patient says "exhausted", the encounter misfires through no fault of either party. The next sections show this misfire in real research.

Explain how culture could influence diagnosis in this comparison.
Through three gates: expression (Country B's bodily idioms vs the criteria's emotional expectations), interpretation (translated items like "hopelessness" shifting meaning; clinicians reading unfamiliar behavior through home-culture norms — imposed etic bias), and recognition (only Country A's help-seekers ever reach a diagnostician). Diagnosis is judgment applied to culture-shaped material, so equal suffering can produce unequal labels.
4. Measurement, Diagnosis, and Reporting

Recorded rates may differ because psychologists do not always measure the same psychological experience in the same way across cultures:

A lower recorded prevalence does not necessarily mean lower actual suffering — it may mean suffering that the tools could not see.
  • Translation differences. A word for an emotional experience may lack an exact equivalent. Example: "hopelessness" may carry moral, spiritual or somatic overtones in another language — so the translated item measures something adjacent, not identical, to the original.
  • Different meanings and interpretations. The same question or behavior can be read differently: "loss of interest" may mean anhedonia to one respondent and laziness-shame to another; the same behavior can signify different things — identical answers, different meanings.
  • Reporting styles, stigma and willingness to report. Some cultures encourage open discussion of emotional problems; others discourage reporting psychological distress because of stigma. The same suffering produces different scores.
  • Clinician interpretation. Diagnosis requires interpretation of behavior against norms — and clinicians may read symptoms through their own cultural expectations without realizing it, over-detecting disorder in unfamiliar behavior or missing it in unfamiliar idioms (imposed-etic bias: a framework built where depression says "I feel low" under-counts where it says "my body hurts").
Standardized criteria improve consistency — but consistency of procedure is not validity of meaning. Both must be checked.

Recorded prevalence also depends on a chain, and every link is cultural: whether people recognize symptoms as illness, whether they report them, whether they seek help, and whether healthcare is available to record them.

Country A

Mental-health discussion is accepted, clinics are accessible, people seek support → distress gets counted.

Country B

Mental illness carries stigma, emotional disclosure is discouraged, treatment is rarely accessed → distress stays home and unrecorded.

Country A may record higher rates even if actual distress is similar — its system sees more of what exists, while Country B's system sees less. Prevalence figures describe recording systems as much as populations, which is why such figures must not be read at face value.

Explain why lower recorded rates in Country B may not mean less depression exists.
Country B's low figure may be a recording artifact: stigma suppresses disclosure, somatic idioms ("exhausted, aching") slip past mood-tuned questionnaires (Kleinman; Parker), and rarely accessed services generate few diagnoses. Lower recorded prevalence here is fully compatible with equal — or greater — actual suffering rendered invisible by expression, reporting and access barriers.
5. Research Evidence — Kleinman (1982) and Parker et al. (2001)

Research Evidence — Kleinman (1982): When Depression Speaks Somatically

Aim. To investigate how depression was expressed among Chinese patients — testing whether Western diagnostic expectations fit a non-Western clinical reality.

Method. A combined clinical and anthropological field study: 100 patients diagnosed with neurasthenia (shenjing shuairuo — "nervous weakness") at the psychiatry outpatient clinic of Hunan Medical College, Changsha, studied with psychiatric clinical interviews and re-assessed against American DSM-III criteria. (Kleinman, A. (1982). Culture, Medicine and Psychiatry, 6(2), 117–190.)

Findings. Patients presented tiredness, pain and bodily discomfort rather than sadness as the leading complaint — yet 87 of the 100 met DSM-III criteria for Major Depressive Disorder (93% for any depression); anxiety disorders were also frequent, and 44 had previously undiagnosed chronic pain syndromes. When treated with antidepressants, most improved symptomatically — supporting the view that the same underlying condition wore different cultural clothing.

Interpretation. Depression may be expressed differently across cultures: neurasthenia functioned as a bioculturally patterned illness experience — "a special form of somatization" — related to depression but communicated through culturally sanctioned bodily idioms, in a setting where psychological labels carried heavy stigma.

Limitations. This does not mean emotional symptoms are absent in Chinese patients — it means culture shapes how distress is experienced, how it is communicated, and how clinicians recognize it. Re-diagnosing with DSM-III assumes the Western category fits (Kleinman's own "category fallacy" warning — imposing Euro-American diagnoses may misclassify), and one specialist clinic in 1980 cannot represent all of China, let alone "non-Western culture". Evidence for an expression difference, not for a fixed cultural essence.

Research Evidence — Parker et al. (2001): Testing Expression Differences Directly

Purpose. To examine whether depressive symptoms genuinely differ across cultural contexts — comparing Chinese and Australian patients rather than assuming either pattern.

Findings. Malaysian Chinese depressed outpatients (matched with Australian Caucasian patients for age and sex) presented somatic complaints in 60% of cases versus 13% — yet affirmed depressive cognitions ("I feel depressed") markedly less, pulling their total inventory scores down. Parker described this as "corporization" (somatosensory amplification): distress registered and reported through the body. The companion review (Parker, Gladstone & Chee, 2001) added that "the Chinese" are heterogeneous, that stigma suppresses emotional disclosure, and that Western influence was already shifting expression by the 1980s.

Importance. If diagnostic systems developed in one cultural context assume depression is reported as persistent low mood, they will under-detect it where distress is corporized — a validity question, not just a prevalence footnote. (Both Parker studies are covered in depth alongside the culture-and-depression material; their verdict is used here through the measurement lens: same disorder possibly counted differently.)

6. Evaluating Cultural Explanations

Strength — reduces Western bias. Studying culture stops psychologists assuming one explanation or measurement system fits everywhere. Kleinman's category-fallacy warning and the imposed-etic concept are bias detectors built from this literature — without them, Parker-style under-detection would pass as genuine low prevalence.

Strength — improves diagnosis. Understanding cultural expression helps clinicians recognize distress accurately: expecting somatic idioms where they are common, checking translations, allowing family-involved assessment where decisions are collective. Better recognition means fairer treatment access.

Limitation — risk of stereotyping. Culture influences individuals; it does not clone them. "Chinese patients somatise" flattens Hunan outpatients, Hong Kong students, diaspora teenagers and rural elders into one script — and Parker explicitly stressed heterogeneity. Assuming everyone from one group expresses distress identically is stereotyping wearing a cultural costume.

Limitation — difficult to separate explanations. Any recorded difference arrives as a bundle: genuine risk differences, measurement differences, reporting differences, access differences — and no single study unpacks all four. Kleinman plus Parker plus HBSC-style surveys triangulate, but triangulation narrows uncertainty without ending it. Cultural conclusions must therefore stay provisional and multi-causal, never single-story.

The bigger picture — culture across the whole pathway. Culture does not replace biological, cognitive or environmental explanations — it runs through them. A complete psychological explanation tracks culture at every station:

Risk factors → experiences → symptom expression → measurement → diagnosis → treatment access → recorded prevalence.

Genes set vulnerabilities; environments supply stressors; cultures shape which stressors occur, how distress is voiced, whether tools hear it, who gets labeled, what help fits, and therefore what the statistics finally say. The biopsychosocial model was always incomplete without its fourth partner: biopsychosocial-cultural explanation, interacting all the way down.

Why would it be misleading to say "Country A has a higher depression rate than Country B, therefore people in Country A are genuinely more depressed"?
The recorded rate is the end product of the whole chain: differences in expression, willingness to report, stigma, access to services, diagnostic practice, measurement tools and cultural interpretation can all shift the recorded figure without any genuine difference in underlying depression. A rate describes what was recorded, not necessarily what exists.
Evaluate whether prevalence differences prove that depression differs between these cultures.
No. Differences may include genuine risk variation — but they may equally result from measurement validity (tools tuned to one idiom), reporting (stigma, disclosure norms) and diagnostic/access differences. Kleinman and Parker show expression alone can move counts dramatically. Proof would require comparable valid measurement, equivalent reporting conditions and matched access — conditions cross-national figures almost never meet. Differences invite interpretation; they never self-interpret.
Identify two pieces of additional evidence that would help distinguish a genuine difference in underlying prevalence from a difference in how depression is recorded.
Useful evidence would include: measurement validity checked in both countries — translations tested and expression assessed directly through interviews, rather than relying on mood-tuned questionnaires that Kleinman and Parker show can misfire; and comparable recording conditions — data on service access, help-seeking and reported stigma in each country, so the same definition of "a recorded case" is actually applied. Evidence of this kind speaks to Measurement, Bias and Reporting before any conclusion about underlying prevalence is drawn.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Culture may shape how distress is experienced, expressed and reported.
  2. Standardized diagnosis improves consistency, but interpretation can still carry cultural bias, including imposed etic bias.
  3. Recorded prevalence differences do not automatically reflect genuine differences in disorder rates.
  4. Treatment engagement may depend on cultural fit; culture shapes tendencies, not every individual.
  5. HL chain: experience → expression → measurement → diagnosis → treatment → recorded prevalence.
  6. Kleinman (1982, 100 Hunan patients, 87 meeting MDD criteria) and Parker et al. (2001, 60% vs 13% somatic presentation) show expression differences with diagnostic consequences — interpret counts through validity, not at face value.

HL Extension: Motivation & Health Behavior Change

HL extension: why knowing a behavior is unhealthy does not automatically change it — connecting motivation to problematic social-media use, prevention and treatment.

Knowing what is healthy does not automatically produce healthy behavior. Whether knowledge turns into action depends on motivation — why the change matters to the person — and on how motivation interacts with psychological processes, existing habits, social support and environmental opportunities.

Motivation and behavior are connected but not identical. A motivated person can still fail when circumstances block the behavior, and knowledge that is not backed by any motivation rarely produces action on its own. The sections below build up these influences, starting with why change is so difficult.

1. The Central Question: Why Is Changing Health Behavior Difficult?

Many people understand what would improve their health — and still struggle: someone wants to exercise more but rarely does; someone wants to improve sleep but keeps unhealthy night-time habits; someone wants to reduce social-media use but repeatedly checks their phone.

The psychological question is why knowing what is beneficial does not always lead to behavior change. Knowledge addresses what someone believes. Behavior answers to motivation, confidence, habit, support and barriers — which is why "I know it is unhealthy" does not equal "I am motivated and able to change it", especially when the unhealthy option is instantly rewarding and the healthy option pays off later.

2. Wanting to Change Is Not the Same as Changing

A person may hold a strong intention — "I want to exercise more" — yet the behavior still answers to motivation (why bother?), confidence (can I?), habits (what do I automatically do?), social support (who helps?) and environmental barriers (what blocks me?).

Example. Maria knows exercise would improve her health. But she works long hours, feels exhausted after work, and has limited access to exercise facilities. Her intention exists; her evenings, energy and location block it. Blaming Maria's "willpower" misdiagnoses a structural problem as a character flaw. Intention is necessary; it is not sufficient.

3. Self-Determination Theory: What Sustains Motivation?

Deci and Ryan proposed that motivation differs in quality, not just amount. People are more likely to maintain behaviors when they feel internally motivated rather than controlled by external pressure. Three psychological needs support sustained, self-directed change:

Autonomy — feeling that the behavior is chosen. People try harder and persist longer when the change feels self-authored. Example: "I exercise because being active matters to me" outlasts "My doctor told me I have to exercise" — the same behavior, different ownership.

Competence — feeling capable of success. Achievable progress builds the belief that effort works. Example: a beginner runner who gradually increases distance collects evidence of capability with every extra kilometer; an all-or-nothing marathon plan collects only failure.

Relatedness — feeling supported and connected. Change is easier inside connection than under ridicule. Example: joining an exercise group works partly through timetables — but mostly because people who feel supported persist through bad weeks instead of quitting alone.

Continuing example: a student reducing late-night use persists longer when they help choose the strategy (autonomy), bank achievable wins like two phone-free evenings a week (competence), and are supported rather than mocked (relatedness).

Application scenario. Emma knows she should sleep more. However: she stays awake scrolling social media; she says she only wants to change because her parents tell her to; she does not personally value reducing her phone use; scrolling is her main way of relaxing after school.

Explain how Self-Determination Theory could improve Emma's motivation.
By feeding each need. Autonomy: help Emma find her reasons — rested mornings, better football, chosen limits rather than imposed ones — converting "parents say so" into "I want this because…". Competence: shrink the goal to achievable wins (one phone-free half-hour, then a full pre-sleep hour) so success builds capability beliefs instead of all-or-nothing failure. Relatedness: recruit support — a friend with the same plan, parents who encourage rather than police — so change happens inside connection. Ng et al. show need satisfaction is associated with healthier behavior; Emma needs all three needs supported, not one lectured.
4. Different Types of Motivation: Intrinsic and Extrinsic

Intrinsic motivation — the behavior is performed because it is personally meaningful or enjoyable. Example: James exercises because he enjoys running and feels better afterwards. The reward lives inside the activity.

Extrinsic motivation — the behavior is performed because of external rewards or pressure. Example: James exercises only because his doctor warned him about health risks. The reward (or threat) lives outside the activity.

Self-Determination Theory further distinguishes forms of extrinsic motivation. When the reason comes purely from outside pressure — deadlines, warnings, other people's expectations — the motivation is controlled. When the person has taken an external reason on board as their own — seeing the doctor's warning as something that genuinely matters to them — the motivation is autonomous. Extrinsic motivation is therefore not the opposite of self-directed motivation: a behavior can start externally and become owned, and the autonomous forms are the ones associated with persistence.

Extrinsic motivation can create change — deadlines, doctors and disappointed parents move millions. But maintenance is fragile when motivation stays controlled: when the pressure lifts, the behavior often lifts with it, because it was never anchored in personal value. Neither type is always better: external structure can start a change later internalised — the runner who begins for the doctor and continues for the joy — while even strong internal drive still fails against impossible barriers.

Why might Emma's motivation be weak?
Her motivation is mainly externally controlled: the drive comes from parental pressure, not from her own values — she does not personally prize less scrolling or more sleep. Controlled motivation tends to produce compliance while supervision continues and is less likely to persist once it lapses, because the behavior was never anchored in what she wants. Weak here means badly sourced, not small in amount: Emma may try hard for a week and still stop, since effort without ownership rarely survives friction.

Link back to Leo. He wants to reduce social-media use. Compare his reasons:

  • Weak motivation: "I should stop because my parents say I spend too much time online." Controlled, borrowed — it may last only as long as the supervision does.
  • Stronger motivation: "I want to reduce my phone use because I want better sleep and more time for activities I value." Owned, connected to sleep, football and mornings that currently hurt.

The reasons behind behavior change influence whether change is maintained: Leo's parental-pressure version suggests compliance that fades when supervision lifts; his sleep-and-values version is more likely to persist through friction — provided competence (achievable steps, not cold-turkey perfection) and relatedness (friends who respect the limits) come with it.

6. Behavior-Change Strategies: Designing Motivation, Not Just Feeling It

Motivation is not only a personal characteristic — psychologists design interventions that support it:

Goal setting. Specific goals beat vague intentions. Weak: "I will use my phone less." Strong: "I will avoid social media during the hour before sleeping." Specificity tells Tuesday-night Leo exactly what to do; vagueness tells him nothing.

Self-monitoring. Recording behavior increases awareness. Example: Leo logs when he checks his phone, what triggered it, and how he felt afterwards — exposing, say, the maths-homework → 11 p.m. → worse-mood chain his memory smoothed over. Tracked loops become choosable loops, and monitoring itself often produces at least some short-term improvement.

Implementation intentions. Specific if–then plans preload decisions past the moment of temptation: "If X happens, I will do Y." Example: "If I feel stressed and want to scroll, I will first take a five-minute walk." The decision is made in advance, so it does not have to be renegotiated each night against the pull of craving.

Social support. Support from others increases persistence. Example: a friend exercising together adds accountability and company — relatedness doing motivational work that willpower alone often cannot. The effect runs both ways: limits announced to mockery are usually abandoned within days, which is what a relatedness failure looks like.

Identify one behavior-change strategy that could help Emma, and explain why.
Any one, with mechanism attached. Self-monitoring: a three-night log (trigger → scroll duration → morning feeling) makes her invisible loop visible and choosable. Implementation intentions: "If I finish homework and reach for the phone, I will read for ten minutes first" preloads the decision past craving. Substitution: replacing scrolling with another genuine relaxant (music, stretching) fills the gap quitting leaves. Specific goals: "no social media after 10:30 p.m." beats "use it less" by telling tonight's Emma exactly what to do. Credit requires the why (awareness, preloading, gap-filling, specificity), not just the tip.
7. Key Study: Ng et al. (2012) — Does Motivation Quality Predict Health?

Aim. To investigate whether Self-Determination Theory variables — autonomy support, need satisfaction, autonomous motivation — are associated with health-related behaviors and outcomes.

Method. A meta-analysis: a statistical synthesis that pools the results of many separate studies into one overall estimate. Ng et al. combined 184 independent datasets from studies applying SDT in healthcare and health-promotion contexts (physical activity, tobacco abstinence, medication adherence, diet, diabetes control and more). (Ng, Ntoumanis, Thøgersen-Ntoumani, Deci, Ryan, Duda & Williams (2012). Perspectives on Psychological Science, 7(4), 325–340.)

Findings. Greater autonomous motivation and need satisfaction were associated with healthier behaviors and improved outcomes — including activity levels, abstinence, adherence, glycaemic control and cholesterol — with mostly small-to-moderate effects. Path modeling showed autonomy-supportive climates predicting all three needs, the needs predicting autonomous motivation (with competence the strongest driver), and competence directly predicting health outcomes. The quality of motivation mattered alongside its amount.

Interpretation. The findings are correlational: they show that autonomous motivation and better health reliably travel together, not that autonomous motivation produces lasting behavior change. Included trials and longitudinal studies strengthen that causal reading, but the direction of the association stays open — healthier patients may feel more competent, and motivated patients may elicit more supportive clinicians.

  • Strength — scale and synthesis: 184 datasets means the evidence rests on patterns across behaviors, clinics and countries — not one laboratory's runners. Path models test the whole theory (climate → needs → motivation → outcomes), not isolated links.
  • Limitation — mostly correlational: the process-model tests are largely cross-sectional associations, which cannot establish causality. Useful for HL evaluation of causality and generalizability: solid evidence that motivation quality and health go together — not proof that one causes the other.
8. Evaluating Motivation-Based Explanations

Strength — explains why knowledge is not enough. Maria knows exercise helps; Leo knows scrolling costs sleep. SDT explains the gap: knowledge without autonomy (chosen reasons), competence (achievable steps) and relatedness (support) is information without engine — which is why lectures rarely move behavior and redesigned motivation sometimes does.

Strength — practical applications. Understanding motivation lets psychologists design change that is more likely to last: choice-preserving limits (autonomy), graded wins (competence), supported plans (relatedness), specific goals, monitoring and if–then rules. Each strategy is a need made concrete — though effectiveness depends on fit, and is strongest when motivation theory is combined with attention to barriers.

Limitation — individual differences. People are moved by different things: one runner lives for the group, another for solitude; one teenager internalises parental rules, another rebels against them. A relatedness-heavy group program may transform the first and alienate the second — no single motivation recipe works equally for everyone, so interventions must be matched, not mass-prescribed.

Limitation — motivation is not sufficient. A highly motivated person still hits walls: financial limits (no gym money), no time (Maria's hours), stressful environments (exam-season scrolling), no access (no facilities, no alternatives). Behavior change requires motivation and opportunity. Maria's barrier is not her heart; it is her timetable.

9. Motivation Does Not Work Alone

Health behaviors emerge from interacting influences:

  • biological factors (energy, sleep debt, arousal),
  • cognitive processes (beliefs, appraisals, automatic thoughts),
  • social influences (norms, modelled behavior, support or mockery),
  • environmental conditions (cues, access, time, money),
  • motivation (quality and amount).

Example: someone may genuinely want to exercise — autonomously, competently, supportedly — yet lack time, money or access. Motivation influences behavior; it does not determine outcomes alone. Single-cause stories fail everywhere, including for the will.

Which approach is better — the ban or the collaborative plan?
There is no universally correct answer, which is the point. An imposed ban may produce faster short-term compliance, while a collaborative plan may better support autonomy, competence and relatedness and therefore more sustained change — but effectiveness depends on the person, the context, the severity of the problem and how well each approach is implemented. A strong answer compares the options using motivation theory and then qualifies the conclusion.
Evaluate whether motivation alone can explain Emma's health behavior change.
Not alone. Even perfectly internalised motivation meets habit (nightly scrolling automaticity), environment (phone beside the bed), stress (school pressure demanding relief) and resources (whether alternatives genuinely relax her) — any of which can block change with motivation intact. That is a barriers explanation, not a willpower verdict. Ng et al.'s evidence is associational, not causal, and bidirectional effects are plausible. Behavior change depends on motivation working together with ability, social support and environmental opportunity — Emma's answer has to consider all of them.

What Should I Remember?

  1. Motivation gives behavior direction: intrinsic reasons come from within, extrinsic reasons from outside — neither is always better.
  2. Self-Determination Theory: autonomy, competence and relatedness support sustained change.
  3. Immediate rewards and habits compete with motivation, so knowledge alone rarely changes behavior.
  4. Imposed versus collaborative interventions differ in compliance speed and sustainability; context decides.
  5. Motivation is one part of a wider system of influences on health behavior.
  6. Ng et al. (2012, 184 datasets): need satisfaction and autonomous motivation track healthier behavior and outcomes — associational overall, so causality stays qualified, with competence the strongest driver.
  7. Quality of motivation (owned, capable, supported) predicts maintenance better than amount of pressure — design interventions for needs, barriers and context together.

HL Extension: Technology & Health

HL extension: technology as a possible influence on health problems and as a tool for prevention and treatment — neither inherently harmful nor inherently beneficial.

Technology can influence psychological health in both helpful and harmful ways. Its effects may depend on what people do online, why they use technology, their individual circumstances, and the environment in which they use it.

For example, social media might provide emotional support for one person while disrupting another person's sleep. Psychologists therefore investigate specific patterns of technology use rather than assuming that all screen time has the same effect.

The question running through this lesson: how, why and for whom does technology influence health?

A. How Can Technology Influence Behavior?

A device or platform does not determine psychological outcomes. Psychologists examine how technology is used, why it is used, the consequences of use and the differences between users — the use, not the device, carries the psychology.

1. When Does Use Become Problematic?

Psychologists distinguish heavy but healthy use from use that has become a problem. A device used for hours each day is not automatically a problem; the markers below decide:

Problematic use means patterns where technology use becomes difficult to control and begins interfering with important areas of life. Three markers are considered together:

  • Loss of control — the person cannot stop as intended.
  • Negative consequences — sleep, school or work, relationships or mood are suffering.
  • Inability to reduce — the person wants to cut down but cannot.

Hours are the weakest of the three signals: a student who maintains friendships, receives support and stops in time to sleep normally does not qualify on the markers — a classmate who checks compulsively to escape stress and loses sleep does.

2. Reinforcement: Why Platforms Grab the Evening

Platform features reinforce use through standard learning mechanisms:

Positive reinforcement. A behavior is followed by an added rewarding outcome, making the behavior more likely in future. Example: a student receives many likes on a post and becomes more likely to post again — approval now carries an expectation of approval.

Negative reinforcement. A behavior is followed by the removal or reduction of something unpleasant, also making it more likely in future. Example: checking social media when anxious reduces the anxiety for a while — the relief strengthens checking.

Cues and variable rewards. Notifications act as cues that trigger checking automatically, and social media delivers unpredictable payoffs: the next open might reveal a warm message — or nothing. Uncertainty sustains responding, because each check could be the good one; ordinary reinforcement schedules predict this without any disease model. Small environmental changes work with this mechanism: silencing notifications or parking the phone elsewhere removes the cue.

Reinforcement is established when the behavior becomes more likely in the future — it is inferred from an increase in the behavior, not from a pleasant moment. Temporary pleasure or relief alone does not prove that reinforcement occurred.
3. Technology as a Coping Strategy: Leo's Evenings

Some people use technology to manage unpleasant emotions. Example: Leo feels stressed after school and scrolls social media; the distraction temporarily reduces the unpleasant feeling. The pattern can build a loop: stress → social-media use → temporary relief → behavior repeated. In learning terms, stress prompts scrolling and relief reinforces it — negative reinforcement in its most common everyday form.

But this does not prove technology caused the stress. The relationship may run in both directions: stress drives scrolling (escape), and scrolling can also feed stress (lost sleep, missed work, social comparison, conflict). Direction has to be tested, never assumed — a theme the research sections pursue.

Leo checks social media whenever he feels anxious. He feels calmer afterward and begins checking more frequently. Explain how negative reinforcement could account for this pattern.
Leo's checking is strengthened by the relief it produces. Anxiety is an unpleasant internal state; checking social media removes or reduces it for a while, and the removal is rewarding. When a behavior is followed by the reduction of something unpleasant, the behavior becomes more likely in the future — the defining feature of negative reinforcement. It differs from positive reinforcement, where a behavior is followed by an added pleasant outcome (such as receiving likes). The increase in checking frequency, not the moment of calm, is what indicates reinforcement has taken place. One caution: the description is consistent with negative reinforcement rather than definitive proof of it — reinforcement is inferred from the increased future likelihood of the behavior, and alternatives such as habit or boredom would have to be ruled out.
B. How Can Technology Influence Health?

The same psychological mechanisms can help or harm, depending on what is used, by whom and why. Potential benefits and risks are specific and conditional — outcomes follow particular uses in particular circumstances, not device ownership.

4. Potential Benefits
  • Access to information. Reliable online sources teach sleep hygiene, exercise principles and mental-health strategies — information many people would never otherwise meet.
  • Social connection. Online communities can supply support where offline life does not: an isolated teenager may find a moderated support group that normalizes help-seeking.
  • Digital interventions and prevention. Apps and programs support exercise tracking, mindfulness practice, medication reminders, structured therapy modules and online CBT — extending care between appointments. "Technology" here spans trackers, reminder tools and behavior-monitoring programs, each a different intervention.
  • Healthcare access. Tele-health reaches people for whom face-to-face care is distant, costly or stigmatised.

Tele-therapy — psychological treatment delivered remotely through video or other secure systems — illustrates both promise and limits: potential benefits include increased access, convenience, reduced travel, reach to geographically isolated clients and continuity of care; potential limitations include unequal digital access, privacy and confidentiality risks, technology failures, fewer non-verbal cues, and differing suitability across clients and problems. It is neither universally better nor universally worse than face-to-face delivery — it is evaluated case by case.

Not every digital tool deserves the benefit of the doubt: availability, popularity and polished design do not by themselves show that an intervention improves health. Evidence of effectiveness has to be checked — the study and evaluation sections below supply the tools.

5. Potential Risks

Equally specific pathways — each a mechanism, not a moral:

  • Sleep disruption. Late-night phone use delays bedtime and fragments sleep (light, arousal, "one more video"), and short sleep degrades next-day mood, concentration and stress tolerance — feeding the stress–scroll–sleep cycle.
  • Social comparison. Idealised feeds invite upward comparison — measuring yourself against curated highlights — and repeated unfavorable verdicts can wear self-evaluation down: content matters more than minutes.
  • Attention disruption. Constant notifications fragment study into pieces, and fragmented attention functions as reduced concentration.
  • Problematic patterns of use. Repeated checking despite wanting to stop — the three markers of problematic use fully present.
These risks are possibilities, not certainties: none of them follows from owning a device. They follow from particular uses, in particular people, under particular conditions — which is what the evidence on individual differences shows.
6. Why Technology Affects People Differently

Same technology, different effects — depending on:

  • personality (comparison-prone vs easygoing),
  • motivation (connection-seeking vs escape-seeking),
  • existing stress (resourced vs overwhelmed),
  • social environment (supportive vs mocking, connected vs isolated),
  • purpose of use (the decisive splitter).

Example: a student using social media mainly to maintain friendships (arranging meetings, shared jokes, visible support) experiences genuine connection; a student using it mainly to escape stress (midnight scrolling, comparison spirals, relief loops) is caught in escape loops. Same app, opposite psychologies — so blanket verdicts on "social media" misdescribe both students at once.

Two students spend three hours per day using social media. One maintains healthy sleep and relationships, while the other experiences disrupted sleep and difficulty controlling their use. Explain why screen time alone is insufficient to determine whether social-media use is problematic.
Screen time describes exposure only; it says nothing about why and how the person uses technology or what follows from it. The students differ on the markers psychologists use to identify problematic use: control (the first student stops in time, the second checks compulsively), purpose (connection and support vs escape from stress) and consequences (intact sleep and relationships vs lost sleep and poorer concentration). Frequency cannot separate them because the same three hours carry different psychological meanings — genuine connection for one, escape-and-relief loops for the other. The judgment also depends on individual differences (personality, existing stress, social support) that shape how the same app affects different people. Three hours by itself is therefore weak evidence; purpose, control and consequences must be assessed together, and high use is entirely compatible with healthy use.
C. What Does the Research Show?

Individual cases matter, but a high-scoring answer also needs the population-level evidence — and the largest single dataset analysis of technology and wellbeing points in a direction that surprises many students.

7. Key Study: Orben and Przybylski (2019)

Aim. To test rigorously how the correlational evidence linking digital-technology use to adolescent wellbeing behaves — whether results are robust or depend on researchers' analytical choices.

Data. Specification curve analysis across three large representative datasets: Youth Risk Behavior Survey and Monitoring the Future (USA) and the Millennium Cohort Study (UK) — together n = 355,358 adolescents aged roughly 12–18, surveyed between 2007 and 2016. Specification curve analysis runs every justifiable specification rather than one — hundreds of millions of possible combinations — exposing how researcher choices move the results, with bootstrapped significance tests and comparison specifications (everyday factors measured in the same data).

Measures. Both variables were self-report survey measures of digital-technology use (for example screens, social media, devices) and of adolescent wellbeing (established wellbeing scales in the datasets) — secondary data, not direct behavioral logs.

Findings. The association with wellbeing was negative but small: median standardized effects around −0.035 (YRBS), −0.005 (MTF — inside the non-significant range) and −0.032 (MCS) — technology use explaining at most about 0.4% of the variation in wellbeing. Results swung with specifications (positive, negative or null all obtainable by choosing analyses) — for context, regularly eating potatoes showed a similar-sized association, and wearing glasses a worse one. The authors judged the population-level effects too small to warrant policy change on their own.

The findings also deliver a statistics lesson in miniature, in student-friendly terms: a result is statistically significant when it would be unlikely under the statistical model if there were no real effect — a detection result. Practical importance asks a different question: is the effect large enough to matter in real life? O&P's associations were detectable, yet so small that daily life changes little at the average level — detecting an effect is not the same as the effect mattering.

Small average association does not mean no individual effects. An average across 355,358 users with different purposes, personalities and conditions can coexist with substantial effects for particular individuals (for example heavy escape-motivated use disrupting sleep). The study also cannot establish causation, because the data are cross-sectional and correlational by design — direction and third variables stay open.
Orben and Przybylski (2019) reported a small association between digital technology use and adolescent well-being. Explain why this finding does not establish that technology causes poorer mental health.
A single correlational finding cannot establish causation, for several reasons. First, direction is open: students already experiencing poorer wellbeing — stress, loneliness, low mood — may increase their technology use to cope (reverse causation), or a third variable such as sleep problems, exam stress or family conflict could produce both more screen use and lower wellbeing; no variable was manipulated, so neither alternative was controlled. Second, the study's own scale limits the causal reading: the average association explained at most about 0.4% of wellbeing variation — comparable in size to eating potatoes — so even a genuine causal component would be small at population level. Third, averages conceal individuals: a small average association does not show technology is harmless for particular users, for whom mechanisms such as sleep disruption or escape-driven use can be substantial. Establishing cause would require longitudinal or experimental designs that separate direction and hold third variables constant; specification-curve analysis shows which associations are robust to researcher choices — not what causes them.
D. How Should Psychologists Evaluate the Evidence?
8. From Association to Argument

Finding that heavier users report poorer wellbeing does not prove technology caused it. Two alternatives fit the same correlation:

Reverse causation. People experiencing stress or loneliness may use technology more. Example: someone feeling isolated increases social-media use to seek connection — the distress drives the hours, not the reverse. Treating their scrolling would then miss the loneliness causing it.

Third variables. Another factor may drive both use and wellbeing. Example: chronic stress simultaneously increases late-night scrolling (escape), shortens sleep, and lowers mood — producing a use–wellbeing correlation with no direct causal path between them.

Only designs that separate these — longitudinal tracking, random assignment, natural experiments — can move from association toward cause. Until then, honest conclusions stay associational and say so.

Whether an intervention improves wellbeing adds further questions: statistical significance must be separated from practical importance (Section 7), short-term gains may not last, and "technology" is never one variable. Digital tools in prevention and treatment — apps, trackers, online CBT, tele-therapy — have to be judged by what was measured, how strong the study was, and whether the difference was meaningful and lasting.

Evaluation summary — technology-and-health evidence:

  • Correlation vs causation: most evidence (including O&P) is associational; direction stays open.
  • Reverse causality: poor wellbeing may drive more use, not only follow from it.
  • Confounding variables: one factor (stress, family conflict) may produce both more use and poorer wellbeing.
  • Self-reported use: recall and honesty limits — softened when objective logs exist, but most large datasets rely on self-report.
  • Differences between individuals: personality, motivation, stress and support split identical hours into different psychologies.
  • Differences between types of use: "technology" spans passive scrolling, connection, work and treatment — a category too broad for one verdict.
  • Short-term vs long-term: detected effects may not persist; durations shape conclusions.
  • Prevention and treatment: target mechanisms (cues, escape loops, sleep protection, evidence-based interventions) — not devices; evaluate digital interventions for what was measured, method strength and lasting practical importance.
A school proposes banning students' access to social media because it believes this will improve their mental health. Using psychological evidence and concepts, evaluate whether the proposal is justified.
The proposal assumes a simple harm model: social media causes poorer mental health, so removing it must improve wellbeing. The evidence gives this only weak support. Orben and Przybylski's analysis of 355,358 adolescents found negative wellbeing associations that were small at population level — at most 0.4% of variance — and correlational, so a ban targets an exposure that on average explains very little and its causal role is unproven. The stronger psychological case concerns patterns of use, not hours: use marked by loss of control, escape-motivated checking (negative-reinforcement loops) and consequences such as sleep disruption is the pattern aligned with problems, while many students use the same apps for connection and support. A blanket ban also carries costs of its own: it removes genuine benefits (information, moderated communities, continuity of care), conflicts with autonomy-supported change — imposed limits tend to be resented and abandoned because they were never chosen — and leaves individually problematic users unidentifiable among their peers. A more evidence-based policy keeps access and targets mechanisms instead: reduce cues (notifications off, phone-free bedtime routines), teach self-monitoring and if–then plans, and support the minority with problematic patterns. The proposal is understandable but not justified as stated: it generalizes a small average association, ignores reverse causation and individual differences, and chooses the bluntest available tool where mechanism-focused interventions better fit the evidence. Mental health would improve only if the ban happened to remove harmful patterns — and the evidence does not support counting on that.

The program’s effectiveness is likely to emerge from the interaction of motivation, culture and technology rather than from its online format alone. Aisha’s participation is externally regulated: her parents insisted that she join, and her own words — “I don’t really see this as my choice… my parents will be angry if I stop” — show that engagement depends on external pressure rather than personal endorsement. Self-Determination Theory distinguishes motivation by how autonomous it is, and sustained behavior change is supported when autonomy, competence and relatedness are satisfied. In Aisha’s case all three are weak: the decision is not her own, her friends mock her effort, and her investment lasts exactly one week — as long as the external pressure is the only reason to continue.

Her statement “I know I’ll fail again” suggests an expectation of failure that may undermine her perceived competence, one of the basic psychological needs: believing that change is impossible makes disengagement more likely. This overlaps with, but is distinct from, self-efficacy — Bandura’s construct refers to a person’s belief in their capability to perform a specific behavior in a specific situation, whereas perceived competence in Self-Determination Theory describes a universal need. Both predict weak persistence here, but they are not interchangeable concepts.

The social environment adds further pressure: her close friends continue messaging late at night and joke about her attempt to reduce her use. The peer group models the old habit and reinforces it socially; changing established behavior is difficult enough, and harder still when relatedness is undermined exactly when support is needed.

Technology, meanwhile, cuts both ways. Online delivery can improve accessibility — video counselling and app-based tracking reach young people who might not attend in person — but it also depends on self-directed engagement (a demand that external regulation struggles to meet), on digital access and privacy, and on the suitability of the format for each participant and problem.

Kenji’s experience introduces the cultural lens. His discomfort with discussing emotional problems and his descriptions of tiredness, headaches and sleep difficulties could reflect cultural influences on how distress is expressed — somatic presentation is well documented — but these details do not establish that culture is the cause; his communication style, personal comfort or the novelty of online counselling could produce the same pattern. The counsellor’s assumption that he is “avoiding the real issue” because he does not talk about sadness could indicate cultural bias or an imposed etic — treating one culture’s norms of expression as universal — but the scenario does not prove the counsellor is culturally biased; bias is a possibility to test, not a conclusion to assert.

The director’s conclusion — that because the program is online “it should work equally well for young people everywhere” — assumes a uniformity the scenario itself contradicts. Delivery removes distance, not differences: motivation, cultural expression, social support, access and individual circumstances still shape outcomes, as the contrast between Aisha’s disengagement and Kenji’s communication style shows. Equal effectiveness across participants or populations cannot be assumed.

Judging effectiveness would require evidence: validated outcome measures of well-being and problematic use rather than goal completion alone; engagement data broken down by different participant groups; indicators of daily functioning such as sleep and school performance; follow-up assessment showing whether improvements persist; and an appropriate comparison condition — a waitlist or alternative program — without which improvement cannot be attributed to the program. Analyses comparing different cultural and motivational profiles would test the claim that the program works “equally well” directly, and would likely show that conclusion to be qualified at best.

IB Concepts in Health & Well-being

Using IB concepts to analyze health-related psychology — the bridge to Paper 1 Section C.

Section 1 — Why IB Concepts Matter

Psychologists do more than describe behavior. They investigate why it occurs, examine the quality of evidence, and consider whether explanations apply across different people and situations.

The six IB concepts help us ask more precise questions about psychological research. In this lesson, you will use them to evaluate explanations and interventions from the Health & Well-being unit and develop arguments supported by evidence.

Section 2 — The Six IB Concepts in Health & Well-being

Each concept is a question to ask of evidence — not a term to recite. The examples below all come from this unit.

Bias. Bias is systematic distortion in how information is gathered, interpreted or reported — pushing conclusions consistently in one direction, unlike random error.

Example: distress expressed through physical symptoms can go undetected by mood-focused assessment tools, and clinician expectations shaped diagnostic judgments — Regier et al. (2013) found two blinded clinicians agreed on an MDD diagnosis far less often than assumed (κ = 0.28).

Question: how might researchers’ assumptions, sampling decisions, or diagnostic expectations influence findings about depression or problematic social-media use?

Causality. Causality asks whether one variable actually produces change in another — a question an observed association alone can never settle.

Example: SSRIs reducing symptoms does not prove that low serotonin caused the depression; and heavier social-media use correlating with poorer well-being fits several competing explanations at once.

Question: does an observed relationship establish that one variable causes another? Could the direction be reversed, or could another variable explain the association?

Change. Change concerns how symptoms and behaviors develop over time — and whether an intervention’s effects appear, persist and matter.

Example: Hunt et al. (2018) found three weeks of limited social-media use reduced loneliness and depression in an experimental sample — but a three-week study cannot say whether the improvement lasts.

Question: what evidence shows whether an intervention produces lasting improvement, rather than a short-term shift?

Measurement. Measurement turns psychological constructs into something recordable — and the operational definition chosen shapes the findings available. A reliable measure gives consistent results; a valid one measures what it claims to measure — and neither guarantees the other.

Example: problematic-use prevalence varies with the questionnaire and cut-off chosen; depression is measured through self-report instruments such as the PHQ-9 and BDI-II, and a questionnaire score is not a complete understanding of a person’s experience.

Question: how do psychologists operationalize depression, problematic social-media use, and well-being — and are the measures reliable and valid for the intended purpose?

Perspective. Perspectives are viewpoints that reveal different aspects of the same phenomenon: biological, cognitive, sociocultural and individual differences each illuminate something the others miss.

Example: genetic vulnerability explains part of depression risk, but the Diathesis-Stress Model needs environmental stress to explain who actually becomes depressed — one perspective contributes without completing the picture.

Question: how might biological, cognitive, sociocultural, and individual perspectives produce different but potentially complementary explanations?

Responsibility. Responsibility concerns the ethical obligations of those who study, diagnose or treat psychological health problems — and those who design the environments that shape them.

Example: if platforms maximize engagement by design, responsibility for problematic use is shared across individuals, parents, schools, health professionals, technology companies and researchers.

Question: what ethical obligations do researchers, clinicians, and institutions have when studying, diagnosing, or treating psychological health problems?

Section 3 — How to Build a Conceptual Argument

Identifying a concept earns little on its own; the skill is using it to reason. A reliable sequence turns any concept into analysis:

Claim. State a precise position worth examining — not a topic restated, but something that could be supported or challenged.

Psychological evidence. Bring in research or scenario evidence, stated accurately, including what its design actually licenses.

Conceptual analysis. Use the concept to interrogate the evidence: what can it show, what can it not show, and what alternative explanations fit the same data?

Qualified judgment. Conclude only as far as the evidence and analysis allow — and say so explicitly.

Weak response: “Social media causes depression because research shows a relationship.”

Developed response: “An association between social-media use and depressive symptoms does not establish causality. Adolescents experiencing distress may increase their social-media use, while factors such as loneliness or sleep disruption could influence both variables. The direction and strength of any causal relationship therefore require further investigation.”

Why the second is stronger. The weak response treats an association as an established causal fact, cites “research” without identifying any study or design, and offers no evaluation. The developed response states what the finding can and cannot show, names the specific alternatives (reverse causality, third variables such as loneliness and sleep disruption) that fit the same data, and ends with a conclusion proportioned to the evidence — not because it used a template, but because it selected relevant reasoning and executed it accurately.

The sequence is a tool, not a guarantee: high marks still depend on selecting relevant evidence and developing accurate psychological reasoning within it.

Section 4 — Worked Example: Causality and Technology

“To what extent can research establish that digital technology use causes poorer psychological well-being?”

The evidence. Orben and Przybylski (2019) analyzed self-reported technology use and well-being in three large representative datasets — together n = 355,358 adolescents aged roughly 12–18 — using specification-curve analysis, which runs every defensible analytical combination to show which results are robust to researchers’ choices and which are artifacts of them.

Findings. The association with well-being was consistently negative but very small: median standardized effects around −0.035, −0.005 and −0.032 across the datasets, explaining at most about 0.4% of the variation in well-being — comparable in size to regularly eating potatoes.

Applying the concept. The study is correlational and cross-sectional, so it cannot establish causation. The direction is open: adolescents already experiencing distress — low mood, loneliness, exam pressure — may increase their technology use to cope, so poorer well-being could drive use rather than follow from it. Third variables could also produce both: chronic stress raises late-night scrolling and shortens sleep while lowering mood, generating a use–wellbeing correlation with no direct causal path between them. No variable was manipulated, so neither alternative was controlled.

The specification-curve design strengthens one conclusion only: that a small negative association is robust to researcher choices at the population level. It does not license a causal reading — and a small average across 355,358 users does not rule out substantial effects for particular individuals, such as escape-motivated use that disrupts sleep.

Qualified conclusion. The research establishes a small, robust negative association between digital technology use and adolescent well-being — consistent with a possible causal contribution, but not demonstrating one. Causal claims would require longitudinal tracking or experiments that separate direction and hold third variables constant; on current evidence, “technology use causes poorer well-being” overstates what the data can support.

Section 5 — Independent Concept Practice

Work each scenario yourself before revealing the model. Justify your judgments — naming concepts is not enough.

Application 1 — Measurement and Bias. Researchers compare depression rates across two cultural groups using the same questionnaire. One group reports substantially fewer symptoms. Using the concepts of Measurement and Bias, explain why this difference may not establish that depression is genuinely less common in one group.

One questionnaire does not guarantee one measurement function. The same instrument can behave differently across groups: translated items may shift meaning, reference periods or thresholds may not fit both groups equally, and the questionnaire’s mood-focused items may fail to capture distress expressed through physical symptoms — a cultural-validity problem, not an automatic bias. Response patterns can also differ: disclosure norms shape what respondents are willing to report, and sampling differences — who was recruited, who completed the survey — can produce different profiles before depression itself differs at all.

Bias is therefore one candidate explanation among several, and the scenario does not establish even that. The counsellor-equivalent caution applies to researchers: diagnostic assumptions — expecting distress in one form — may distort interpretation, but this too must be tested rather than presumed.

Before concluding that depression is genuinely less common, researchers would need evidence that the measure functions equivalently in both groups — validated translations, equivalent thresholds, checks on differential item functioning — alongside comparable sampling procedures and convergent evidence from clinical interviews or indicators of daily functioning. Until then the finding licenses a question, not a conclusion: the difference may be real, may be measurement, may be bias — and the study as described cannot decide between them.

Application 2 — Change and Responsibility. A school introduces an intervention to reduce problematic social-media use. After four weeks, students report spending less time online, but their sleep, stress, and academic functioning have not improved. Using the concepts of Change and Responsibility, evaluate whether the school should describe the intervention as successful.

The intervention changed a behavior — reported time online — but not the outcomes that presumably motivated it: sleep, stress and academic functioning are unchanged. Change as a concept demands evaluation on the outcomes that matter, measured validly, over timeframes long enough to show persistence, against an appropriate comparison. By each criterion the evidence is weak: self-reported screen time is easily distorted; four weeks cannot show whether reduced use lasts; and without a comparison group, the reduction might reflect ordinary term-time variation rather than the intervention itself.

Responsibility extends the evaluation to honesty. The school has an obligation not to overstate: describing the program as “successful” on usage data alone risks misleading students, parents and staff, and committing resources to an approach whose benefits are undemonstrated. Responsible reporting states precisely what changed, what did not, and what remains unknown — and if the program continues, the school should monitor well-being outcomes rather than usage alone.

As currently evidenced, the intervention cannot be described as successful: it achieved behavioral change without demonstrated benefit. The defensible conclusion is qualified — promising in direction, unevaluated for well-being — with the responsibility falling on the school to measure what matters before claiming more.

What Should I Remember?

  1. IB concepts are evaluation tools: they decide whether evidence supports a conclusion — never vocabulary to recite.
  2. Understanding: individual differences (same event, different outcomes), interaction (effects depend on combinations), context (evidence travels with its circumstances).
  3. Evaluating I: measurement (operational definitions shape findings; scores ≠ experience), reliability (Regier κ = 0.28 for MDD — consistency must be demonstrated), validity (reliable ≠ right).
  4. Evaluating II: bias (same thing for everyone?), association ≠ causation (cause, reverse, third variable — design decides).
  5. Applying: interventions are judged by target, measure, comparison, size and alternatives — effectiveness never proves origin.
  6. Run every claim through the Big Picture questions before concluding how confident the science allows you to be.

Section 1 — What Does a Strong IB Psychology Answer Look Like?

Knowing a theory or remembering a study is only the beginning. Strong psychology answers use that knowledge to explain behavior, apply psychological ideas to unfamiliar situations, and evaluate what the evidence actually supports.

The aim of this lesson is to practice those skills using Major Depressive Disorder and problematic social-media use.

Four levels of psychological work are involved, and they are not interchangeable:

Describing a concept states what it is: “Beck's model proposes negative schemas and negative automatic thoughts.” Description alone answers “what?”.

Explaining a mechanism says how it works — the schema filters experience, generating interpretations that shape mood and behavior.

Applying uses the mechanism on the specific material in front of you — the person, the study, the scenario — rather than on psychology in general.

Evaluating judges what the evidence actually supports, states what it cannot establish, and reaches a conclusion justified by the analysis.

Weak response: “Beck's model says depression involves negative thinking.”

Developed response: “Beck's model proposes that negative schemas influence how experiences are interpreted. For example, a student who believes they are a failure may interpret one disappointing grade as evidence that they will never succeed. This can activate negative automatic thoughts and contribute to hopelessness and withdrawal, potentially maintaining depressive symptoms.”

Why the second is stronger. It moves from label to mechanism (schemas filter interpretation), illustrates the mechanism with a concrete case (one disappointing grade read as global failure), and traces the psychological consequences (automatic thoughts → hopelessness → withdrawal → maintained symptoms). Each step does psychological work the label alone cannot.

Section 2 — Selecting and Using Research Evidence

Strong answers do not list every study remembered; they select the evidence that bears directly on the question and state precisely what it can and cannot show. Three studies from this unit illustrate the discipline.

McGuffin et al. (1996) — genetic vulnerability. What it investigated: whether MDD runs in families beyond shared environment, comparing monozygotic and dizygotic co-twins of people diagnosed with depression. What the findings support: concordance was substantially higher in monozygotic pairs, indicating genetic influence on vulnerability to MDD. What they do not establish: concordance below 100% shows genes are not deterministic — but twin concordance alone does not identify which environmental influences operate, or how; it licenses “genes contribute and do not act alone,” nothing more specific. Exam contribution: the evidence base for genetic vulnerability, best paired with a model that includes environmental stress.

Brown and Harris (1978) — environmental adversity. What it investigated: the circumstances surrounding the onset of depression among working-class women in an inner-London community, using interviews about recent life events and ongoing difficulties. What the findings support: severe, lasting difficulties and losses were far more common before onset, and identifiable vulnerability factors — such as loss of one's own mother in childhood and lacking a close confidant — characterized women who became depressed under adversity. What they do not establish: the study was retrospective and correlational, so it cannot prove adversity caused the depression, and the sample limits generalizability. Exam contribution: evidence that environmental stress is associated with onset — the environmental half of a diathesis–stress argument.

Hunt et al. (2018) — intervention effects. What it investigated: the effect of limiting social-media use to roughly ten minutes per platform per day, with participants randomly assigned, over three weeks. What the findings support: random assignment makes the groups comparable, so improvements in loneliness and depression can be attributed to the reduction in use. What it does not establish: three weeks of self-reported use in one experimental sample says nothing about whether effects persist, which mechanisms produced them, or whether they generalize to other populations and patterns of use. Exam contribution: the strongest available causal evidence that reducing social-media use can improve some outcomes short-term — not proof of long-term treatment effectiveness.

Application. You are asked to advise on the claim: “Reducing social-media use improves adolescent well-being.” Which of the three studies would you select as your central evidence, why is it the right choice — and what would you need to say about its limits before the claim could be accepted?

Hunt et al. (2018) is the only choice that can support a causal version of the claim: random assignment means the groups were comparable, so the improvement can be attributed to the reduction in use rather than to pre-existing differences. McGuffin's twin study concerns genetic vulnerability to depression, and Brown and Harris concern adversity preceding onset — neither bears on whether changing social-media behavior changes well-being, however good those studies are for other questions.

The limits must then be stated: the effect was demonstrated over three weeks, in one experimental sample, with self-reported use — so the claim can be supported only as short-term and provisional. “Improves adolescent well-being” stated without those qualifications would overstate the evidence; stated with them, the selection shows exactly the judgment the question is testing.

Section 3 — Paper 1 Section A: Explanation

Section A questions call for short, mechanism-focused responses. “Explain” requires more than naming: it requires showing how the model's components produce the outcome. A few connected sentences often outperform a paragraph of loose summary.

Practice question: “Explain how the Diathesis–Stress Model can account for the development of Major Depressive Disorder.”

A practice question written in the style of Paper 1 Section A — not an official IB examination question.

Attempt a concise explanation before revealing the model.

The model proposes that depression arises from an interaction between a pre-existing vulnerability — the diathesis — and environmental stress. The diathesis is a predisposition that raises the probability of depression without producing it on its own; it may reflect biological factors, such as a family history of the disorder, or psychological characteristics such as pessimistic patterns of thinking. An environmental stressor is an adverse experience or circumstance — significant life events, chronic difficulties such as ongoing financial or relationship strain, or major transitions — that demands adjustment from the person.

The model's central claim is that the two interact: a person carrying substantial vulnerability may remain well while life is uneventful, yet develop depressive symptoms when a stressor arrives, whereas the same stressor may have little effect on someone with little vulnerability. Vulnerability changes how stressful events are experienced and processed, which is why the same event affects different people differently.

The model is probabilistic rather than deterministic: neither the diathesis nor the stressor guarantees depression, and their combination raises risk rather than ensuring the outcome. This fits the unit's evidence — McGuffin et al. (1996) found genetic influence without inevitability — and it is why the model explains depression better than an account resting on genetics or environment alone.

Why the model answer is effective. It defines both components before connecting them, explains the interaction as a mechanism (vulnerability changes how stressors are experienced), and closes by grounding the probabilistic claim in the twin evidence — answering the question asked rather than describing the model in the abstract.

Section 4 — Paper 1 Section B: Scenario Application

Section B rewards using the material in front of you: every mechanism you introduce should be tied to something the person in the scenario actually does or experiences, and more than one psychological process should be involved.

Scenario. Emma is 19 and has recently moved away from home to attend university. She has a family history of depression and has been experiencing considerable academic pressure. After receiving disappointing grades, she repeatedly thinks, “I'm not intelligent enough to succeed.” She has started avoiding friends and spending most evenings alone.

Emma has also begun checking social media whenever she feels stressed. She says scrolling sometimes makes her feel better temporarily, although she often loses sleep as a result.

Task: “Explain how two psychological processes could contribute to Emma's difficulties. Use specific details from the scenario.”

Model response — attempt your own explanation using two processes before revealing this one.

The first process is the Diathesis–Stress interaction. Emma carries a diathesis in the form of a family history of depression — a vulnerability that raises her risk without determining her outcome. The stressors are specific and cumulative: moving away from home, sustained academic pressure, and the disappointing grades that followed. The model explains why these ordinary transitions and setbacks coincide with growing difficulties — vulnerability changes how demanding events are experienced, so the same semester that other students absorb pushes Emma toward depression.

The second process is Beck's cognitive model, which explains how the stressors become sustained low mood. The recurring thought “I'm not intelligent enough to succeed” is a negative automatic thought arising from an underlying negative self-schema: one disappointing result is interpreted not as a setback but as confirmation of a global belief about herself. This interpretation promotes the hopelessness and withdrawal the scenario describes — avoiding friends, spending evenings alone — which in turn removes social contact and absorbing activities, potentially maintaining her low mood rather than allowing it to pass.

Why the scenario does not justify diagnosing Emma with MDD. A diagnosis requires clinical assessment: symptoms must be established in number and duration, functional impairment confirmed, and alternative explanations ruled out. The scenario describes a pattern consistent with depression, but a fictional case description is not a clinical interview — writing “Emma has MDD” claims more than the material licenses. The skill being tested is explaining processes, not assigning disorders.

A caution on negative reinforcement. Checking social media “whenever she feels stressed” and finding it “sometimes” relieving suggests a possible maintaining mechanism: if scrolling reliably removed an unpleasant state, that relief could make checking more likely in future — negative reinforcement. But relief alone does not demonstrate it. The scenario gives no evidence that checking has become more frequent over time or that relief actually increases future checking, and the report is Emma's own, self-described and qualified (“sometimes”). Treat it as a possible mechanism the scenario suggests — not one it establishes.

Extension question: “How might a biological explanation and a cognitive explanation complement one another rather than compete?”

The two explanations operate at different levels of the same problem, so each can supply what the other lacks. A biological account — genetic vulnerability present in Emma's family — explains why she may be more reactive to stress than others, but it cannot say what her depression is about: which experiences, interpretations and behaviors sustain it. Beck's model supplies exactly that — it identifies the schema-driven interpretation (“I'm not intelligent enough to succeed”) and the withdrawal that keep the low mood in place — but it treats the heightened reactivity itself as given.

The diathesis–stress framework makes the complementarity explicit: the biological diathesis sets the vulnerability, cognitive processes shape the response to stress, and each perspective's evidence — family history on one side, schema-consistent interpretation and withdrawal on the other — fits the integrated account rather than competing with it. The perspectives also converge in practice: medication targeting the biological dimension and cognitive therapy targeting the interpretations could each contribute, which would be hard to explain if only one account were correct.

Section 5 — Paper 1 Section C: Conceptual Argument

Section C asks for a sustained argument, and the IB concepts are the tools for building one. The previous lesson introduced the six concepts; here they are used, not re-defined. The sequence from that lesson still applies:

Claim. State a precise position the question can support or challenge.

Relevant evidence. Select research whose design bears on the claim — and state what that design licenses.

Conceptual analysis. Use the concept to interrogate the evidence: what can it show, what can it not, and what alternatives fit the same data?

Qualified judgment. Conclude only as far as the analysis allows — and say so.

Practice question (concept: Causality): “To what extent can research establish that social-media use causes poorer psychological well-being?”

Draw on Orben and Przybylski (2019) and Hunt et al. (2018). Attempt your own answer before revealing the model.

The evidence base. Orben and Przybylski (2019) examined the association between self-reported digital-technology use and well-being in very large adolescent datasets, using specification-curve analysis to test whether the negative association survived every defensible analytical choice. Hunt et al. (2018) randomly assigned participants to limit social-media use to roughly ten minutes per platform per day for three weeks, and compared their well-being outcomes with a control group.

Model response — Causality.

The two studies contribute fundamentally different kinds of evidence, and a causal claim can only be built on what each design actually licenses. Orben and Przybylski's findings are observational: however robust the small negative association, an association cannot separate the three stories that fit it — use worsening well-being, poorer well-being driving greater use, or third factors such as sleep loss or loneliness producing both. Hunt et al. move the question into experimental territory: random assignment makes the groups comparable, so the improvement in loneliness and depression can be attributed to the reduction in use rather than to differences that preceded it.

The distinction matters for every part of the claim. The experimental evidence establishes causation only for what was actually manipulated: reduced use over three weeks, in the sample studied, on the outcomes measured. It does not establish that ordinary use causes harm, that effects persist beyond three weeks, or that the result generalizes across populations, platforms and patterns of use. “Social-media use” in the datasets was self-reported screen time — not the same construct as the problematic, compulsive use this unit examined — and a small average association leaves room for some individuals to be substantially affected while most are not.

The qualified judgment is therefore: current research can establish that substantially reducing social-media use improved some short-term outcomes under experimental conditions, and that a small negative association between use and well-being is robust at population level — but it cannot yet establish that social-media use causes poorer psychological well-being in general. The claim exceeds the evidence; the honest answer states what each design supports and stops there.

Why the two studies are not interchangeable. One observes; the other manipulates. The observational study offers scale and robustness but cannot rule out reverse causality or third variables; the experiment offers causal attribution but only for its manipulation, duration and sample. Citing both as if they said the same thing discards exactly the information the concept of Causality is designed to expose.

Second prompt: “How does the concept of Measurement influence our understanding of depression?”

Consider the PHQ-9 and BDI-II, reliability and validity, and the difference between a questionnaire score and a diagnosis — before revealing the model.

Measurement determines what we can claim to know about depression at all, because depression cannot be observed directly — it is inferred from instruments. The PHQ-9 and BDI-II operationalize depression as responses to fixed items covering mood, loss of interest, sleep and other symptoms; the operational definition chosen shapes the findings available, since instruments weight symptoms differently and may capture some presentations better than others — distress expressed mainly through physical symptoms, for example, can score low on mood-focused items.

Reliability and validity constrain every conclusion built on those scores. A reliable measure gives consistent results; a valid one measures what it claims to measure — and neither guarantees the other. Regier et al. (2013) found that two clinicians applying structured criteria agreed on a diagnosis of MDD far less often than assumed, showing that even expert judgment is a measurement with error; prevalence figures and group comparisons are only as trustworthy as the measurement beneath them.

The deepest influence of Measurement, though, is on the distinction between a questionnaire score and a clinical diagnosis: a score is a number produced by an instrument under fixed conditions, while a diagnosis is a clinical judgment integrating history, context and impairment. Treating scores as if they were the disorder itself inflates certainty — the concept of Measurement keeps our understanding of depression honest by marking the distance between the construct and its instruments.

Section 6 — Final Challenge: Evaluate a Psychological Claim

The most demanding task on this page. Evaluate how justified the claim is using relevant psychological evidence, and let the conclusion follow the analysis — not the other way round.

The claim: “Because biological treatments can reduce depressive symptoms, biological explanations must be the most accurate explanations of depression.”

Evaluate the claim yourself before revealing the model. A sustained argument is required — not a list of strengths and limitations.

The claim's evidence base is real: biological treatments can reduce depressive symptoms, and medication helps many patients. But treatment effectiveness does not establish the original cause, and the claim builds its entire conclusion on that gap. An intervention can act on a system without that system being the disorder's origin — painkillers relieve headaches that are not caused by a painkiller deficiency, and the analogous inference here, that response to antidepressants proves depression is a serotonin deficiency, is precisely the overclaim this unit warned against. What treatment evidence shows is that biological systems are involved in the disorder's expression, not that they are its explanation.

Biological explanations do have direct evidence of their own: McGuffin et al. (1996) demonstrated genetic influence on vulnerability to MDD, and family-history findings point the same way. But the same evidence qualifies the claim rather than confirming it — concordance below 100% shows that genetic vulnerability is not deterministic and leaves room for other factors, without specifying how those influences operate. A claim that biology is “the most accurate” explanation must account for why genetically similar people diverge, and biology alone does not.

Cognitive and environmental explanations account for what the biological account leaves open. Beck's model explains why the same setback produces different interpretations in different people; Brown and Harris (1978) showed that severe difficulties and losses cluster before onset, with identifiable vulnerability factors. Each perspective illuminates mechanisms the others miss — the pattern the Diathesis–Stress Model formalizes: vulnerability, stress and the cognitive processing of both interact in producing and maintaining depression.

The claim is therefore weakly justified. It correctly treats biological factors as important, but “most accurate” falsely assumes that treatment response settles etiology and that the perspectives compete for a single winner. The evidence better supports interaction: biological treatments can reduce symptoms because biological systems are involved, while genetic, cognitive and environmental strands each explain part of the disorder — and the most accurate explanations of depression are those that specify how these influences combine.
