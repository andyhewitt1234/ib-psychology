Research Questions

A good research question is specific, testable, and focused.

A strong research question should be:

  • Specific: Clearly defines what is being studied
  • Testable: Can be investigated through data collection
  • Focused: Addresses one clear relationship or phenomenon
  • Relevant: Contributes to psychological understanding

A hypothesis is a testable prediction derived from a research question.

Example question: Does sleep deprivation affect memory recall?

Hypothesis: Participants who sleep fewer than 5 hours will recall fewer words on a memory test than those who sleep 8+ hours.

The hypothesis must be falsifiable — it must be possible to prove it wrong.

Directional: Predicts the direction of the effect. "More sleep leads to better memory."

Non-directional: Predicts a difference but not the direction. "Sleep duration affects memory recall."

Directional hypotheses are used when previous research supports a specific prediction.

Why is it important that a hypothesis be falsifiable?
Falsifiability is an important feature of scientific hypotheses. A useful scientific hypothesis must make a claim that evidence could potentially show to be wrong. If no possible evidence could challenge a claim, it cannot be meaningfully tested.

Quantitative & Qualitative Research

Before choosing a method, decide what kind of evidence would best answer the research question — and why.

Quantitative evidence is numerical. It measures amounts, frequencies, scores, differences, or relationships.

Examples: Reaction time in milliseconds, questionnaire scores, the number of times a behaviour occurs, test scores.

Analysis: Can be analysed statistically — for example, means, standard deviations, and tests of difference or relationship.

Qualitative evidence is non-numerical. It captures experiences, meanings, perspectives, and behaviour in detail.

Examples: Interview transcripts, open-ended questionnaire responses, field notes from observation, focus-group discussions.

Analysis: Commonly analysed by identifying patterns and themes (for example, thematic analysis).

The appropriate type of evidence depends on the research question. Neither quantitative nor qualitative evidence is automatically better.

"Does sleep duration predict exam performance?" Quantitative evidence may be appropriate — it can measure the relationship numerically across many students.

"How do students experience exam stress?" Qualitative evidence may be appropriate — interviews can capture the meaning of the experience in students’ own words.

Researchers may also combine both when useful — for example, exploring an experience with interviews and then measuring how common it is with a questionnaire.

A researcher counts aggressive behaviours each child displays during a 30-minute session. What type of data is this?
This is quantitative data because it involves counting and producing numerical data that can be statistically analysed.
A psychologist wants to understand why some students feel unable to speak during class discussions. Would quantitative or qualitative evidence be more useful initially, and why?
Qualitative evidence would probably be more useful initially: interviews could provide detailed insight into students’ experiences and the reasons behind them. Quantitative measures could then test how common particular factors are across a larger group, once the key factors are known.

The Main Research Methods

A map of the major ways psychologists investigate behaviour. For each method, focus on what the researcher does, what kind of question it can answer, what the evidence can support, and one important limitation. Each method is covered in more depth later in the unit.

In an experiment, the researcher deliberately changes one factor — the independent variable (IV) — and measures whether this produces a change in another factor — the dependent variable (DV).

Example: A psychologist investigates whether sleep affects memory. One group is assigned to sleep for 8 hours and another for 4 hours. The next morning, both groups complete the same memory test. Hours of sleep is the IV and memory-test score is the DV.

Because the researcher manipulates the IV, experiments can provide particularly strong evidence for cause and effect when alternative explanations are adequately controlled and appropriate random assignment is used.

Important limitation: Experimental situations can sometimes be artificial, and an experiment does not automatically establish causation if important confounding variables have not been controlled.

A quasi-experiment compares groups that already differ on an important characteristic. The researcher does not randomly assign people to these naturally existing groups.

Example: A psychologist compares memory performance in people with and without a naturally occurring neurological condition. The researcher cannot randomly assign participants to have the condition.

The study can show whether the groups differ in memory performance, but we must be cautious about saying that the neurological condition caused the difference.

Important limitation: Because the groups were not created through random assignment, they may differ in other important ways that could also explain the results.

In a natural experiment, an event or situation that occurs naturally creates the conditions researchers want to compare. The researcher studies what happens but does not create the event itself.

Example: Psychologists could compare stress levels in students affected by a sudden school closure with students whose schools remained open.

Natural experiments allow psychologists to investigate real-world events that would be impossible or unethical to create deliberately.

Important limitation: Researchers have less control over who experiences the event and over other differences between the groups, so alternative explanations may remain.

A correlational study measures two co-variables to see whether they are related. The researcher does not manipulate either variable.

Example: A psychologist records students’ average hours of sleep and their exam scores and finds that students who sleep more tend to achieve higher scores.

This allows the researcher to conclude that sleep and exam performance are associated. It does NOT show that additional sleep caused the higher scores.

Important limitation: Another variable — such as stress, health, or study habits — could explain the relationship, and the direction of the relationship may also be unclear.

A survey or questionnaire collects self-report data by asking participants a standardised set of questions. Questions may produce numerical responses, written responses, or both.

Example: A psychologist asks 500 students how many hours they use social media each day and asks them to rate their stress from 1–10.

This can efficiently show patterns in what a large group of people report about themselves.

Important limitation: People’s answers are not always completely accurate. Memory, misunderstanding, and social desirability can influence how they respond.

In an observation, researchers systematically watch and record behaviour. Observations can take place in natural or controlled settings and may be overt or covert.

Example: A psychologist observes children on a playground and records how many times each child shares a toy during a 30-minute period.

Observation allows researchers to study what people actually do rather than relying only on what they say they do.

Important limitation: People may change their behaviour when they know they are being observed, and researchers’ expectations can sometimes influence how behaviour is recorded.

A case study is an in-depth investigation of one person, group, organisation, or unusual event. Researchers often combine several sources of evidence, such as interviews, observations, psychological tests, and records.

Example: A psychologist could study a patient who developed severe memory difficulties after a brain injury, combining memory tests, interviews, medical records, and brain scans.

Case studies can provide extremely detailed evidence and are especially useful for studying unusual cases that would be difficult to investigate with large samples.

Important limitation: A single case may be unusual, so its findings may not generalise to other people.

Longitudinal and cross-sectional designs are not separate data-collection methods. They describe how a study compares people across time or age and can be combined with methods such as interviews, observations, or psychological tests.

Longitudinal example: A psychologist measures the same children’s self-control at ages 6, 10, and 14 to examine how it changes as they develop.

Cross-sectional example: A psychologist measures self-control in groups of 6-year-olds, 10-year-olds, and 14-year-olds during the same year.

Longitudinal research can show how the same people change over time, whereas cross-sectional research allows researchers to compare different groups much more quickly.

Important limitations: Longitudinal studies take considerable time and may lose participants along the way. Cross-sectional studies can confuse genuine developmental differences with cohort differences between the groups.

How do you choose the right research method?
Match the method to the research question. Experiments suit questions about cause and effect; quasi-experiments, natural experiments, and correlational studies suit variables that cannot be manipulated; surveys gather self-report data at scale; observation captures real behaviour; case studies provide depth. The research question should determine the method — not the other way around.

Variables

The building blocks of any psychological study.

A variable is any characteristic, event, or condition that can take on different values. Psychological research studies the relationships between variables.

This page introduces the full family of variables you will meet in studies — independent, dependent, co-variable, extraneous, and confounding — and why knowing which is which matters for interpreting any result.

The independent variable (IV): In a true experiment, the IV is the variable deliberately manipulated by the researcher — it is what the researcher changes.

Example — caffeine and memory: The researcher sets the conditions of the IV: 0 mg (placebo) versus 200 mg of caffeine.

Ask yourself: “What did the researcher CHANGE?” → The IV.

The dependent variable (DV) is the outcome that is measured to determine whether it differs across the levels or conditions of the IV.

Example — caffeine and memory: Number of words correctly recalled on a memory test.

Ask yourself: “What did the researcher MEASURE?” → The DV.

In correlational research, researchers normally do not manipulate an IV. Instead, they measure two variables to see whether they are associated. These are called co-variables.

Example: Research question — “Is the number of hours students sleep associated with their examination performance?”

  • Co-variable 1: Hours of sleep per night
  • Co-variable 2: Exam score

Neither is manipulated — the researcher simply measures both and looks for an association.

Experiment vs correlation:

  • Experiment: IV → manipulated; DV → measured.
  • Correlation: Co-variable 1 → measured; Co-variable 2 → measured.

An extraneous variable is any variable other than the IV that could influence the DV.

Study: A psychologist investigates whether background music affects memory.

  • IV: Study condition — Condition A: students learn a word list in silence; Condition B: students learn a word list while music plays.
  • DV: Number of words correctly recalled.

Ask: “What ELSE could affect how many words a student remembers?”

  • how much sleep the student had
  • natural memory ability
  • motivation
  • caffeine consumed that morning
  • noise outside the room
  • time of day

All of these are potential extraneous variables because they could affect the DV — memory performance — even though they are not the variable the researcher is trying to investigate.

Important: The mere existence of an extraneous variable does not automatically mean the study is confounded.

Example 1 — extraneous, but NOT confounding:
Does background music affect memory? Condition A: 20 students learn words in silence. Condition B: 20 students learn words with music. Imagine one student in the music group slept only 4 hours the previous night.

Lack of sleep could affect that student’s memory, so sleep is an extraneous variable. But one tired student does not automatically make sleep a confounding variable — because sleep has not been shown to systematically differ between the two conditions. It is simply another factor that could influence performance.

Example 2 — now it becomes a confound:
Keep exactly the same experiment. But now the silence group is tested at 9:00 AM after arriving at school, while the music group is tested at 4:00 PM after a full school day.

  • Silence group → tested at 9 AM
  • Music group → tested at 4 PM

Now time of day systematically changes with the IV condition. Suppose the music group recalls fewer words. We now have two possible explanations: (1) music reduced memory performance, or (2) the students tested at 4 PM were more tired.

Therefore time of day is now a confounding variable — it systematically differs between conditions and gives us an alternative explanation for the result.

One student in the music group was tired, but tiredness was not shown to differ between the groups. Is tiredness a confound? Now suppose every silence participant is tested at 9 AM and every music participant at 4 PM — is time of day a confound?
Tiredness in one student is an extraneous variable — it could affect the DV but has not systematically differed between the groups, so it is not yet a confound. Time of day, however, now systematically changes with the condition (all silence at 9 AM, all music at 4 PM), so it is a confounding variable because it offers an alternative explanation for any difference in recall.

Study: A psychologist investigates whether a mindfulness programme reduces anxiety.

  • Group A: receives a 6-week mindfulness programme.
  • Group B: receives no mindfulness programme.
  • DV: anxiety score after six weeks.

Potential extraneous variables: previous experience with meditation, stressful life events, amount of sleep, other mental-health support, baseline anxiety.

Now one becomes a confound: Suppose everyone in the mindfulness group also receives weekly individual counselling, while nobody in the control group does. Counselling now systematically differs between the groups. If the mindfulness group later reports lower anxiety, we cannot tell whether the difference resulted from mindfulness or from counselling. Therefore counselling is a confounding variable.

Make the reason explicit: The problem is not simply that counselling “might affect anxiety.” The problem is that counselling changes systematically with group membership — that is what turns it into a confound.

Researchers try to prevent potential extraneous variables from becoming confounds through techniques such as:

  • standardised procedures
  • keeping testing conditions similar
  • random assignment where appropriate
  • controlling important environmental differences

Example: If both the music and silence groups are tested in the same room, at similar times, with identical instructions, and for the same amount of time, then these potential alternative explanations are better controlled.

(1) What are the IV and DV? (2) Student A happened to sleep badly — is sleep automatically a confound? Sleep: No. Sleep could affect test performance, so it is a potential extraneous variable. But unless sleep systematically differs between the groups, it is not necessarily a confound.

Noise: Yes. Noise could affect test performance and systematically differs between the two conditions (quiet classroom vs construction site), so it provides an alternative explanation for any difference in scores — it is a confounding variable.

Operationalization

Turning abstract concepts into concrete, measurable procedures.

Operationalization is the process of defining a variable in terms of exactly how it will be measured or manipulated. Psychology deals with abstract constructs — aggression, intelligence, stress — that cannot be observed directly. An operational definition translates the construct into something concrete and observable.

Example: The abstract concept "aggression" becomes "the number of verbal attacks recorded during a 10-minute observation."

Without operational definitions:

  • Hypotheses cannot be tested — there is nothing concrete to measure.
  • Studies cannot be replicated — other researchers do not know exactly what was done.
  • Conclusions become vague — two people can interpret "improved memory" differently.

Precise operational definitions are what allow psychology to be a shared, cumulative science.

The IV: Specify the exact levels of manipulation. Weak: "caffeine." Strong: "participants receive either 0mg (placebo) or 200mg of caffeine, in identical-looking tablets."

The DV: Specify the exact measure and procedure. Weak: "memory performance." Strong: "number of words correctly recalled from a 20-word list after a 60-second distraction task."

Measurement, Reliability & Validity

Psychologists cannot directly see things such as anxiety, intelligence or memory. They have to decide how to measure them — and then ask whether those measurements and conclusions can actually be trusted.

IB Concept — Measurement: distinct kinds of validity and reliability ask different questions about a study. Before judging a result, check whether the measure was good (construct validity / reliability) and whether the conclusion is justified (internal / external validity).

A psychologist wants to test whether a 10-minute mindfulness exercise reduces examination anxiety. Twenty students complete the mindfulness exercise; twenty complete a neutral reading task. Afterwards, every student completes a 10-item anxiety questionnaire scored 0–30.

We use this one study to ask the different quality questions a good methodologist considers.

Construct validity asks: “Did we actually measure the psychological thing we claim to measure?”

In our study, does the questionnaire really capture examination anxiety? Items such as “How worried do you feel about your upcoming examination?”, “How difficult is it to stop thinking about the examination?” and “How physically tense do you feel when thinking about the examination?” look reasonably connected to it.

Now contrast a poor measure: measuring anxiety only with “How many hours did you study yesterday?” Study time may be related to anxiety, but it is not the same thing as anxiety. A measure can produce a number without measuring the intended construct.

Construct validity concerns the link between the abstract psychological concept and the operational measure.

Reliability asks: “Would the measurement give reasonably consistent results if nothing important had changed?”

Test–retest: give a stable personality questionnaire on Monday and again on Thursday. Scores like 12, 25, 18 followed by 27, 8, 30 suggest little consistency; similar scores suggest higher reliability.

Inter-rater: two trained observers watch the same video of children playing and independently count aggressive acts. Observer A counts 7 and Observer B counts 8 — very consistent. Observer A = 7 but Observer B = 21 suggests coding is unreliable.

Why clear operational definitions help: coding “aggression = any push, hit or kick directed toward another child” is far easier to apply consistently than “aggression = acting badly.”

A measure can be reliable yet not valid. Imagine a questionnaire that gives very similar scores every week, but most of its questions actually measure general tiredness rather than depression.

It may be reliable (consistent) but have poor construct validity (not measuring the intended construct).

The mindfulness group reports lower anxiety. Was it the mindfulness exercise — or could the time of day / exam experience explain the difference?

What Makes an Experiment?

The experimental method’s defining feature: the researcher actively intervenes.

The defining logic: The researcher deliberately manipulates an independent variable and measures an outcome while attempting to control alternative explanations.

Randomised controlled trial (RCT): A specific experimental design involving random allocation to conditions, commonly used to evaluate interventions. Not every experiment is an RCT, so the two terms should not be used interchangeably.

Example: Testing whether caffeine improves memory by randomly allocating participants to a caffeine or a placebo condition.

Strength: A well-designed experiment can provide strong evidence for a causal relationship because manipulation, control, and random assignment help rule out plausible alternative explanations.

Limitation: Laboratory settings can seem artificial, so findings may not always transfer to everyday life.

A strong controlled experiment typically combines several important design features:

  • Manipulation — the researcher sets the levels of the IV, establishing a clear temporal ordering between cause and measured outcome.
  • Measurement — the DV is measured precisely to detect any effect of the IV.
  • Control — everything except the IV is held as constant as possible, reducing competing explanations.
  • Random assignment — when the design allows it, participants are allocated to conditions by chance to reduce systematic pre-existing differences between groups. Random assignment strengthens causal inference, but it is not what defines every experiment.

Together, these features are why a well-designed experiment can provide much stronger evidence for a causal relationship than correlational or descriptive research.

Why do experiments provide much stronger evidence for cause and effect than correlational or descriptive research?
A well-designed experiment manipulates the IV, attempts to control extraneous variables, and often randomly assigns participants — so plausible alternative explanations are reduced. Correlational and descriptive research does not manipulate the IV or control competing explanations, so it can show association but provides far weaker evidence for causation.

Causality

The difference between showing that two things are related and proving that one causes the other.

A causal claim states that changes in one variable produce changes in another — not merely that they occur together. "Smoking causes lung cancer" is a causal claim; "smoking is associated with lung cancer" is not.

Psychology aims for causal explanations, but causal claims require stronger evidence than simple co-occurrence.

  • Covariation: The cause and effect must vary together — when the IV changes, the DV changes.
  • Temporal precedence: The cause must come before the effect in time.
  • No plausible alternative explanations: No third factor (confound) can account for the relationship.

Well-designed experiments are particularly powerful for causal inference because manipulation, temporal ordering, control, and random assignment can substantially reduce plausible alternative explanations.

Ice cream sales correlate strongly with drowning deaths — but ice cream does not cause drowning. A third variable (hot summer weather) causes both. This is the third-variable problem.

There is also the directionality problem: even if A predicts B, B might actually cause A. Does low self-esteem cause social media overuse, or does overuse lower self-esteem? A correlation cannot tell you.

Strong experimental designs support causal reasoning through three design features:

  • Manipulation — the researcher actively sets the levels of the IV, establishing a clear temporal ordering between cause and measured outcome.
  • Control — everything except the IV is held as constant as possible, so alternative explanations are reduced or ruled out as far as the design allows.
  • Random assignment — participants are allocated to conditions to reduce systematic pre-existing differences between groups.
A study finds that students who eat breakfast get higher grades. Why can we not conclude that breakfast causes better grades?
This is a correlational finding. Third variables such as family income, sleep routines, or overall health could influence both breakfast habits and grades. There was no manipulation of breakfast and no random assignment, so alternative explanations remain open.

Manipulation & Control

The levers an experimenter pulls to make results trustworthy: changing the IV on purpose, and holding everything else steady.

Manipulation means the researcher actively creates the conditions of the IV rather than waiting to observe them. If you want to know whether caffeine affects memory, you give some participants caffeine and others a placebo — you do not simply ask people how much coffee they usually drink.

Manipulating the IV before measuring the outcome establishes a clear temporal ordering between the IV and the DV.

Some participant characteristics cannot ethically or practically be manipulated: gender, age, personality, trauma history. Researchers may compare naturally occurring groups or measure relationships involving these characteristics.

The classification of the overall study depends on the complete research design, not simply on whether a variable was measured rather than manipulated. Where the grouping is not randomly assigned, causal claims are typically weaker, because people who differ on the characteristic may also differ in other ways (motivation, upbringing, health).

A manipulation check verifies that participants actually perceived the manipulation as intended. If a researcher induces mood with film clips, they might ask participants to rate their current mood before measuring memory.

Without a manipulation check, a null result may simply mean the manipulation failed — not that there is no effect.

Ethics forbids manipulating harmful variables (you cannot randomly assign childhood abuse), and practicality sometimes forbids others. Researchers then use natural experiments, quasi-experiments, or correlational designs — accepting weaker causal claims as the price of feasibility and ethics.

Why does manipulation strengthen a causal conclusion?
Because the researcher controls the IV directly, the manipulated change comes before the measured outcome, so rival explanations about "what came first" are reduced. When manipulation is combined with appropriate control and random assignment, experimental evidence can provide particularly strong support for a causal explanation.

Control means managing the research situation so that the only systematic difference between conditions is the IV. Any uncontrolled difference is a potential confound — an alternative explanation for your results.

An extraneous variable is any factor other than the IV that could influence the DV. It becomes a confounding variable when it systematically differs between conditions and therefore provides an alternative explanation for the results.

  • Standardised procedures: Every participant receives identical instructions, timing, and materials.
  • Control groups and placebos: A comparison condition that receives no treatment or an inert treatment.
  • Random assignment: Distributes participant differences evenly across conditions.
  • Counterbalancing: Half the participants do condition A then B; half do B then A — removes order effects in repeated measures designs.
  • Single- and double-blind procedures: Hide condition assignment from participants (and researchers) to prevent expectation effects.

Laboratory settings tend to maximise control but can feel artificial, which may threaten ecological validity. Real-world settings tend to feel authentic but usually surrender control to weather, noise, and other unmanaged influences.

Every design involves a trade-off between internal validity (control) and external validity (realism) — researchers must decide which matters most for their question.

If Group A is tested in a quiet room at 9am and Group B in a noisy cafeteria at 9pm, any difference in performance could be due to noise, tiredness, time of day — or the IV. With multiple uncontrolled differences, the study cannot isolate the effect of the IV at all.

A researcher tests the effect of a "brain training" app by testing Group A in a computer lab and Group B at home. What is the flaw?
Setting differs systematically with the IV. Distractions at home versus the structured lab could explain any performance difference. Setting should be held constant (or randomised across participants) so that only the app varies.

Random Assignment & Experimental Designs

Using chance — not choice — to place participants into conditions.

Random assignment means every participant has an equal chance of being placed in any condition — decided by coin flip, random number generator, or drawing names. Crucially, neither the participant nor the researcher chooses the group.

Participants arrive with countless differences: motivation, sleep, IQ, anxiety, mood. Individually controlling all of them is impossible. Random assignment reduces systematic pre-existing differences between conditions. Particularly as sample size increases, it makes groups more likely to be comparable on average, although chance imbalances can still occur.

When combined with appropriate control, random assignment strengthens the case that differences between conditions resulted from the IV rather than pre-existing participant differences — which is why it helps protect internal validity.

These are frequently confused:

  • Random assignment happens after recruiting participants: it decides which condition each person joins. It protects internal validity (causal claims).
  • Random sampling happens during recruitment: it decides who from the population takes part. It protects external validity (generalisability).

A study can have either, both, or neither — each serves a different purpose.

Sometimes participants already belong to groups: smokers vs non-smokers, children raised in different countries. When assignment is impossible, the design becomes a quasi-experiment or natural experiment, and causal claims must be made cautiously because pre-existing differences may explain the results.

A drug trial assigns participants alphabetically by surname: A–L get the drug, M–Z get the placebo. Why is this not random assignment?
Surname order is not chance — it may correlate with culture, ethnicity, or family background, which could relate to drug response. True random assignment requires a genuinely random process (e.g., a random number generator) applied to every participant.

Types of Experiments

Psychologists use different kinds of experiments depending on what they want to investigate and what they are able to manipulate or control.

Psychologists use different kinds of experiments depending on what they want to investigate and what they are able to manipulate or control. These labels do not all describe the same feature of a study. Laboratory and field experiments mainly describe WHERE research takes place, while terms such as natural experiment, quasi-experiment and randomized controlled trial (RCT) describe important features of HOW the study is organised.

So these are not five separate boxes to memorise — they describe different features. Laboratory and field describe the setting; natural experiment describes how the IV/event occurs; quasi-experiment describes a pre-existing grouping; and RCT describes random assignment to conditions.

When reading any experiment, ask:

  1. WHERE did it happen?
  2. WHAT was manipulated — if anything?
  3. WHO or WHAT created the groups?
  4. Were participants RANDOMLY ASSIGNED to conditions?
  5. How much CONTROL did the researcher have?

Definition: A laboratory experiment takes place under controlled conditions created by the researcher. The researcher normally manipulates an IV, measures a DV, and attempts to control other factors that could influence the outcome.

Real example — Bandura, Ross & Ross (1961):

  • Aim: To investigate whether children would imitate aggressive behaviour they observed in an adult model.
  • What happened: Children observed an adult behaving either aggressively or non-aggressively toward a Bobo doll. The children were later given an opportunity to play with toys, including the Bobo doll, and their aggressive behaviour was observed.
  • IV: Type of adult behaviour/model observed.
  • DV: Children’s subsequent aggressive behaviour.
  • Finding: Children who observed the aggressive model displayed more imitative aggressive behaviour.

Why is this a laboratory experiment? The researchers deliberately created the conditions and controlled important parts of the environment and procedure. This gave them greater control over possible alternative explanations. However, the situation was artificial — children hitting an inflatable doll in a controlled research setting may not perfectly represent aggression in everyday life.

LAB EXPERIMENT = researcher-created controlled setting + manipulation + measurement.

Definition: A field experiment involves the researcher manipulating something in a naturally occurring, real-world environment.

Real example — Darley & Batson (1973):

  • Aim: To investigate factors affecting whether people help someone apparently in need.
  • What happened: Seminary students were asked to travel to another building. Some were told they were running late, while others were given more time. On the way, they encountered a person apparently needing help.
  • IV: How much time pressure participants were placed under.
  • DV: Whether/how much they helped the person.
  • Finding: Participants under greater time pressure were substantially less likely to stop and help.

Why is this a field experiment? The researchers manipulated time pressure, but participants encountered the situation while moving through an ordinary real-world environment rather than sitting in a traditional laboratory.

Advantage: Behaviour may be more natural. Limitation: Researchers generally have less control over what else is happening in the environment.

FIELD EXPERIMENT = researcher manipulates something + real-world setting.

Definition: In a natural experiment, the researcher studies the effects of a naturally occurring event or situation that the researcher did not manipulate.

Real example — La Greca, Silverman, Vernberg & Prinstein (1996): Following Hurricane Andrew, researchers studied children’s posttraumatic stress symptoms in the months after the disaster. A naturally occurring event (the hurricane) exposed children to different levels of damage and disruption; the researchers compared outcomes across children with different levels of exposure.

  • What naturally occurred: Hurricane Andrew struck, exposing children in the affected region to a major natural disaster.
  • Groups/outcomes compared: Children’s levels of exposure to the hurricane and their posttraumatic stress symptoms.
  • Major finding: Children who experienced more severe exposure generally reported more posttraumatic stress symptoms.
  • Why it is a natural experiment: The “IV-like” exposure — how severely the hurricane affected each child — occurred naturally and was not created or assigned by the researchers.

The researcher did not decide who would experience the naturally occurring event. That is the critical difference from a conventional experiment.

Advantage: Psychologists can investigate events that would be impossible or unethical to create deliberately. Limitation: Because researchers have less control over exposure and other differences, alternative explanations are harder to eliminate.

NATURAL EXPERIMENT = the “IV-like” event occurs naturally rather than being created by the researcher.

Definition: A quasi-experiment compares groups based on a characteristic or grouping that already exists rather than randomly assigning participants to that characteristic.

Real example — Maguire et al. (2000): Researchers compared the brains of experienced London taxi drivers (who spend years navigating complex routes) with control participants. The two groups differ by a pre-existing characteristic — years of professional navigation experience — that researchers cannot randomly assign.

  • Research question/aim: Whether long-term navigation experience is associated with differences in a brain region involved in spatial memory (the hippocampus).
  • Groups: Experienced London taxi drivers vs non-taxi-driver controls.
  • What was measured: Structure of the hippocampus.
  • Key finding: The taxi drivers’ posterior hippocampi differed from the controls in a way linked to their navigation experience.
  • Why this is quasi-experimental: The groups are defined by an important pre-existing characteristic (being a taxi driver), and participants could not be randomly assigned to it.

Researchers cannot randomly assign people to have a neurological condition or years of experience. The grouping already exists. This limits causal conclusions, because the groups may differ in other ways.

QUASI-EXPERIMENT = comparison involving an important pre-existing participant characteristic/grouping.

A researcher compares the memory of people with and without a naturally occurring neurological condition. Why is this best classified as a quasi-experiment rather than a natural experiment?
Because the key grouping variable (the neurological condition) is pre-existing and cannot be randomly assigned by the researcher — the defining feature of a quasi-experiment. A natural experiment compares groups produced by a naturally occurring event or exposure; here the comparison is based on a pre-existing personal characteristic, so a quasi-experiment fits best.

Definition: An RCT is a controlled experiment in which participants are randomly assigned to different conditions, typically including an intervention/treatment condition and a comparison or control condition.

Random assignment: Each participant has a chance of being allocated to the different conditions. Its purpose is to reduce systematic pre-existing differences between groups.

Real example — Elkin et al. (1989), National Institute of Mental Health Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program: A large RCT testing psychological and medical treatments for depression.

  • AIM: To compare the effectiveness of different treatments for major depression.
  • PARTICIPANTS: Adults diagnosed with major depression.
  • RANDOM ASSIGNMENT: Participants were randomly allocated to different treatment conditions.
  • INTERVENTION: One group received cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT); another received interpersonal therapy (IPT).
  • CONTROL/COMPARISON: Other groups received antidepressant medication (imipramine) with clinical management, or a placebo pill with clinical management.
  • DV/OUTCOME: Severity of depressive symptoms.
  • FINDING: Depressed patients improved substantially across the treatments; the active psychological and medical treatments reduced depressive symptoms compared with receiving a placebo.

Why is this an RCT? Because participants were randomly assigned to conditions. That makes it less likely that one condition started with systematically different kinds of participants. Therefore, if the groups later differ, researchers have stronger grounds for considering the intervention as a cause of that difference — assuming other important aspects of the study were well controlled.

RCT = controlled experiment + random assignment to conditions.

RCT and laboratory experiment answer different questions:

  • LABORATORY / FIELD → WHERE is the experiment taking place?
  • RCT → HOW are participants allocated to controlled conditions?

Therefore an RCT could take place in a laboratory or in a school, clinic, hospital, or other real-world setting. RCT and laboratory experiment are not interchangeable terms.

Not every experiment is an RCT. Random assignment is an important method of strengthening causal inference, but it is not present or possible in every experimental design.

  • A researcher can manipulate an IV in a field experiment without necessarily conducting a formal RCT.
  • A quasi-experiment cannot randomly assign participants to the defining pre-existing characteristic.
  • A natural experiment involves an event/exposure the researcher did not create or randomly assign.

So do not use “experiment” and “RCT” interchangeably.

Sampling Methods

Who you study determines who your findings can apply to.

The target population is everyone your research question applies to (e.g., all IB students). The sample is the smaller group you actually study. A sample should be representative — a miniature version of the population — so findings can be generalised.

Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected, typically using a random number generator applied to a full list (e.g., the electoral roll).

Strength: Random sampling reduces systematic selection bias and can improve representativeness when an appropriate sampling frame is available. It does not guarantee that a particular sample will perfectly represent the population.

Limitation: Needs a complete list of the population; hard to achieve; some selected people will refuse to participate.

The population is divided into relevant subgroups (strata) such as age or gender, and participants are drawn from each subgroup in proportion to their presence in the population.

Example: If 20% of a school are IB students, 20% of the sample must be IB students.

Strength: Guarantees subgroup representation.

Limitation: Time-consuming; requires knowing the population's structure.

Using whoever is easily available at the time — often other students or passers-by.

Example: Approaching people sitting alone in a library.

Strength: Fast, easy, inexpensive.

Limitation: Opportunity samples may be unrepresentative because people who are easiest to access may differ systematically from the target population — this is why so much psychology relies on similar, WEIRD samples (university students).

Participants choose themselves by responding to advertisements or appeals.

Example: People answering a newspaper advert about sleep research.

Strength: Easy; participants are willing and engaged.

Limitation: Volunteers differ systematically from non-volunteers (more motivated, more time), limiting generalisability.

A study of adolescent sleep recruits only 16-year-olds from one school's IB programme. What sampling problems does this create?
This is an opportunity sample that is also unrepresentative: it excludes younger adolescents, students in other programmes, and other schools. Generalising conclusions to "all teenagers" would be unjustified — external validity is severely limited.

Sampling Bias & Generalisability

How a flawed sample undermines the claim that findings apply beyond the study.

What it is: The way participants are recruited produces a sample that does not represent the target population. Some types of people end up systematically over-represented while others are missing entirely.

Common causes:

  • Opportunity sampling: using whoever is conveniently available — often university students, who are young, educated and WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic).
  • Volunteer bias: people who volunteer differ systematically from those who don’t — they tend to be more motivated, more confident, or more interested in the topic.
  • Channel bias: online-only surveys exclude elderly people, those without internet access, or those less comfortable with technology.

Example: A researcher surveys daily screen time but advertises only on Instagram. Predictably, teenagers dominate the responses, and the researcher concludes “people spend six hours a day on their phones.” Older adults — a large part of the actual population — were almost invisible in the sample.

An important trap: A large sample is not automatically representative. Ten thousand volunteers recruited through the same biased channel share the same skew as ten. Size improves precision, not representativeness.

Why it matters: Sampling bias threatens external validity (generalisability): the findings may accurately describe the sample but fail to transfer to the wider population.

How to reduce it: Random sampling from the full target population, stratified sampling to mirror population proportions, multiple recruitment channels — and if a biased sample is unavoidable, acknowledging the limitation honestly when drawing conclusions.

What it is: Research designed within one cultural framework — usually a Western one — can produce misleading results when applied to other cultures, because the assumptions, materials or interpretations quietly embed one culture’s norms. Cultural bias can enter at any stage — through sampling (studying a narrow group and treating it as universal), measurement (using measures built on one culture’s assumptions), or interpretation (reading behaviour through the wrong cultural lens).

Three ways it creeps in:

  • Built-in assumptions: Tests designed around Western experiences — e.g. a memory task using shopping lists assumes supermarket experience; a timed IQ format favours cultures familiar with speeded written testing.
  • Translation problems: Emotion words rarely map perfectly across languages, so translated questionnaires may measure something subtly different.
  • Interpreting behaviour through the wrong lens: Avoiding eye contact reads as respect in some cultures and evasiveness in others — a Western observer may code the same behaviour as dishonesty.

Example: A psychological measure developed in one cultural context may not have the same meaning or construct validity in another. Questionnaire wording, testing conventions, or assumptions about appropriate behaviour may differ across cultures, so a test that works well in one setting can assess something different in another.

Why it matters: Cultural bias produces false “cultural differences”, reinforces stereotypes, and claims universality for findings that only hold in one culture. This connects to imposed etic: taking one culture’s tools and treating them as universal standards.

How to reduce it: Adapt instruments with local researchers, use translation-and-back-translation to check meaning, replicate studies across cultures before claiming universals, and prefer culturally grounded (emic) explanations alongside universal ones.

Why doesn’t a large sample fix a biased one?
Size improves precision, not representativeness — ten thousand volunteers recruited through one biased channel share the same skew as ten. The findings may then describe the sample rather than the wider population, threatening external validity.

Participant Effects & Response Bias

Participants are not passive: they react to cues, audiences, and their own self-image.

What it is: Cues in the study itself — the title on the consent form, the setting, the tasks, even rumours between participants — allow participants to work out what the study is about and what the researcher expects to find.

Why it happens: Once people think they know the aim, they rarely behave naturally. Instead they adjust their behaviour based on their guess, not their genuine response.

Participants who think they know the aim may deliberately or unintentionally change their behaviour. They might behave in ways they believe support the hypothesis, resist what they think the researcher expects, or simply behave differently because they know they are being studied.

Example: A student takes part in a study titled “Sleep and Memory”. From the title alone she guesses that tired participants are expected to perform badly — so after a bad night’s sleep she makes an extra effort on the memory test to prove herself wrong. The researcher records good performance from a sleep-deprived participant — not because sleep doesn’t matter, but because the participant guessed the hypothesis and fought against it.

Why it matters: You are no longer measuring genuine behaviour — you are measuring participants’ beliefs about the hypothesis. This threatens internal validity: the results may reflect guesswork rather than the variable being studied.

How to reduce it: Deception (where ethically acceptable), single-blind designs, filler tasks that disguise the real measure, naturalistic settings, and post-study interviews asking “What did you think this study was about?” to check whether guessing occurred.

What it is: In self-report studies, participants answer in ways that make them look good rather than truthfully — underreporting undesirable behaviours and exaggerating desirable ones.

Why it happens: Sensitive topics trigger it most strongly — prejudice, alcohol consumption, aggression, exercise habits, hygiene. It intensifies when answering face-to-face with a researcher present, because the perceived social judgement feels immediate.

Example: A teacher hands out a questionnaire asking how many energy drinks students consume per week. One student drinks five but writes “one or two” — she likes her teacher and doesn’t want to seem unhealthy. The following week, the identical questionnaire appears as an anonymous online form, and she honestly reports five. Same question, same student, completely different answer — purely because of who might be judging.

Why it matters: Self-report is often the only practical way to measure beliefs, feelings and private behaviour — but if the answers are filtered through image management, the data are inaccurate and conclusions built on them are invalid.

How to reduce it: Guarantee anonymity and stress that responses are confidential, collect data online without identifying details, use indirect questions, normalise the behaviour in the wording (“Many students drink energy drinks — how many do you have?”), and triangulate self-reports with behavioural observation where possible.

Anonymity vs confidentiality: Anonymity means the participant’s responses cannot be linked to their identity by the researcher. Confidentiality means the researcher may know who the participant is but protects that information from being disclosed.

Participants in a memory study try extra hard because they want the researcher's hypothesis to succeed. Which biases could explain this?
Demand characteristics — they have guessed the aim and adjusted their behaviour accordingly. If the researcher also treats eager participants differently, researcher bias may compound it. Blind designs and standardised procedures reduce both.

Researcher Bias & Blinding

Expectations can leak into a study from both sides — blinding limits the damage.

What it is: The researcher’s expectations unconsciously influence how they treat participants, record observations, score responses, or interpret the data.

The key word is unconsciously. This is not fraud or lying — even honest, careful researchers are affected. Expectations leak out through small signals: nodding more at one group, reading scoring criteria more leniently for responses that fit the hypothesis, or interpreting ambiguous answers in the expected direction.

A memorable illustration: Student experimenters were told their rats were either “maze-bright” or “maze-dull”. In reality, the rats were randomly assigned — both groups were ordinary. Yet the “bright” rats recorded faster maze times. The students never lied: they simply handled the “bright” rats more confidently and timed more generously. Expectation alone changed the data.

Everyday parallel: A teacher marking essays expects strong work from a usually high-achieving student and grades generously — the same essay submitted under an unknown name might earn a lower mark.

Why it matters: The findings may show what the researcher wanted rather than what actually happened, undermining the objectivity of the measurement.

How to reduce it: Double-blind procedures (neither participant nor person scoring knows the condition), standardised scripts and protocols, operationalised scoring rubrics, and inter-rater reliability checks with blind coders.

Two sources of expectation threaten results:

  • Participant expectancies: People who know which condition they are in respond differently (placebo effects, demand characteristics).
  • Researcher expectancies: Researchers who know the hypothesis treat groups subtly differently or interpret ambiguous behaviour selectively.

Participants are unaware of which condition or treatment they receive — for example, receiving either a real drug or an identical placebo without being told which.

Reduces: Participant expectancy and placebo effects, and some demand characteristics.

Does not reduce: Researcher bias — the researcher still knows the assignments.

Participants and the relevant researchers who interact with participants or assess outcomes are unaware of condition allocation. The relevant people are those who deliver the treatment or measure the outcome — not necessarily every researcher involved in the project.

Reduces: Both participant expectancy effects and researcher expectancy effects.

Example: In antidepressant drug trials, neither patients nor prescribing doctors know who receives the drug versus placebo until after the data is analysed.

Limitation: More complex and costly to organise; sometimes impossible (e.g., a therapy condition is obvious to all).

A placebo is an inert treatment identical in appearance to the real one. Comparing drug vs placebo isolates the drug's true effect from the effect of merely believing one is treated.

Double-blind placebo-controlled designs can provide strong evidence about treatment effects because they reduce both participant and researcher expectancy effects.

Why are drug trials double-blind rather than single-blind?
Single-blinding removes participant expectations but leaves researchers knowing the assignments — they might encourage treated patients more or score outcomes leniently. Double-blinding removes both sources at once, so observed differences reflect the drug itself.

Confounding & Extraneous Variables

The hidden variables that can derail a cause-and-effect claim.

A confounding variable provides an alternative explanation for results. It systematically differs between conditions.

Example: If the caffeine group also arrived earlier and is less tired, alertness confounds the results.

An extraneous variable is any variable other than the IV that could affect the DV. It only becomes confounding if it varies systematically between conditions.

What is the difference between an extraneous variable and a confounding variable?
A confounding variable is an extraneous variable that varies systematically with the IV, providing an alternative explanation. All confounding variables are extraneous, but not all extraneous variables are confounding.

Evaluating Research Quality

Judging the quality of research depends on the type of research and the claims being made.

There is no single checklist that works for every study. The questions you ask depend on the research design and on what the researcher is claiming.

Quantitative research often asks:

  • Is the measure reliable?
  • Is the measure valid?
  • Were variables controlled?
  • Are causal conclusions justified?
  • Is the sample representative enough for the intended conclusion?

Qualitative research often asks:

  • Are the interpretations well-supported by the data?
  • Has the researcher represented participants’ experiences carefully?
  • Has the researcher actively checked alternative interpretations?
  • Is the analytical process transparent and defensible?

Credibility is the extent to which a qualitative interpretation is believable and well-supported by the evidence collected.

Credibility does not mean “the study has been proven true.” It means the researcher has stronger grounds for defending the interpretation.

Later in this unit, when we study qualitative research in detail, we will look at the specific strategies researchers use to strengthen credibility.

A questionnaire producing the same score twice is a reliability issue.

An interview researcher carefully checking whether their interpretation fits participants’ experiences is a credibility issue.

Different research designs require different ways of judging quality.

You are evaluating two studies. One measures the same construct twice on a large sample with a standardised questionnaire; another studies a few cases in depth using interviews. How would you judge the quality of each, and why do the criteria differ?
For the questionnaire study, you would focus on reliability and validity, how variables were operationalised and controlled, and how representative the sample is. For the interview study, you would focus on whether the interpretation is well supported by the data, whether the researcher represented participants’ experiences carefully and checked alternative interpretations, and whether the analytical process is transparent. The criteria differ because the two designs make different kinds of claims and gather different kinds of evidence.

Correlations

Measuring how two co-variables move together without manipulating either.

A correlation measures how two co-variables change together, without manipulating either. Results are plotted on a scattergram and summarised with a correlation coefficient ranging from -1 to +1:

  • +1: perfect positive relationship.
  • 0: no linear relationship.
  • -1: perfect negative relationship.

Ice cream sales correlate with drownings (summer heat drives both); self-esteem correlates with grades (either could drive the other, or a third variable like family support explains both). Correlations cannot resolve the third-variable or directionality problems.

They allow study of variables that cannot ethically or practically be manipulated — smoking and cancer (Doll and Hill's classic studies), poverty and achievement, trauma and depression. They also generate hypotheses that experiments can later test.

Case Study

An in-depth investigation of one person, group, or event.

Case studies combine multiple sources — interviews, observation, records, testing (triangulation) — often over long periods (longitudinal). They suit rare or unusual cases that experiments cannot create.

The cases below are useful illustrations of what a case study can reveal — examples of the method in action, not separate research-methods studies to memorise.

  • H.M. (Scoville & Milner, 1957): After surgery removed substantial medial temporal-lobe tissue, including much of the hippocampal region, H.M. developed profound difficulty forming new explicit memories — providing important evidence about the hippocampal region’s role in memory.
  • Clive Wearing: A musician with profound amnesia whose case showed procedural memory surviving when episodic memory did not.
  • Genie (Curtiss, 1977): A child raised in extreme isolation, studied for evidence of a critical period in language acquisition.
  • Rich, detailed, holistic data impossible to obtain otherwise.
  • Enables study of rare conditions and events.
  • Generates hypotheses and can challenge existing theories (H.M. reshaped memory science).
  • Limited generalisability: findings from one unusual individual, group, or event may not apply to the wider population.
  • Researcher bias: interpretation can drift toward expectations.
  • Inaccurate recall: retrospective accounts may be wrong.
  • Ethics: vulnerable participants, consent and privacy challenges.
Can we conclude from H.M. that the hippocampus is essential for forming new memories in everyone?
The case strongly supports the role of the hippocampus, and later work with other patients converged on the same conclusion. But a single case alone cannot establish universality — it is the replication across cases, plus animal and imaging evidence, that secures the claim.

Observation

Watching behaviour directly instead of asking about it.

  • Naturalistic: Behaviour observed where it normally happens, no interference (e.g., playground interactions). Naturalistic observation often has greater ecological validity because behaviour is studied in its usual context — although this depends on the actual study.
  • Controlled: Behaviour observed in a structured setting created by the researcher (e.g., Ainsworth's Strange Situation). Controlled observation usually allows greater standardisation and control, but the structured setting may sometimes influence behaviour.

Overt: Participants know they are being watched — ethical, but behaviour may change (reactivity/demand characteristics).

Covert: Participants unaware — behaviour is genuine, but there is no informed consent, which raises important issues around consent and privacy. Its ethical acceptability depends on factors such as the setting, participants’ reasonable expectations of privacy, the potential for harm, whether obtaining consent would make the research impossible, and ethical review.

Participant observation: The researcher joins the group (e.g., embedded in a workplace) — insider insight, but risks losing objectivity.

Non-participant: The researcher observes without joining the group — this can reduce the researcher's involvement in the situation, but observer bias can still influence what is noticed or recorded.

Strengths: Captures real, spontaneous behaviour; avoids problems of self-report; useful when people cannot verbalise their experience.

Limitations: Observer bias in interpreting behaviour; no control over variables; cannot establish causation; ethical tensions with covert designs.

A researcher secretly observes patients' behaviour on a hospital ward. What ethical tension does covert observation create?
Covert observation skips informed consent, yet overt observation could make patients self-conscious and distort the very behaviour of interest. There is no simple universal rule: acceptability depends on the setting, patients’ reasonable expectations of privacy, the potential for harm, whether consent would make the research impossible, and independent ethical review. A hospital ward is a setting with high privacy expectations, so the tension is considerable and would need strong justification before proceeding.

Researchers watch behaviour in natural settings and record rich field notes. Combined with interviews, this gives context to what people say versus what they do. Threatened by observer bias and reactivity if participants notice they are watched.

Surveys & Questionnaires

Collecting standardised self-report data from many people at once. A questionnaire is not just a list of questions — the wording and response options determine what data you get, and bad questions can produce bad data.

We follow one research question: “How is social-media use related to stress among teenagers?” As we design a questionnaire, each question type gives a different kind of data.

Closed-ended:

Do you use social media every day?
_Yes  _No

Easy to answer and analyse, but tells us very little about how or why.

Multiple choice:

Which social-media activity takes up most of your time?
_Messaging  _Watching short videos  _Posting content  _Gaming/social interaction  _Other

This produces categories we can count and compare across participants.

Likert-style item:

“When I cannot check social media, I feel uncomfortable.”
Strongly disagree / Disagree / Neither agree nor disagree / Agree / Strongly agree

The categories are ordered, but do not assume every Likert response automatically becomes interval data.

Open-ended:

“Describe a time when social media made you feel stressed.”

Potentially richer qualitative data, but harder and slower to code — and answers may reveal ideas the researcher never predicted.

Leading question:

Bad: “How much does excessive social-media use damage your mental health?” — it already assumes the use is excessive and damaging.

Better: “To what extent, if at all, do you think your social-media use affects your stress?”

Double-barrelled question:

Bad: “How satisfied are you with your sleep and school workload?” — someone may be satisfied with one and not the other. Ask two separate questions.

Ambiguous question:

Bad: “Do you use social media regularly?” — what does “regularly” mean?

Better: “On a typical school day, approximately how many minutes do you spend using social media?”

Socially sensitive question:

“Do you waste hours every day scrolling on your phone?” would encourage defensive responding; neutral wording is better.

Bad wording does not just “create bias”; it flows through the whole study:

BAD QUESTION → DISTORTED RESPONSE → POOR MEASUREMENT → WEAKER CONCLUSION.

Example: if students under-report social-media use because the wording sounds judgmental, the psychologist may underestimate the relationship between use and stress.

Strengths: efficient and cheap — 500 students can complete the same questionnaire quickly; standardised questions make answers comparable across participants.

Limitations: answers rely on self-report. A student selecting “3 hours” may have estimated badly. Social desirability means students may under-report behaviours they think adults disapprove of. Closed items are easy to compare but may force participants into categories that do not fit.

Three proposed questionnaire questions: (1) “Are you addicted to your phone?” (2) “Do you check social media often when you should be studying, and does it make you feel guilty?” (3) “How many times did you check social media yesterday?” Which would you rewrite first, and why?
Rewrite (1) first — it is leading and assumes an “addiction,” prompting defensive answers; better: “To what extent do you find it hard to limit your phone use?” (2) is double-barrelled (checks frequency and guilt) and should be split into two questions. (3) is the most workable, though “yesterday” still relies on accurate recall.

Interviews & Focus Groups

Guided conversations that capture participants’ meanings in their own words. We follow one research question: “How do teenagers experience examination stress?”

Every participant receives the same questions in the same order. For example:

  1. “How many examinations are you taking this month?”
  2. “On a scale from 1–10, how stressful do you find examination periods?”
  3. “How many hours did you sleep last night?”
  4. “Have you ever missed school because of examination stress?”

Advantage: responses are easier to compare because everyone was asked the same thing.

Limitation: if a student suddenly says “I only become anxious when my parents talk about grades,” the researcher may have little freedom to explore that important idea.

The researcher prepares core questions but can probe important responses.

Researcher:“What is the hardest part of examination season for you?”
Participant:“At night I can’t stop thinking about failing.”
Researcher:“What happens when you start thinking about failing?”
Participant:“I check the grade calculator over and over and then I can’t sleep.”

The follow-up question produces information a rigid checklist could not obtain. The benefit is depth + some consistency.

The researcher begins with a broad topic and lets the conversation develop from what the participant says.

Example opening: “Tell me about what examination season is like for you.”

Participant responses determine the direction. This gives great depth and unexpected insight, but it is much harder to compare participant-to-participant, and the researcher’s influence can be greater.

Imagine the researcher says: “You don’t actually get very anxious about exams, do you?”

This is leading — it pushes the participant toward a particular answer. A better wording: “How, if at all, do examinations affect how you feel?”

Even when wording seems neutral, tone, facial expression and follow-up choices can influence answers — this is the interviewer effect.

A moderator leads a small group (roughly 6–10 people) through open-ended questions while recording the discussion. The key point is that it is not just “people talking” — it is methodologically different from six individual interviews.

Topic: school pressure.

Moderator:“What makes students feel pressure about grades?”
Student A:“Parents.”
Student B:“For me it’s not parents — it’s everyone comparing scores.”
Student C:“Yeah, once someone asks what you got, everyone starts talking about rankings.”

The important data are not only three individual answers. Participants build on and challenge one another. That interaction can reveal shared norms, common language, disagreement, and ideas prompted by other participants — a main reason for using a focus group.

One confident student says: “Nobody actually gets stressed about grades.” Three quieter students nod even though they privately disagree.

Conformity / dominant participant effects can distort what appears to be a group consensus. A skilled moderator might say: “I’d like to hear from someone who has a different experience.”

Limitations also include confidentiality — researchers cannot fully protect what participants reveal about each other afterwards — and limited generalisation from small, non-random groups.

Types and Levels of Data

Four levels of measurement — from least to most precise.

Nominal data place people, responses, or observations into separate categories. The categories do NOT have a meaningful numerical order.

Psychology example: A psychologist asks 100 students which coping strategy they use most often before an important exam. The results are:

  • Exercise — 32 students
  • Talk to someone — 27 students
  • Listen to music — 24 students
  • Other — 17 students

Why these are nominal: The responses are different categories. We can count how many students are in each category. But the categories cannot meaningfully be ranked from lowest to highest. “Exercise” is not greater than “music,” and “talk to someone” is not twice as much as “other.”

If numbers such as 1, 2, 3 and 4 were entered into a spreadsheet to represent these categories, those numbers would only be labels. They would not represent quantities.

What we can do with these data: Count frequencies and identify the most common category.

What we cannot do: Treat the category labels as meaningful numerical scores.

Memory rule: Nominal = NAME the category.

Ordinal data can be placed in a meaningful order, but the gaps between positions are not necessarily equal.

Psychology example: A psychologist asks students “How stressed do you feel before your psychology exam?” using this scale:

  • 1 = Not stressed
  • 2 = Slightly stressed
  • 3 = Moderately stressed
  • 4 = Very stressed
  • 5 = Extremely stressed

Example responses: Student A — 1 · Student B — 2 · Student C — 4 · Student D — 5.

Why these are ordinal: A score of 5 represents more reported stress than a score of 4, and a score of 4 represents more reported stress than a score of 2. The responses clearly have an order.

However, we cannot assume that the psychological difference between 1 and 2 is exactly the same size as the difference between 4 and 5. The numbers tell us the order of the responses, but not necessarily equal psychological distances between them.

Be very careful: 5 is higher than 1. But that does NOT mean the student selecting 5 is “five times as stressed” as the student selecting 1.

What we can tell: Which responses indicate more or less reported stress.

What we cannot safely assume: That every one-point gap represents exactly the same increase in psychological stress.

Memory rule: Ordinal = ORDER matters, but the gaps may not be equal.

Interval data are numerical scores where equal differences between numbers are treated as representing equal differences on the scale. However, the scale does NOT have a meaningful true zero representing the complete absence of the thing being measured.

Psychology example: IQ scores.

  • Person A — IQ 90
  • Person B — IQ 100
  • Person C — IQ 110

Why these are interval: The difference between 90 and 100 is 10 IQ points, and the difference between 100 and 110 is also 10 IQ points. On an interval scale, those equal 10-point differences are treated as equal intervals on the scale. This is different from ordinal data, where we know the order but cannot assume that the gaps between positions are equal.

The zero issue: IQ does not have a meaningful true zero. An IQ score of 0 would not mean “this person has absolutely no intelligence.” Therefore ratios are not meaningful: someone with an IQ score of 120 cannot be described as “twice as intelligent” as someone with an IQ score of 60.

A familiar second illustration — temperature in Celsius: 20°C → 25°C is an increase of 5°C, and 25°C → 30°C is also an increase of 5°C. The intervals are equal. But 0°C does NOT mean that temperature is completely absent. Therefore 20°C is not “twice as hot” as 10°C.

Ordinal vs Interval:

  • Ordinal: We know the order. We cannot assume equal gaps.
  • Interval: We know the order. The gaps are treated as equal. But there is no true zero.

Memory rule: Interval = equal INTERVALS, but no true zero.

Ratio data have all the properties of interval data (order and equal intervals), but they ALSO have a meaningful true zero — zero genuinely represents the absence of the measured quantity.

Psychology example 1 — reaction time in milliseconds: Participant A — 300 ms · Participant B — 600 ms · Participant C — 900 ms.

Why this is ratio: The numerical differences are meaningful: 600 ms − 300 ms = 300 ms, and 900 ms − 600 ms = 300 ms. The scale also has a meaningful zero: 0 milliseconds would represent no elapsed reaction time. Because ratio data have a true zero, ratio comparisons are mathematically meaningful — 600 ms really is twice as long as 300 ms.

Psychology example 2 — number of errors on a memory task: Participant A — 0 errors · Participant B — 2 errors · Participant C — 4 errors. Here, 0 genuinely means NO errors were made — that is what makes the zero meaningful.

What makes Ratio different from Interval?

  • Interval: Equal gaps + no true zero.
  • Ratio: Equal gaps + true zero.

Memory rule: Ratio = equal intervals + REAL zero.

The level of measurement affects which summaries, comparisons, graphs, and statistical analyses are meaningful for the data.

Measurement decisions shape the whole chain of evidence:

  • Construct: what is being studied — for example, stress.
  • Operationalisation: how the construct is turned into something measurable — for example, a questionnaire with low/medium/high categories.
  • Data produced: with that operationalisation, the researcher produces ordinal data.
  • Analysis: which summaries, comparisons, and graphs are meaningful follows from the kind of data produced.
  • Conclusions: what can be claimed is constrained by everything above.

A different operationalisation of the same construct could produce a different form of data. Measurement decisions therefore affect the evidence researchers can produce.

Descriptive Statistics

Raw data (a page full of scores) is hard to read. Descriptive statistics organise and summarise a dataset so the pattern becomes visible — before any claim is made beyond it.

Imagine a psychologist has 30 memory-test scores. A page full of numbers is difficult to understand. Descriptive statistics turn those raw scores into a summary humans can quickly read and interpret.

RAW DATA → ORGANISE IT → SUMMARISE IT → MAKE THE PATTERN VISIBLE.

Descriptive statistics describe what the collected data look like. They tell us “what did our data look like?” — they do not yet tell us whether the result would probably exist in the wider population (that is inferential statistics).

A psychologist tests memory after two study conditions. Scores = number of words recalled.

Mean: add the scores and divide by the number of scores. It uses every score, and is useful when extreme scores are not badly distorting it.

Median: order the scores and take the middle value. It is useful when extreme scores would pull the mean around.

Mode: the most common score/category. It is useful for the most frequent value, and can also be used with nominal categories (e.g. “the most common eye colour”).

Outlier example: memory scores 8, 9, 9, 10, 10, 11, 12, 30. Does 30 pull the mean upward? Yes. Would the median better represent a typical participant? Yes — that is why we report it for skewed data.

  • Low SD: scores cluster relatively close to the mean.
  • High SD: scores are more spread out.

In psychology: two treatment groups can have the same mean anxiety score but very different consistency of responses.

Descriptive statistics also include ways of organising and displaying data: a frequency table, bar chart, histogram, or a scattergram where appropriate. Not every graph suits every kind of data.

The central message: descriptive statistics tell us “what did our data look like?” They do not yet tell us “would this result probably exist in the wider population?” That transition leads to inferential statistics.

A psychologist reports for her sample: mean anxiety = 14.2, median = 12, SD = 8.1. What does each number tell you — and can these descriptive statistics alone tell you that the treatment works in the wider population?
The mean (14.2) is the average score; the median (12) is the middle score/participant; the SD (8.1) shows scores vary substantially around the average. These describe the sample only — no, they cannot tell us the treatment works in the wider population. That requires inferential reasoning.

Correlation & Graphical Patterns

Reading the strength and direction of relationships from coefficients and scattergrams — asking “when one variable changes, what tends to happen to the other?”

A correlation asks a simple question: when one measured variable changes, what tends to happen to the other?

Neither variable is necessarily an IV or DV — they are co-variables. We are not manipulating anything; we are observing how two measured variables move together.

A researcher measures sleep the night before and reaction time (ms) on a reaction-time task:

As sleep increases, what happens to reaction time? Reaction time tends to decrease. Because one variable goes up while the other tends to go down, this is a negative correlation.

  • Positive: both rise together — revision hours and exam scores.
  • Negative: one rises as the other falls — sleep and reaction time.
  • Zero / very weak: no clear linear pattern — hair length and memory-test performance.

Important: negative does not mean “bad,” and positive does not mean “good.” These words only describe direction.

If knowing someone’s value on variable A tells you little about variable B, the linear correlation will be near zero. Say “no clear linear relationship”, not automatically “no relationship of any kind” — there could be a non-linear pattern.

Correlational results are plotted on a scattergram. Each dot = one participant/case; the x-axis is one co-variable and the y-axis is the other.

When reading one, ask four questions:

  1. Which direction do the points move?
  2. How tightly do they follow that pattern?
  3. Are there obvious outliers?
  4. Is the pattern approximately linear?

The correlation coefficient runs from -1 to +1:

  • r = +0.15 — weak positive linear association.
  • r = -0.72 — relatively strong negative linear association.
  • r = +0.93 — very strong positive linear association.

The sign (+/-) tells you the direction; the size (absolute value) tells you the strength.

So r = -0.80 is a stronger relationship than r = +0.30, even though -0.80 is numerically “smaller.” Strength depends on how close |r| is to 1.

Suppose students with more screen time report more anxiety. There are several possible explanations:

  1. More screen time increases anxiety.
  2. More anxious students use screens more.
  3. A third variable — such as loneliness or poor sleep — affects both.

A correlation alone does not tell us which causal explanation is correct. This is why researchers must design studies carefully before drawing causal conclusions.

One extreme participant can strongly affect a correlation. Students should therefore inspect the graph rather than blindly trusting r — an outlier can pull the coefficient in a misleading direction.

A study reports r = -0.68 between sleep duration and anxiety. Write ONE careful sentence interpreting this result. Then answer: can we say sleep caused the lower anxiety?
First: “There is a moderately/relatively strong negative association: students who sleep more tend to report lower anxiety.” Second: No — correlation alone cannot establish causation. Directionality (less anxious students may sleep better) and a possible third variable both remain possible.

Inferential Statistics & Statistical Significance

Descriptive statistics answer “What happened in the people we actually studied?” Inferential statistics answer “What might this sample tell us about the wider population?”

Example: a psychologist gives 20 students mindfulness training, and their mean anxiety score is lower than the control group’s. Descriptive statistics can tell us: “In this sample, the mindfulness group scored 5 points lower.”

But samples naturally vary. If we recruited another 40 students tomorrow, the difference would probably not be exactly 5 points. Inferential statistics help us judge whether the observed pattern is sufficiently convincing to make a cautious inference beyond this particular sample.

The reasoning chain:

  1. Population — the wider group of interest.
  2. Take a sample.
  3. Observe a difference / relationship.
  4. Ask whether that pattern could plausibly arise through sampling variation under the null model.
  5. Make a cautious inference.

Follow one continuous example through every idea on this page. A psychologist investigates whether a short mindfulness programme reduces examination anxiety.

40 students participate. 20 complete the mindfulness programme; 20 are in a comparison condition. Afterwards, anxiety is measured using the same anxiety scale.

Suppose the mindfulness group mean anxiety score = 18 and the comparison group mean anxiety score = 23.

1. The sample result describes only this sample. The mindfulness group scored 5 points lower on average. But that alone does not tell us whether a similar difference would probably exist in the wider population — that is why inferential statistics are needed.

2. The null hypothesis. There is no genuine difference in anxiety between the mindfulness and comparison conditions in the population; any difference in this sample is due to sampling variation/chance. The statistical test temporarily works from this assumption.

3. The p-value. Suppose the appropriate test gives p = .03. This does not mean “there is a 3% chance that the null hypothesis is true.” Instead: if the null hypothesis were true, a result this extreme or more extreme would occur about 3% of the time under the statistical model. Because .03 < .05, the result crosses the conventional significance threshold and is described as statistically significant.

4. What can we conclude?

  • Supported: “The mindfulness and comparison groups differed statistically on anxiety scores.”
  • Not supported: “Mindfulness definitely works”; “Mindfulness has a large effect”; “There is a 97% probability that mindfulness works”; “The result will replicate.”

5. Statistical significance is ONE piece of evidence. Researchers must still consider effect size, the sample, measurement, design, possible confounds, replication, and practical importance.

The p-value is the probability of obtaining results this extreme, or more extreme, if the null hypothesis were true.

A plain-language way to ask it: “If there really were no population effect, would a result this extreme be unusual?” For example:

  • p = .40 — not particularly unusual under the null model.
  • p = .03 — relatively unusual under the null model.

Researchers commonly use p < .05 as a significance threshold. If the p-value falls below it, the observed result would be relatively unlikely under the null hypothesis, so the result is described as statistically significant.

Two things NOT to say: “there is a 3% chance the result happened by chance,” and “there is a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true.” Neither is what the p-value means.

Statistically significant does not mean important, large, or definitely true — it is a comment on the role of chance, not a verdict on the result.

Inferential statistics ask whether results from the sample probably hold in the population — or whether they could easily be due to chance alone.

The null hypothesis proposes that there is no genuine difference or relationship in the population. Tests start from that assumption: if the null hypothesis were true, how surprising would results as extreme as ours be?

A result can be statistically significant yet trivially small, especially with huge samples. That is why researchers report effect size alongside significance.

Statistical significance asks: Is the observed pattern difficult to explain as sampling variation under the null model? Effect size asks: How large is the difference or relationship?

Using the mindfulness example: A statistically significant difference could still be tiny. With a very large sample, even a very small difference may reach statistical significance. So finding p = .03 does not by itself tell us how big the anxiety reduction was in practical terms — effect size tells us that. (You do not need to learn effect-size formulas.)

A concrete illustration: a study of 50,000 participants finds an intervention improves a wellbeing score by an average of 0.2 points with p < .001. Statistically significant? Potentially yes. Big or practically important? Not necessarily. Significance and importance are different questions.

Significance is decided on probabilities, so mistakes are possible:

  • Type I error: rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true — a false positive.
  • Type II error: failing to reject the null hypothesis when a real effect actually exists — a false negative.

Applied to the mindfulness example:

  • Type I (false positive): the researcher concludes there is evidence of a mindfulness effect when in reality there is no genuine effect.
  • Type II (false negative): the researcher fails to detect a mindfulness effect even though a genuine effect exists.

Memory aid: Type I = finding an effect that isn’t really there. Type II = missing an effect that really is there.

Whatever the details of a test, the reasoning always follows the same arc:

  1. Sample result — the pattern observed in the data.
  2. Null hypothesis — what the population would look like if there were no effect.
  3. p-value — how likely such an extreme result would be under the null hypothesis.
  4. Statistical decision — significant or not, relative to the chosen threshold.
  5. Effect size / practical importance — how large the effect actually is.
  6. Cautious conclusion — interpret the result in light of the study’s limitations.

Choosing and Interpreting Statistical Tests

Matching the analysis to the research question, design, and type of data.

Difference questions compare conditions or groups (does therapy reduce anxiety more than no therapy?). Relationship questions measure association between two co-variables (do screen time and sleep relate?).

For difference questions:

  • Unrelated / independent groups: different people in each group.
  • Related / repeated measures: the same people tested more than once.
  • Matched pairs: different people deliberately matched on relevant characteristics.

Think about the level of measurement produced (as covered on the Types and Levels of Data page):

  • Nominal: categories with no natural order (e.g., pass/fail, gender).
  • Ordinal: ordered data where the intervals between values are not necessarily equal (e.g., ranked ratings).
  • Interval / ratio: continuous amounts with equal intervals between values (ratio adds a true zero).

A small set of common tests is the starting point — these are tools to reason with, not a table to memorise. For each test, read what it does in plain English and see a psychology example. You do not need to calculate these tests — only choose between them and interpret them.

  • Pearson correlation — examines a linear relationship between two appropriate continuous variables. Example: Is number of hours slept related to reaction time? Both variables are measured.
  • Spearman correlation — examines a relationship when the data are ranks/ordinal. Example: Is students’ rank for academic stress associated with their rank for sleep quality?
  • Unrelated / independent t-test — compares the means of two separate groups when appropriate continuous data and assumptions are met. Example: Do students receiving a mindfulness programme have different mean anxiety scores from students in a separate control group?
  • Related / paired t-test — compares two related sets of appropriate continuous scores, often from the same participants measured twice. Example: Are mean anxiety scores different in the same participants before and after therapy?
  • Mann-Whitney U — a common non-parametric test for comparing two separate groups when the outcome is ordinal/ranked or relevant parametric assumptions are not met. Example: Two separate groups give ordinal ratings of examination stress.
  • Wilcoxon signed-rank — a common non-parametric test for two related sets of ordinal/ranked scores. Example: The same students rate their stress before and after a relaxation exercise.
  • Chi-square — used with frequency/count data in categories to examine whether observed category patterns/associations differ from what would be expected under the null hypothesis. Example: Researchers examine whether preferred coping strategy category is associated with year group.

Choosing a test means matching the analysis to the question (difference or relationship), the design (related or unrelated), and the type of data.

"Does caffeine improve recall?" Two independent groups (caffeine vs placebo), recall measured as number of words (ratio data): use an unrelated t-test. If instead participants only ranked their confidence (ordinal data): Mann-Whitney U. If the outcome were simply pass/fail (nominal): chi-squared.

  • Comparing two diets with weight loss in kg (ratio, two independent groups) → unrelated t-test.
  • Testing whether gender is associated with subject choice (both nominal) → chi-squared.
  • Same participants rate stress before and after mindfulness (ordinal ratings) → Wilcoxon.
Participants rank their preference for two brands of cola, and the same tasters rate both brands. What analysis approach fits?
Ranking data are ordinal, and the same tasters provide rankings for both brands, so this is a related (repeated-measures) design with two conditions. That combination rules out t-tests and Pearson r, which require interval or ratio measurements. With two related conditions and ordinal data, a Wilcoxon test is a common non-parametric choice.
Two separate groups rate satisfaction on a 1–10 ordinal scale. Which test compares them?
An unrelated design with ordinal data calls for the Mann-Whitney U test. An unrelated t-test would require interval/ratio data with a roughly normal distribution.

Working With Qualitative Data

What qualitative data actually look like, and where they come from.

Qualitative data usually consist of words, descriptions, observations, documents, images, or other material that captures experiences, meanings and behaviour in context. Instead of a number in a spreadsheet, the researcher works with what people actually said or did.

The pages below use concrete psychology examples so you can see what real qualitative data look like.

Scenario: A psychologist interviews teenagers about examination stress.

Example transcript:

Researcher: “What is the hardest part of exam season for you?”

Participant: “I can’t switch off at night. Even when I’ve finished studying, I keep thinking about everything I might forget the next day.”

Why this matters: The data are not simply “exam stress.” The actual qualitative data include the participant’s words and the meanings expressed in them. A researcher could look for patterns such as difficulty switching off, worry about forgetting, sleep disruption, and the pressure surrounding performance.

Example:

Student A: “Everyone talks about grades constantly.”

Student B: “Yeah, and when someone says they studied six hours, you feel guilty if you didn’t.”

Student C: “Exactly. It makes it feel competitive.”

A focus group gives researchers individual comments and interaction between participants. Students may agree, disagree, build upon ideas, or influence one another — and that interaction can itself be informative.

Example field note:

“During the 20-minute playground observation, Student A approached three different groups but remained with each for less than one minute. The student spent approximately eight minutes alone.”

Qualitative observation can involve detailed field notes describing behaviour and context. More structured observations may also code behaviour into categories and produce quantitative frequency data — showing that qualitative and quantitative material can be closely linked.

Example: A psychologist studies diary entries written by university students during their first semester away from home. An entry might say:

“I thought moving away would make me feel independent, but some evenings I feel completely disconnected from everyone I know.”

A researcher could investigate feelings and processes such as loneliness, identity, adjustment, independence, and social connection.

Keeping the existing point in mind: Diaries, letters, medical records, and media provide naturally occurring qualitative data — valuable because they were not created for the researcher. Availability and authenticity must still be checked.

Qualitative studies often use relatively small, purposively selected samples when researchers want detailed understanding from people with relevant experience. Purposive sampling means participants are deliberately selected because they have characteristics or experiences relevant to the research question. The goal is often depth and contextual understanding rather than statistical representativeness — so findings are judged by their richness and transferability rather than by statistical generalisation.

Concrete example: For the research question “How do first-generation university students experience the transition to university?”, a researcher deliberately recruits students who ARE first-generation university students. That is purposive sampling, because participants are selected for having experience directly relevant to the research question — the aim is depth and relevance, not creating a statistically representative random sample.

Why do qualitative researchers use purposive samples?
They want detailed understanding from participants whose characteristics or experiences are relevant to the research question, so participants are deliberately selected rather than randomly recruited. The goal is often depth and contextual understanding, with findings judged by richness and transferability rather than statistical representativeness.

Qualitative Analysis

Turning transcripts and field notes into systematic findings.

The most widely used approach (Braun & Clarke, 2006 — a useful source, though not one you need to memorise as a study). Steps:

  1. Familiarise yourself with the data.
  2. Generate initial codes.
  3. Search for themes among codes.
  4. Review themes against the data.
  5. Define and name themes.
  6. Write up, supporting the themes with vivid examples from the data.

Content analysis systematically codes material into categories. Researchers may count the frequency of categories, producing quantitative summaries, although content analysis can also involve interpretation of the content. For example, researchers might code every act of violence in children’s TV programmes into a set of categories and count how often each occurs.

Strength: systematic and replicable. Watch for: categories that oversimplify meaning.

Whatever the approach, analysis moves through the same arc:

  1. Raw qualitative data — transcripts, field notes, documents.
  2. Coding — labelling meaningful chunks of the data.
  3. Patterns — noticing what recurs or relates.
  4. Themes or categories — grouping patterns into broader ideas.
  5. Interpretation — reading meaning into the patterns.
  6. Evidence from the data — supporting the interpretation with quotations or examples.

Research question: “How do high-school students experience examination stress?”

RAW INTERVIEW EXTRACTS:

  • “I lie awake thinking about everything I still need to revise.” (Participant A)
  • “Even when I stop studying, my brain keeps going through what might come up.” (Participant B)
  • “I’ve been sleeping badly because I keep worrying that I haven’t done enough.” (Participant C)

STEP 1 — RAW DATA: Students read the actual participant statements.

STEP 2 — INITIAL CODES: A code is a short label attached to a meaningful part of the data. Possible codes here: difficulty sleeping, repetitive exam thoughts, worry about preparation, inability to switch off.

STEP 3 — LOOK FOR PATTERNS: Several codes relate to students being unable to mentally disengage from examination concerns.

STEP 4 — DEVELOP A THEME: A theme is broader than a single code — it captures an important recurring pattern of meaning across the data. A possible theme: “Exam stress follows students beyond study time.”

STEP 5 — INTERPRET: Students’ examination stress appears not to be confined to periods of active revision. Persistent thoughts about preparation may intrude into rest and sleep.

STEP 6 — SUPPORT WITH DATA: Return to the participant quotations as evidence supporting the interpretation.

The central sequence to remember:

  1. RAW QUOTE
  2. → CODE
  3. → PATTERN
  4. → THEME
  5. → INTERPRETATION
  6. → EVIDENCE

Use the same general topic to show the difference from thematic analysis. Imagine researchers analyse 100 student diary entries and code references to categories such as: sleep problems, worry, physical symptoms, avoidance, and social pressure. They can then count how frequently each category occurs.

Content analysis can therefore turn qualitative material into systematic categories and sometimes into numerical frequency summaries.

Compare the two:

  • Thematic analysis asks: “What patterns of meaning appear in these accounts?”
  • Content analysis asks: “What categories occur in the material, and potentially how frequently?”

Note that not all content analysis is purely quantitative — it can also involve interpretation of the content.

What is the key difference between thematic and content analysis?
Thematic analysis interprets patterns of meaning (themes). Content analysis systematically codes material into categories and may count category frequencies, which can produce quantitative summaries — but it can also involve interpretation of the content. Thematic analysis asks “what does this mean?”; content analysis often also asks “how often does this occur?”

Credibility in Qualitative Research

How researchers test, challenge and strengthen their interpretation of qualitative data.

In qualitative research, the researcher usually interprets interviews, observations or other rich data. That creates an important question: How do we know the interpretation is not simply the researcher’s own assumption? Credibility strategies help the researcher test, challenge and strengthen their interpretation.

Credibility: the extent to which the researcher’s interpretation is believable, well-supported and grounded in the data.

Credibility does not mean the interpretation has been objectively “proven true.” It means the researcher has taken steps to make the interpretation more defensible.

A researcher interviews 20 secondary-school students about academic stress. The interviews suggest a possible theme:

“Students feel most stressed when they believe they have little control over deadlines and workload.”

We will use this one study to demonstrate all five credibility strategies, so you can compare them directly.

Definition: Participants are asked to check whether the researcher’s interpretation accurately reflects their experience.

What the researcher does: The researcher sends a short summary of the emerging theme to several students and asks: “Does this reflect what you were trying to explain in your interview?”

Why it helps: It reduces the risk that the researcher has misunderstood or distorted what participants meant.

Limitation: Participants do not automatically have the final word on every interpretation, and agreement does not prove the analysis is objectively true.

Definition: The researcher compares more than one source, method, researcher or form of evidence.

What the researcher does: The interview findings are compared with student diary entries, observations of workload during assessment weeks, or data collected by another researcher.

Why it helps: If different sources point toward a similar interpretation, the researcher has stronger grounds for defending it.

Clarification: Triangulation increases confidence. It does not “verify” or “prove” that the conclusion is true.

Definition: The researcher spends substantial or repeated time with participants or in the research setting.

What the researcher does: Instead of interviewing students once for 15 minutes, the researcher returns over several weeks and observes how stress changes during normal weeks and assessment periods.

Why it helps: Longer engagement gives the researcher more context and makes it less likely that the interpretation is based on a temporary or unusual moment.

Limit: More time does not automatically eliminate researcher bias.

Definition: Another knowledgeable researcher critically questions the researcher’s coding, themes and interpretations.

What the researcher does: A colleague reviews anonymised interview extracts and asks: “Why did you code this as lack of control rather than fear of failure?” The original researcher must justify or revise the interpretation.

Why it helps: It prevents one researcher’s assumptions from going unchallenged.

Clarification: The colleague is not simply approving the researcher’s work. Their role is to challenge it.

Definition: The researcher actively searches for cases that contradict the emerging pattern.

What the researcher does: Most students say lack of control increases stress. But three students say strict deadlines actually reduce stress because they provide structure. The researcher must account for these cases instead of ignoring them.

Why it helps: A strong interpretation should explain exceptions or be revised when the data do not fit. This reduces confirmation bias.

  • Member checking: who challenges the interpretation? → participants
  • Triangulation: what is compared? → different sources / methods / researchers
  • Prolonged engagement: what changes? → amount of time and context
  • Peer debriefing: who challenges the interpretation? → another knowledgeable researcher
  • Negative case analysis: what does the researcher actively seek? → contradictory evidence

Students often confuse these five, so keep the central question of each one distinct.

  • After identifying themes, the researcher asks participants whether the summary reflects their experiences. → Member checking
  • Interview findings are compared with observation data. → Triangulation
  • The researcher spends six months embedded in the setting. → Prolonged engagement
  • Another researcher challenges the coding decisions. → Peer debriefing
  • The researcher deliberately searches for participants who contradict the dominant pattern. → Negative case analysis
A researcher interviews 15 students and concludes: “All teenagers experience academic stress because they lack control.” Why is this conclusion weak, and how could credibility be improved?
“All teenagers” overgeneralises from a small qualitative sample. The interpretation may also reflect researcher assumptions. Credibility could be improved by using member checking (checking the interpretation with participants), triangulation (comparing interview data with another source), and actively searching for students whose experiences contradict the theme. These strategies strengthen the interpretation, but they do not make universal generalisation appropriate.
  1. Credibility = how believable and well-supported a qualitative interpretation is.
  2. Member checking = participants check the interpretation.
  3. Triangulation = compare multiple sources, methods or researchers.
  4. Prolonged engagement = spend enough time to understand context.
  5. Peer debriefing = another researcher challenges the interpretation.
  6. Negative case analysis = actively seek contradictory evidence.
  7. These strategies strengthen an interpretation; they do not prove it objectively true.

Ethical Principles in Psychological Research

Modern ethical standards developed partly in response to serious historical abuses in research.

IB Concept — Responsibility: Researchers are responsible for balancing the pursuit of knowledge with the rights, dignity and welfare of participants.

Participants should receive enough relevant information to make a meaningful, voluntary decision about taking part. Complete disclosure may sometimes be impossible — for example when deception is scientifically justified — but that requires careful ethical consideration.

Participants should be able to stop participating at any point without inappropriate pressure or penalty. Where appropriate, researchers should also make clear what happens to a participant’s data if they withdraw.

Researchers should minimise foreseeable physical and psychological harm. Research should not expose participants to unjustified levels of risk or distress.

These are related but not the same thing:

  • Privacy: whether researchers should have access to particular behaviour or personal information at all.
  • Confidentiality: researchers may know who provided the data but protect identifying information from disclosure.
  • Anonymity: the data cannot reasonably be linked to the participant’s identity.

Deception means deliberately withholding or misrepresenting information relevant to the study. It may sometimes be scientifically justified, but should be:

  • necessary,
  • proportionate,
  • ethically reviewed, and
  • unlikely to expose participants to unacceptable harm.

After participation, researchers explain relevant information about the study, especially any deception, answer questions, address misconceptions or distress, and explain the purpose of the research. Debriefing does not make all deception ethical.

In Milgram’s study, participants were ordered to deliver what they believed were dangerous electric shocks. Which ethical principles were at stake, and why?
Milgram is useful to think with because several principles collide: participants could not give fully informed consent because key information (the real purpose, the shocks being fake) was withheld; deception was central to the design; the pressure to obey undermined the right to withdraw without penalty; participants showed visible distress, raising protection from harm; and the study became a landmark debate about whether debriefing can ever justify such deception. For each principle, ask what was at risk and why it matters.

Ethical Decision-Making & Sensitive Research

Ethical decisions are about balancing competing considerations rather than applying simple yes/no rules.

IB Concept — Responsibility: Responsible research means identifying possible harm, anticipating it, minimising it, explaining safeguards, and justifying any remaining risks.

Good ethical decisions weigh competing considerations — for example the value of the knowledge to be gained against the risks and costs to participants. Bad ethics also produce worse science: scandals damage public trust and make future recruitment harder, while transparent, respectful research sustains the voluntary participation psychology depends on.

Research may be especially sensitive when it involves topics or participants where disclosure, questioning or participation could create increased risk of distress, stigma, privacy violations or other harm.

Examples include mental health, trauma, relationships or sexual behaviour, illegal behaviour, discrimination, highly personal experiences, and vulnerable participants.

This does not mean such topics can never be researched. Instead, the responsible question is: What additional safeguards would responsible research require?

Consider the wording of questions, intrusive questions, the possibility of traumatic memories, whether participants can skip questions, stopping procedures, and what support information is available.

Consider confidentiality, anonymity where possible, secure handling of data, and whether the study collects unnecessary identifying information.

Consider age, vulnerability, comprehension, pressure or power relationships, and whether deception is involved. Where deception is unnecessary, a prior general consent approach — participants agreeing in advance to take part in studies of a particular kind — can reduce the need to withhold information.

Confidentiality may have limits in some situations, for example when there are immediate risks to the participant or others. Researchers need clear procedures established before collecting sensitive data, although the specific rules vary between jurisdictions.

Debriefing does not retroactively create informed consent. Debriefing explains the true purpose of the research and any deception, addresses misconceptions or distress, allows participants to ask questions, and may include information about withdrawing their data where appropriate.

Before relying on deception, run a simple decision framework:

  1. Is deception necessary?
  2. Is there a reasonable non-deceptive alternative?
  3. Is the risk acceptable?
  4. Has it received ethical review?
  5. Will participants receive an appropriate debrief?

Responsible research follows a fixed chain:

  1. Identify possible harm.
  2. Anticipate it.
  3. Minimise it.
  4. Explain the safeguards.
  5. Justify any remaining risks.
A researcher plans an anonymous online survey asking adolescents about self-harm. There is no deception. Does this study still need careful ethical planning? Explain.
Yes — even anonymous and deception-free research can be sensitive. Working through the four questions: (1) the wording could cause distress, so skip options and support information matter; (2) although the data are anonymous, secure handling still matters; (3) the participants are minors, so meaningful consent requires attention to age, comprehension and any pressure to take part; (4) if a participant discloses serious risk, confidentiality has limits and procedures must be planned before data collection. The study is not forbidden — but responsible research asks what additional safeguards it requires.

Animal Research in Psychology

Uses, justifications, and safeguards of research with non-human animals.

  • Processes shared across species: basic learning, motivation and behavioural processes are similar enough to be informative.
  • Greater experimental control: genetics and environment can be controlled in ways often impossible with humans.
  • Questions that cannot ethically be manipulated in humans: some procedures could not be carried out with people.

To understand what these ideas mean in practice, read the three real studies below — they show what psychologists actually did and the ethical issues raised.

B. F. Skinner investigated how consequences influence behaviour. In controlled experimental chambers, animals such as rats or pigeons could perform behaviours such as pressing a lever or pecking a key. Researchers could systematically change what happened after the behaviour — for example, whether food was delivered.

Skinner’s research demonstrated how reinforcement can increase the probability of behaviour and contributed substantially to theories of operant conditioning.

Why use animals? The controlled environment allowed researchers to manipulate consequences and repeatedly observe learning under tightly controlled conditions.

Limitation: Human behaviour is influenced by language, culture, beliefs and complex social contexts, so findings from animal learning cannot simply be assumed to explain all human behaviour.

In Seligman & Maier’s learned-helplessness research with dogs, dogs were exposed to situations involving aversive stimulation. Some animals had previously experienced stimulation they could not control or escape. Later, when escape became possible, some of these animals were slower or less likely to attempt escape.

This contributed to the development of the concept of learned helplessness — the idea that experience with uncontrollable negative events can influence later behaviour.

Scientific value: The controlled animal model allowed researchers to manipulate controllability in ways that would be ethically unacceptable with humans.

Ethical problem: The animals were exposed to significant distress and aversive stimulation. This research therefore provides a powerful example of the tension between scientific knowledge and animal welfare.

Important: This experiment did not “prove” human depression. Learned helplessness was later used as a model relevant to understanding aspects of human depression, but animal behaviour is not identical to human clinical depression.

In Harlow’s rhesus-monkey research, infant monkeys were separated from their biological mothers and given access to surrogate “mothers.” One surrogate was typically made from wire and could provide food; another was covered with soft cloth. The infant monkeys spent much more time clinging to the cloth surrogate, including when frightened.

Psychological importance: The findings challenged the simple idea that attachment develops only because a caregiver provides food. They suggested that contact comfort was an important component of attachment.

Ethical issue: The research involved maternal separation and procedures that caused serious distress and abnormal behaviour. It therefore became an important example in debates about whether the scientific value of animal research can justify the welfare costs.

  • Animal suffering and welfare must be taken seriously.
  • Limits to generalising from animals to humans — animal findings do not automatically transfer.
  • Scientific value must justify costs: it must be weighed against animal welfare, the availability of alternatives, and how far animal findings can reasonably inform understanding of humans.
  • Alternatives should be considered before animals are used.
  • Replacement: use non-animal alternatives where possible.
  • Reduction: use no more animals than necessary to answer the research question reliably.
  • Refinement: modify procedures to minimise pain, suffering and distress and improve welfare.

Animal research is typically subject to ethical review and relevant legal or regulatory requirements, although specific requirements vary between jurisdictions.

Applying the 3Rs to real studies: The 3Rs are not descriptions of what historical studies like those above necessarily did. They are a framework students can use to evaluate how animal research should be designed and justified:

  • Replacement: Could the research question be answered without animals?
  • Reduction: Could the necessary question be answered with fewer animals?
  • Refinement: Could the procedure be changed to reduce pain, distress or suffering?

Designing a Study

A chain of connected decisions, from research question to justified conclusion.

Start with a focused question: What exactly do I want to find out? The question should determine the method — not the other way around.

Decide what kind of evidence answers the question:

  • Want to test whether changing X affects Y? → an experiment may be appropriate.
  • Want to examine whether two measured variables are associated? → a correlation may be appropriate.
  • Want detailed accounts of experiences or meanings? → an interview or focus group may be appropriate.
  • Want to investigate behaviour in context? → an observation may be appropriate.
  • Want standardised self-report data from many participants? → a survey or questionnaire may be appropriate.

Ask: Who is the target population? Who will actually participate? How will they be sampled? Could the sampling method introduce bias? Is the proposed sample appropriate for the research question?

Not every sample must be statistically representative — for some questions, purposive or small samples are appropriate. What matters is that the sample fits the question and that its limits are recognised.

Define the focus and the data-collection tool, depending on the method:

  • Experiment: the IV, the DV, and operational definitions.
  • Correlation: the co-variables and how each will be measured.
  • Interview / focus group: the phenomenon or topic and the interview questions/prompts.
  • Observation: the behaviour, with operational definitions or coding categories where relevant.
  • Survey: the construct and the questionnaire items/scales that measure it.

Ask: What exactly will participants do? Is the procedure clear enough to follow? What needs to be standardised? Could participant or researcher effects influence the results? Are there plausible confounds? What controls are appropriate?

For experiments only, also consider where relevant: experimental design, random assignment, and control of confounding variables. These do not apply to every research method.

Ask: What ethical issues could arise from THIS specific design, and how will they be addressed? Specify the safeguards for the design — rather than merely listing “consent, confidentiality, debrief” without application.

Ask: What kind of data will this design produce, and how could those data answer the research question? This might mean descriptive or inferential quantitative analysis, correlations, or coding/themes for qualitative data — nothing more advanced than the design requires.

Ask: What conclusions would this design actually allow? An experiment may provide stronger causal evidence if appropriately designed; a correlation establishes association, not causation; qualitative research may provide detailed contextual understanding; and the sampling method affects how far findings can be generalised or transferred.

Good research design is a chain of connected decisions: question → method → measurement → procedure → data → conclusion. A weakness anywhere in that chain can affect what the research allows us to conclude.

What should come first: the method or the research question?
The research question — it determines which variables, design, sampling, and analysis are appropriate. Choosing a method before clarifying the question often forces the wrong fit.

Evaluating an Unseen Study

Using the six IB concepts and evidence from the study itself to judge unfamiliar research.

  1. Read the study.
  2. Identify the issues that actually matter.
  3. Connect those issues to relevant IB concepts.
  4. Use specific evidence from the study.
  5. Explain how the issue affects the findings or conclusions.
  6. Reach a reasoned judgement.

Do not mechanically discuss all six concepts every time — only the ones relevant to the issues that actually matter in the study.

This is a fictional study presented as a teaching exercise to show how the framework works — not a study to memorise.

Study scenario. A psychologist investigates whether listening to instrumental music while studying improves memory. The researcher recruits 40 volunteers from one school. Students choose whether they want to study: Group A with instrumental music; Group B in silence. Both groups study the same list of 30 words for 10 minutes. The music group studies in a quiet classroom in the morning; the silence group studies in a classroom beside construction work in the afternoon. Students then complete the same recall test, and the music group recalls more words. The researcher concludes: “Listening to instrumental music causes better memory.”

Step 1 — Don’t just hunt for vocabulary. Ask: What are the biggest problems with this study? Students should identify issues such as volunteer sampling, self-selection into conditions, lack of random assignment, time of day differing systematically between conditions, environmental noise differing systematically between conditions, alternative explanations/confounding, operationalisation of memory, limits to generalisation, and causal overclaiming. We do not need to force all six IB concepts into the answer — we identify the issues that genuinely matter.

Bias. Evidence: participants were volunteers from one school. This may create sampling bias: students willing to participate may differ from students who do not volunteer, and a single-school sample may poorly represent the wider population in other schools, cultures or contexts. Consequence: generalisability is limited.

Causality. Evidence: students chose which condition to join. There was no random assignment. The music group also studied in a quiet classroom in the morning, while the silence group studied beside construction work in the afternoon — so time of day and environmental noise also differed systematically between the conditions. These differences are confounds that provide alternative explanations. The finding is consistent with instrumental music improving recall, but this design provides weaker evidence for the strong claim that music caused better memory.

Measurement. Evidence: memory was operationalised as the number of words recalled on one test. This is a concrete, easy-to-score measure, but a single recall test captures only one aspect of memory, and recall might be affected by the differing study conditions (time of day and noise) as well as by the music itself. A stronger study might use a validated memory measure and would need to control the study environment.

Responsibility. Nothing in the short scenario automatically demonstrates a major ethical violation. Students should not invent ethical problems simply because Responsibility is one of the six concepts. However, researchers would still need appropriate informed consent, privacy/confidentiality, and safeguards appropriate to student participants; if participants are minors, relevant consent requirements must be considered.

Perspective. The researcher interprets the difference as an effect of instrumental music. But another interpretation is possible: perhaps the groups differed because of the time of day and noise levels where they studied, or because students who chose music were already different in some way. This demonstrates why considering alternative perspectives/explanations can improve evaluation.

Change. Do not force this concept. The study is a snapshot after a single study session, and the limited design does not necessarily establish how memory performance changes over longer periods. A design that tests recall again over time could answer a different question about whether a pattern persists.

Final judgement. “The study provides some evidence that students who studied with instrumental music recalled more words. However, self-selection into conditions and systematic differences in time of day and noise between the groups create alternative explanations, the volunteer sample from one school limits generalisability, and memory was measured using a single recall test. The findings therefore do not justify the strong conclusion that listening to instrumental music causes better memory.”

Notice the structure of each point: Evidence from study → Identify the issue → Connect to a relevant concept → Explain why it matters → Limit the conclusion → Reach a judgement.

This worked example is not a checklist to memorise. Its purpose is to model the thinking process. A strong evaluation does not say “Bias, Causality, Measurement, Responsibility, Perspective, Change…” and then mechanically write something about every concept. A strong evaluation identifies the most important methodological issues in the actual study and explains their consequences.

Ask: What could systematically distort the evidence?

Examples: sampling bias, researcher bias, participant expectations, social desirability, culturally biased measures.

Ask: Does the design justify the causal claim?

Consider manipulation, temporal order, confounding variables, alternative explanations, and random assignment or control where relevant.

Ask: How were the psychological constructs turned into measurable variables or data?

Consider operationalisation, reliability, validity, self-report limitations, and whether the measure actually captures the intended construct.

Ask: Were the participants and the research process handled responsibly?

Consider informed consent, harm, privacy, confidentiality, deception, debriefing, and vulnerable participants.

Ask: Could the evidence or interpretation look different from another perspective?

This may involve a methodological perspective, a cultural or contextual perspective, alternative psychological explanations, or participant versus researcher interpretation.

Use this concept when it is actually relevant. Ask: Does the design capture change over time, or is it only a snapshot?

Possible considerations: longitudinal versus cross-sectional evidence, development, intervention or treatment change, and whether observed differences really demonstrate change. Do not force Change into every evaluation.

Structure each point as: issue → evidence from the study → concept → consequence.

“The researchers used an opportunity sample of 40 students from one international school. This creates a potential bias because the sample may differ systematically from the wider adolescent population. Therefore, the findings may not generalise well beyond students with similar educational and cultural backgrounds.”

“Stress was measured using one self-report question. This creates a measurement problem because a single item may not capture the complexity of the construct. Therefore, the conclusion about participants’ stress levels should be treated cautiously.”

Two or three developed points beat five undeveloped labels.

An unseen study reports a correlation between hours of social media use and anxiety in teenagers, measured with one self-report question at a single time point. Which concepts apply to the issues that actually matter?
Measurement — a single self-report item may not capture the construct, so conclusions about “anxiety” should be cautious. Causality — the design is correlational and cross-sectional, so it supports association, not causation; direction and third variables remain possible. Perspective — a single measure from one sample might look different with other measures or cultural groups. Responsibility — data from teenagers raises consent, privacy, and support questions. Change is not really relevant here because the design is a snapshot, so you would not force it in. Reach a reasoned judgement about how far the findings and conclusions should be trusted.

Data Interpretation & Research Conclusions

Turning results into defensible conclusions.

  1. Evidence
  2. Pattern
  3. Analysis
  4. Design
  5. Alternative explanations
  6. Scope
  7. Conclusion

Ask: what pattern is actually present in the evidence?

  • Quantitative evidence: differences, relationships, central tendency, spread, and visible patterns in the data.
  • Qualitative evidence: recurring themes, patterns, similarities and differences, and relevant participant extracts or observations.

Ask: what analysis supports the interpretation?

  • Quantitative data may be supported by descriptive statistics, correlations, statistical significance where relevant, and effect size or practical importance where relevant.
  • Qualitative data may be supported by coding, categories, themes, and patterns backed by evidence from the data.

Not every study requires a significance test — the analysis should fit the data and the design.

Ask: what does the research design actually allow us to conclude?

  • Experiment versus correlation — was anything manipulated and controlled?
  • Qualitative versus quantitative evidence — what kind of claim does each support?
  • Manipulation and control, and the possible confounds that remain.
  • Alternative explanations that the design cannot rule out.

Ask: how far can the conclusion reasonably extend?

  • Sample and population: who was studied?
  • Context, culture, and setting: where and under what conditions?
  • Transferability and generalisability: which wider claim is justified?
  • Claiming causation from a correlation.
  • Equating statistical significance with practical importance.
  • Generalising beyond the sample (e.g. WEIRD participants).
  • Ignoring alternative explanations such as confounds.
  • Letting expectations (researcher bias) steer interpretation.

A strong conclusion says no more — and no less — than the evidence and research design justify.

A study reports a statistically significant difference with a very large sample. What must the researcher check before concluding?
Whether the effect is large enough to matter (effect size and practical importance) — huge samples can make trivial differences significant. They should also confirm the design supports the claim and that the sample justifies generalisation.

Research Methods Exam Skills

The core research-methods reasoning skill: identify the methodological feature, apply it to the actual evidence, and explain the consequence.

METHODOLOGICAL POINT → EVIDENCE FROM THE STUDY → CONSEQUENCE FOR THE EVIDENCE OR CONCLUSION

  1. Identify the methodological feature. This might be a research method, experimental design, sampling method, variable, operationalisation, measurement issue, bias, confound, ethical issue, statistical or data pattern, or a qualitative feature. Identification alone is not enough.
  2. Apply it to the specific evidence. Point to concrete information from the study, stimulus, source, or scenario. Avoid generic claims like “opportunity sampling causes bias”; instead write “the researchers recruited 40 volunteers from one international school...”
  3. Explain the consequence. Why does this feature matter for the evidence or conclusion? Consider weaker or stronger causal inference, measurement validity, reliability or consistency, sampling bias, generalisability, participant behaviour, researcher influence, interpretation of findings, ethical responsibility, and confidence in the conclusion.

Worked example: “The researchers used an opportunity sample of students from one school. Because these students may differ systematically from the wider adolescent population, sampling bias may limit how confidently the findings can be generalised.”

Where relevant, connect research-methods reasoning to the six concepts. Choose the concepts that genuinely help you analyse the evidence — do not force all six into every answer.

  • Bias: could something systematically distort the evidence?
  • Causality: does the design justify a causal conclusion?
  • Measurement: does the operationalisation actually capture the intended construct?
  • Responsibility: were participants treated responsibly and ethically?
  • Perspective: could the evidence or interpretation look different from another methodological, cultural, or psychological perspective?
  • Change: does the evidence genuinely demonstrate change over time, or only differences at one point?

Research-methods reasoning supports the 2027 HL assessment:

  • Paper 1: Apply psychological knowledge, concepts, contexts, research methodology and ethical reasoning where relevant to the questions.
  • Paper 2: Apply understanding of research methodology through the class practicals, including explaining, comparing and designing research using the methods studied.
  • Paper 3: Analyse and interpret quantitative and qualitative research material and reason carefully from the evidence provided.
  • IA: Make and justify research-design decisions in the research proposal.
  • Confusing correlation with causation when evaluating studies.
  • Vague operational definitions (“measured aggression”) instead of concrete ones.
  • Mixing up random assignment and random sampling.
  • Listing limitations without explaining how they affect conclusions.
  • Naming a methodological issue without applying it to the actual study.
  • Assuming statistical significance means the effect is important.
  • Claiming a study is “invalid” because it has one limitation.
  • Forcing every IB concept into every answer.
  • Describing the method without explaining what it means for the conclusion.

Understand measurement and data type, but do not let statistical-test memorisation dominate your exam preparation.

Research Methods Challenge

The capstone challenge: apply the whole unit to one realistic study. Read the study, then work through Challenges A–I and reveal a strong response to check your reasoning.

A researcher wants to investigate whether smartphone use before bed affects sleep quality in adolescents. They recruit 60 students from one international school who volunteer after seeing an advertisement. The students report their average nightly smartphone use and complete a sleep-quality questionnaire. The researcher finds that students reporting more smartphone use tend to report poorer sleep. The researcher concludes: “Using smartphones before bed causes poor sleep in adolescents.”

What research method or design is being used?

Reveal a strong response
Smartphone use and sleep quality are both measured rather than manipulated, so this is correlational evidence: an association between two measured variables.

Identify the co-variables and how each was operationalised.

  • Co-variable 1: smartphone use — operationalised as the students’ self-reported average nightly smartphone use.
  • Co-variable 2: sleep quality — operationalised as the score on a sleep-quality questionnaire.

Could either operationalisation be improved?

Reveal a strong response
Yes. Self-reported smartphone use relies on memory and may be inaccurate, so objective screen-time logs from the device could improve it. A single questionnaire gives a subjective snapshot, so a validated sleep scale — or sleep diaries or an activity monitor — could strengthen the measure of sleep quality. Any new measure would need checks of reliability and validity.

What sampling issue should we consider?

Reveal a strong response
The sample is a volunteer or self-selected sample drawn from one international school. Volunteers may differ systematically from the wider adolescent population (self-selection bias), and a single school limits how far the findings can be generalised.

Identify at least one plausible alternative explanation for the association.

Explain how a third variable could influence both smartphone use and sleep quality.

Reveal a strong response
Stress, homework workload, mental health, caffeine use, or existing sleep problems could act as a third variable. For example, a heavy homework load could increase evening phone use (for contact with friends or procrastination) and reduce sleep, producing the association even if phone use itself has no direct effect on sleep.

The researcher reports r = +0.48 between reported smartphone use and sleep scores. On the questionnaire, a higher score means poorer sleep.

What does this result tell us? Interpret the association without any calculations.

Reveal a strong response
There is a moderate positive association: higher reported smartphone use is associated with poorer reported sleep. This describes a relationship between the two measured variables — it does not establish that smartphone use caused the poor sleep.

Is the researcher’s conclusion justified?

Original conclusion: “Using smartphones before bed causes poor sleep in adolescents.”

Rewrite the conclusion so that it matches the evidence.

Reveal a strong response
No. The design is correlational — nothing was manipulated and confounds were not controlled — so a causal conclusion cannot be justified. A defensible rewrite: “In this sample, greater reported smartphone use was associated with poorer reported sleep quality.”

Identify one relevant ethical consideration for this study.

Reveal a strong response
The participants are adolescents, so researchers should arrange meaningful informed consent and assent procedures, protect privacy and confidentiality, and handle potentially sensitive wellbeing information carefully. You do not need to cite jurisdiction-specific rules.

Choose two or three IB concepts that are most useful for evaluating this study, and explain why each one matters. Do not settle for listing concept names.

Reveal a strong response
Causality matters because a correlational design cannot justify a causal claim. Bias matters because self-selected volunteers from one school may systematically distort the evidence. Measurement matters because the findings rest on self-reported phone use and a questionnaire score, so the operationalisations must genuinely capture the intended constructs.

What can we reasonably conclude from this study, and what can we not conclude?

Your answer should integrate the method, measurement, sampling, alternative explanations, causality, and the scope of the conclusion.

Reveal a strong response
We can conclude that, in this volunteer sample of 60 students from one international school, reported smartphone use and reported sleep quality were moderately associated (r = +0.48). We cannot conclude that smartphone use caused poor sleep: nothing was manipulated, third variables were not controlled, and the self-report measures and non-representative sample limit the scope of generalisation. The conclusion should say no more and no less than the evidence and design justify.

Good research reasoning asks:

  1. What did the researchers actually do?
  2. What evidence did that produce?
  3. What could have influenced that evidence?
  4. What conclusion does the evidence justify?
