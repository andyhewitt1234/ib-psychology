Parts of an Experiment

Every experiment has key components. Understanding each part is essential for designing, evaluating, and critiquing research.

Click each component below to learn what it is, why it matters, and see a real example.

Independent Variable (IV)

+

What It Is

The variable that the researcher deliberately manipulates or changes. It is the presumed cause. The IV is what differs between the experimental and control groups.

Why It Matters

Without an IV, there is no experiment. The IV is what the researcher controls to see whether it produces an effect on the DV.

Real Example

Asch (1951): The IV was whether confederates gave correct or incorrect answers. Asch manipulated this to see if it influenced participants’ conformity.

Dependent Variable (DV)

+

What It Is

The variable that is measured or observed. It is the presumed effect. The DV is expected to change depending on what happens to the IV.

Why It Matters

The DV is how the researcher determines whether the IV had an effect. It must be clearly operationalised (defined in measurable terms) so the study can be replicated.

Real Example

Asch (1951): The DV was the number of times the participant conformed to the group’s incorrect answer. This was clearly countable and measurable.

Experimental Group

+

What It Is

The group that receives the manipulation of the independent variable. This is the group where the researcher expects to see an effect.

Why It Matters

The experimental group shows what happens when the IV is present. Its results are compared with the control group to determine whether the IV had an effect.

Real Example

Drug trial: The experimental group receives the actual medication. Researchers compare their outcomes with the control group (who receive a placebo) to determine whether the drug works.

Control Group

+

What It Is

The group that does not receive the manipulation. It serves as a baseline for comparison. The control group tells you what happens when the IV is absent.

Why It Matters

Without a control group, you cannot know whether the IV actually caused any change. The control group shows what would have happened without the intervention.

Real Example

CBT therapy trial: The control group remains on a waiting list and receives no therapy. After 12 weeks, their anxiety levels are compared with the group that received CBT. If the CBT group improved more, the therapy likely caused the improvement.

Random Assignment

+

What It Is

Participants are allocated to the experimental or control group by chance (e.g., coin flip, random number generator). Every participant has an equal chance of being in any condition.

Why It Matters

Random assignment ensures that any differences between groups at the start of the study are due to chance, not systematic bias. This means that if a significant effect is found at the end, it can be confidently attributed to the IV, not pre-existing differences between groups.

Real Example

Clinical trial for CBT: 100 patients with anxiety are randomly assigned to either the therapy group or the waiting list group. Because assignment is random, both groups should be equivalent in terms of age, gender, severity of anxiety, and other factors at the start of the study.

Extraneous Variables

+

What They Are

Any variable other than the IV that could potentially influence the DV. If not controlled, they can introduce noise or alternative explanations into the results.

Why They Matter

Extraneous variables reduce the clarity of results. If they are not controlled, you cannot be sure whether the IV or something else caused the effect. When an extraneous variable systematically varies with the IV, it becomes a confounding variable — a serious threat to internal validity.

Real Example

Asch (1951): Noise in the room, time of day, and the physical attractiveness of confederates were all extraneous variables. Asch controlled these by running the study in a quiet room, at consistent times, and with standardised confederates.

Operationalised Definitions

+

What They Are

Defining variables in specific, measurable terms so the study can be replicated. The researcher specifies exactly how the IV will be manipulated and how the DV will be measured.

Why They Matter

Without operationalisation, research cannot be replicated and findings are ambiguous. Other researchers need to know exactly what was done to repeat the study and verify the results.

Real Example

Operationalising “aggression”: Instead of vaguely studying aggression, a researcher defines it as “the number of times a child hits, kicks, or pushes another child during a 20-minute observation period.” This is specific, measurable, and replicable.

Standardised Procedures

+

What They Are

Using the same instructions, conditions, and measures for all participants. Every participant experiences the study in the same way, except for the manipulation of the IV.

Why They Matter

Standardisation ensures that any differences between groups are due to the IV and not to differences in how the study was conducted. It increases reliability — the study should produce consistent results if repeated.

Real Example

Asch (1951): All participants received the same instructions, saw the same line comparisons, and were seated in the same room with the same number of confederates. The only thing that changed was whether confederates gave correct or incorrect answers (the IV).
