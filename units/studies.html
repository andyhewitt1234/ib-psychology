Diagnosis and Classification Studies

Aim: Test whether psychiatric hospitals could reliably distinguish sane from insane people.

Research Method: Field experiment using pseudopatients.

Procedure: Healthy participants falsely reported hearing voices to gain admission, then behaved normally.

Findings: Pseudopatients were admitted and diagnosed (often schizophrenia); normal behaviours interpreted through diagnostic label.

Conclusion: Psychiatric diagnosis can be unreliable; labels strongly shape perception.

Strengths: High ecological validity (real hospitals).

Weaknesses: Deception and serious ethical concerns.

Use this study to explain: Labelling theory, confirmation bias, validity, reliability, DSM diagnosis, stigma, limitations of classification systems.

Aim: Examine how psychiatric diagnosis may affect self-stigma and identity.

Research Method: Correlational study.

Procedure: Examined how diagnosis and stigma sentiments related to patients’ self-meanings and self-identity.

Findings: Diagnosis can contribute to self-stigma by changing how people understand themselves.

Conclusion: Diagnostic labels may not simply describe a disorder but also shape personal identity.

Strengths: Directly supports the idea that diagnosis has social and psychological effects.

Weaknesses: Self-stigma is difficult to measure objectively.

Use this study to explain: Labelling theory, self-stigma, identity, diagnosis, limitations of mental health classification.

Aim: Review evidence for race and gender bias in psychological diagnosis.

Research Method: Review article.

Procedure: Examined research on whether clinicians diagnose disorders differently depending on patient race or gender.

Findings: Evidence of race bias (schizophrenia, mood-related differential diagnosis) and gender bias in some disorders.

Conclusion: Diagnosis can be affected by clinician bias and social expectations.

Strengths: Brings together a wide range of diagnostic evidence.

Weaknesses: Review conclusions depend on quality and comparability of included studies.

Use this study to explain: Clinician bias, cultural bias, validity, reliability, race, gender, diagnostic decision making.

Aim: Test the reliability of selected DSM-5 diagnoses in real clinical settings.

Research Method: Field study.

Procedure: Patients assessed by clinicians using DSM-5 criteria; reliability measured by consistency across assessments.

Findings: Reliability varied between diagnoses; some showed weaker reliability than expected.

Conclusion: DSM diagnosis can be standardized but is not equally reliable across all disorders.

Strengths: Conducted in realistic clinical settings.

Weaknesses: Real clinical complexity may reduce control over variables.

Use this study to explain: DSM-5, reliability, test-retest reliability, interrater reliability, diagnosis, measurement.

Aim: Examine racial differences in diagnosis, especially tendency for Black Americans to be diagnosed with schizophrenia.

Research Method: Correlational study.

Procedure: Examined racial patterns in diagnostic outcomes for psychotic disorders.

Findings: Race may influence diagnostic outcomes, especially in psychotic disorder diagnosis.

Conclusion: Diagnosis may be affected by racial bias or cultural misunderstanding.

Strengths: Directly useful for evaluating diagnostic validity.

Weaknesses: Diagnostic differences may be influenced by confounding variables (access to healthcare, clinician training, symptom presentation).

Use this study to explain: Racial bias, cultural bias, validity, reliability, diagnosis.

Aim: Examine gender differences in diagnosis and prevalence of mental disorders.

Research Method: Correlational study.

Procedure: Analyzed gender patterns in diagnosis and disorder rates for mood and anxiety disorders.

Findings: Women more frequently diagnosed with mood and anxiety disorders.

Conclusion: Gender may influence diagnosis, prevalence, symptom expression, or help seeking.

Strengths: Helps explain population differences in mental health.

Weaknesses: Gender differences may reflect real prevalence, reporting patterns, clinician bias, or social expectations — causality difficult.

Use this study to explain: Gender bias, prevalence, diagnosis, cultural or social influences on mental health.

Biological and Depression Studies

Aim: Investigate whether genes and stressful life events interact to increase depression risk.

Research Method: Longitudinal study.

Procedure: Participants followed over time; assessed for stressful life events, depressive symptoms, and 5-HTTLPR gene variant.

Findings: People with short allele more likely to develop depression after stressful experiences.

Conclusion: Depression influenced by both genetic vulnerability and environmental stress.

Strengths: Longitudinal design allowed study of development over time.

Weaknesses: Later studies produced mixed replication results.

Use this study to explain: Gene-environment interaction, genetic vulnerability, diathesis-stress model.

Aim: Identify biological subtypes of depression using brain connectivity patterns.

Research Method: fMRI study.

Procedure: Depressed patients and controls underwent fMRI scans; whole-brain connectivity patterns analyzed.

Findings: Four depression biotypes linked to different symptom profiles and treatment response.

Conclusion: Depression may not be one single biological disorder but may involve different neural network patterns.

Strengths: Supports precision psychiatry and more personalized treatment.

Weaknesses: Later replication and methodological questions mean findings should be treated cautiously.

Use this study to explain: fMRI, neural networks, biomarkers, precision psychiatry, biological explanations, reductionism.

Aim: Propose a neural circuit taxonomy for depression and anxiety.

Research Method: Review article.

Procedure: Synthesized research on brain circuits involved in depression and anxiety (default mode, salience, negative affect, positive affect, attention, cognitive control circuits).

Findings: Depression and anxiety may be better understood through circuit dysfunctions rather than broad symptom labels.

Conclusion: Precision psychiatry could improve diagnosis and treatment by identifying specific neural circuit patterns.

Strengths: Provides a modern biological framework.

Weaknesses: Theoretical review rather than a single experimental test.

Use this study to explain: RDoC, precision psychiatry, brain networks, biomarkers, depression, anxiety.

Aim: Evaluate the effectiveness of antidepressants compared with placebo.

Research Method: Meta-analysis.

Procedure: Examined clinical trial data comparing antidepressant drugs with placebo treatments.

Findings: Placebo effects account for large part of improvement; drug-placebo difference small, especially for mild/moderate depression.

Conclusion: Antidepressants may help some patients (especially severe cases), but serotonin-based drug treatment is not a complete explanation.

Strengths: Challenges simplistic biological explanations.

Weaknesses: Effectiveness varies by severity, drug type, and patient group.

Use this study to explain: SSRIs, placebo effect, serotonin hypothesis, treatment effectiveness, reductionism.

Aim: Assess the antidepressant tianeptine in depressive disorders.

Research Method: Review article.

Procedure: Summarized clinical and pharmacological evidence on tianeptine.

Findings: Tianeptine has antidepressant effects despite increasing serotonin uptake (contrasts with SSRIs that block reuptake).

Conclusion: Depression cannot be explained by a simple “more serotonin equals less depression” model.

Strengths: Directly challenges the simple serotonin hypothesis.

Weaknesses: Review of existing evidence rather than a single controlled experiment.

Use this study to explain: Serotonin hypothesis limitations, antidepressant treatment, reuptake, tianeptine, biological reductionism.

Aim: Investigate whether serotonin transporter gene variation and family history link to vulnerability to depression.

Research Method: Laboratory experiment.

Procedure: Participants assessed for 5-HTTLPR genotype and family history; exposed to serotonin depletion procedures.

Findings: Short allele and family history may increase vulnerability to mood changes.

Conclusion: Serotonin systems and genetic vulnerability may interact in depression risk.

Strengths: Directly tests a biological mechanism.

Weaknesses: Artificial serotonin depletion may not fully reflect real life depression.

Use this study to explain: Serotonin, 5-HTTLPR, genetic vulnerability, serotonin depletion, biological explanations.

Aim: Summarize heritability evidence across human traits using twin studies.

Research Method: Large meta-analysis.

Procedure: Analyzed data from 2,748 publications covering 17,000+ traits and millions of twin pairs.

Findings: Many human traits influenced by both genetic and environmental factors.

Conclusion: Genetic influence is widespread but does not remove the importance of environment.

Strengths: Massive sample size and broad evidence base.

Weaknesses: Twin studies rely on assumptions about shared and non-shared environments.

Use this study to explain: Heritability, twin studies, genetic influence, nature-nurture interaction, behaviour not caused by genes alone.

Aim: Estimate heritability of major depressive disorder using family-based data.

Research Method: Genetic correlational study.

Procedure: Used large family health data (Generation Scotland) to estimate genetic contribution to MDD.

Findings: Supported genetic contribution to depression but heritability is not total.

Conclusion: MDD has genetic influences but remains strongly shaped by environmental and psychological factors.

Strengths: Family-based designs offer alternative to twin-only assumptions.

Weaknesses: Family studies can still be affected by shared environmental influences.

Use this study to explain: Genetic vulnerability, heritability, MDD, limits of biological explanations.

Aim: Explain depression through negative thinking patterns.

Research Method: Clinical observation study.

Procedure: Analyzed common thought patterns in people with depression.

Findings: Cognitive triad — negative views of self, world, and future.

Conclusion: Depression maintained by automatic negative thoughts and schemas.

Strengths: Model directly led to CBT, a major evidence-based treatment.

Weaknesses: Difficult to prove whether negative thinking causes depression or results from it.

Use this study to explain: Beck’s cognitive triad, cognitive distortions, rumination, automatic thoughts, CBT, cognitive explanations.

Aim: Examine whether negative views of self, world, future mediated relationship between subjective social status and depressive symptoms.

Research Method: Correlational study.

Procedure: Participants completed measures of subjective social status, cognitive triad beliefs, and depressive symptoms.

Findings: Lower subjective social status linked to more negative cognitive triad views and depressive symptoms.

Conclusion: Social status may influence depression through cognitive patterns.

Strengths: Connects sociocultural and cognitive explanations.

Weaknesses: Correlation cannot prove causation.

Use this study to explain: Beck’s cognitive triad, subjective social status, depression, cognitive vulnerability, sociocultural influence.

Culture and Environmental Studies

Aim: Investigate whether Chinese people express depression through somatic symptoms.

Research Method: Cross-cultural study.

Procedure: Compared depressed Malaysian Chinese and Euro-Australian patients in symptom expression.

Findings: Chinese participants more likely to report physical symptoms (fatigue, bodily complaints) as central.

Conclusion: Culture shapes how depression is expressed and reported.

Strengths: Directly supports cultural variation in symptom expression.

Weaknesses: Findings from one Chinese population may not generalize to all Chinese communities.

Use this study to explain: Somatization, cultural bias, diagnosis, symptom expression, prevalence differences.

Aim: Examine relationship between collectivism, individualism, 5-HTTLPR gene, and rates of anxiety/mood disorders.

Research Method: Cross-cultural correlational study.

Procedure: Compared cultural values, genetic allele frequencies, and disorder prevalence across countries.

Findings: Collectivist cultures had higher frequencies of short allele but lower anxiety/mood disorder prevalence.

Conclusion: Collectivist culture may act as protective factor against genetic vulnerability.

Strengths: Integrates biological and cultural explanations.

Weaknesses: Cross-national correlations cannot prove causality; may be affected by confounding variables.

Use this study to explain: Collectivism, individualism, gene-culture interaction, protective factors, why one perspective is not enough.

Aim: Examine stress, anxiety, and depression among Ukrainians during Russian invasion.

Research Method: Survey.

Procedure: 706 Ukrainian participants completed questionnaires about mental health six months into war.

Findings: Elevated stress, anxiety, and depression symptoms.

Conclusion: War exposure and environmental stressors can affect population mental health.

Strengths: Real world crisis with high ecological validity.

Weaknesses: Survey data is self-reported; cannot isolate all causal factors.

Use this study to explain: Trauma, war exposure, PTSD, anxiety, depression, stress, environmental factors, social responsibility.

Aim: Investigate whether long-term air pollution exposure is associated with late-life depression.

Research Method: Longitudinal correlational study.

Procedure: Older US adults followed using pollution exposure estimates and depression diagnosis data.

Findings: Long-term exposure to air pollutants associated with increased risk of late-life depression.

Conclusion: Air pollution may be an environmental risk factor for depression.

Strengths: Large scale and real world environmental data.

Weaknesses: Observational studies cannot fully prove causation; may include confounding variables.

Use this study to explain: Pollution, environmental stressors, depression, public health, correlation, government responsibility.

Aim: Summarize evidence linking air pollution to depression and mental illness.

Research Method: Literature review.

Procedure: Discussed evidence that PM2.5 is associated with increased likelihood of mental illness.

Findings: Every 1 standard deviation increase in PM2.5 may increase likelihood of mental illness including depression.

Conclusion: Pollution may be an important environmental factor in mental health.

Strengths: Connects public health and mental health.

Weaknesses: Much of the evidence is correlational.

Use this study to explain: PM2.5, pollution, depression, environmental risk, confounding variables, responsibility.

Social Media, Cognition, and Wellbeing Studies

Aim: Investigate the relationship between social media use and addiction-like symptoms.

Research Method: Cross-sectional survey.

Procedure: Participants completed measures of social media use frequency, duration, and engagement, alongside validated addiction scales measuring withdrawal, tolerance, and preoccupation.

Findings: High levels of social media use were positively correlated with addictive symptoms. Personality traits such as narcissism and lower self-esteem predicted greater susceptibility to problematic use.

Conclusion: Social media use can develop addictive patterns, particularly in individuals with certain personality traits. Excessive use may negatively affect psychological well-being.

Strengths: Large sample, validated measures, directly relevant to modern health concerns.

Weaknesses: Correlational design cannot establish causation. Self-report data may be unreliable. Cross-sectional design cannot show direction of effects.

Use this study to explain: Social media addiction, problematic use, personality factors, self-esteem, well-being, correlation.

Aim: Test whether limiting social media use to 30 minutes per day leads to improvements in well-being.

Research Method: Experimental (randomised controlled trial).

Procedure: Participants were randomly assigned to either limit their social media use (Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat) to 30 minutes per day or continue using social media as normal for three weeks.

Findings: The limited-use group showed significant reductions in loneliness and depression compared to the control group. Both groups showed decreases in anxiety and fear of missing out (FOMO).

Conclusion: Reducing social media use can lead to meaningful improvements in well-being, particularly in reducing loneliness and depression.

Strengths: Randomised controlled trial with objective measure (screen time data). Short-term intervention with measurable effects.

Weaknesses: Short duration (3 weeks). Participants were university students (limited generalisability). Self-selected sample may be more motivated to change.

Use this study to explain: Social media, well-being, loneliness, depression, experimental design, random assignment, intervention studies.

Aim: Examine fear of missing out as predictor of problematic social media/smartphone use.

Research Method: Correlational survey research.

Procedure: Participants completed measures of FOMO, social media use, smartphone use, and psychological variables.

Findings: FOMO strongly associated with problematic social media/smartphone use.

Conclusion: Fear of missing out may help explain compulsive checking and problematic use.

Strengths: Directly explains a relevant modern health problem.

Weaknesses: Correlation cannot prove whether FOMO causes problematic use or results from it.

Use this study to explain: FOMO, social media addiction, anxiety, problematic use, directionality.

Aim: Examine whether FOMO and self-compassion moderated relationship between social media use and anxiety in adolescents.

Research Method: Questionnaire study.

Procedure: Adolescents reported social media use, anxiety symptoms, FOMO, and self-compassion.

Findings: Adolescents with high FOMO showed stronger links between social media use and anxiety.

Conclusion: FOMO may make some teenagers more vulnerable to anxiety during social media use.

Strengths: Focuses on adolescent mental health.

Weaknesses: Self-report and correlational design limit causal conclusions.

Use this study to explain: FOMO, anxiety, adolescent wellbeing, social media use, moderation.

Aim: Investigate whether social comparison mediates relationship between problematic social media use and depression.

Research Method: Correlational study.

Procedure: Participants completed measures of problematic social media use, social comparison, and depressive symptoms.

Findings: Social comparison may be one mechanism linking problematic social media use with depression.

Conclusion: Upward or negative comparison can partly explain why social media may harm mood.

Strengths: Identifies a clear cognitive mechanism.

Weaknesses: Design cannot prove causation.

Use this study to explain: Upward comparison, downward comparison, self-esteem, depression, social media, cognitive explanations.

Aim: Review evidence on social media use, depression, and suicidality in adolescents.

Research Method: Review article.

Procedure: Reviewed studies examining links between social networking site use and adolescent depression or suicidality.

Findings: Evidence of bidirectional associations between social media use and depression; emphasized complexity.

Conclusion: Social media may be linked to depression, but causality and directionality remain difficult.

Strengths: Reviews a broad body of evidence.

Weaknesses: Scoping reviews depend on quality of included studies.

Use this study to explain: Social media, depression, adolescent mental health, bidirectionality, directionality problem.

Health Behaviour and Treatment Studies

Aim: Compare effectiveness of different treatments for major depression.

Research Method: True experiment (RCT).

Procedure: 250 outpatients with major depression assigned to CBT, IPT, imipramine + clinical management, or placebo + clinical management for 16 weeks.

Findings: Several treatments effective; medication often performing strongly for more severe depression.

Conclusion: Both psychological and biological treatments can reduce depressive symptoms.

Strengths: Controlled comparative design.

Weaknesses: Treatment effects may vary by severity and individual differences.

Use this study to explain: CBT, IPT, antidepressants, placebo, treatment effectiveness, biological treatment, psychological treatment.

Aim: Test whether theory of planned behaviour could explain adolescent physical activity.

Research Method: Questionnaire study.

Procedure: Adolescents completed measures of attitudes, subjective norms, perceived behavioural control, intentions, and physical activity.

Findings: Attitudes and perceived behavioural control important predictors of intention and behaviour.

Conclusion: Theory of planned behaviour can help explain health behaviour such as exercise.

Strengths: Applies a clear model to real health behaviour.

Weaknesses: Intentions do not always become behaviour because habits, emotions, and environment also matter.

Use this study to explain: TPB, exercise, intention, perceived behavioural control, prevention, health behaviour.

Aim: Review dissonance-based interventions for health behaviour change.

Research Method: Systematic review.

Procedure: Reviewed studies using cognitive dissonance techniques (including hypocrisy paradigm) to change health behaviours.

Findings: Dissonance-based interventions often effective, especially the hypocrisy paradigm.

Conclusion: Creating discomfort between beliefs and behaviour can motivate healthier choices.

Strengths: Examines multiple health behaviours.

Weaknesses: People may reduce dissonance without changing behaviour.

Use this study to explain: Cognitive dissonance, hypocrisy paradigm, prevention, attitude-behaviour gap, health behaviour change.

Aim: Identify modifiable protective factors for depression.

Research Method: Correlational study.

Procedure: Examined over 100 possible factors linked to depression risk.

Findings: Social connection identified as one of the strongest protective factors.

Conclusion: Improving social connection may help prevent depression.

Strengths: Examined many possible modifiable factors.

Weaknesses: Broad population research may not explain individual cases in detail.

Use this study to explain: Protective factors, social connection, prevention, depression, resilience.

Aim: Examine whether social identification is linked to anxiety and depression symptoms, especially in migrants and ethnic minorities.

Research Method: Meta-analysis.

Procedure: Analyzed studies on social identity and common mental disorders.

Findings: Stronger social identification linked to lower depression and anxiety (small effect sizes).

Conclusion: Social identity and belonging can support mental health.

Strengths: Uses meta-analytic evidence.

Weaknesses: Effects are small and may vary across groups.

Use this study to explain: Social connection, belonging, protective factors, acculturation, migrants, depression, anxiety.

Aim: Investigate how acculturation affected substance use among Hispanic participants.

Research Method: Survey study.

Procedure: Examined drug and alcohol use among Hispanic groups with different levels of acculturation.

Findings: Acculturation associated with higher illicit drug use and risky substance use.

Conclusion: Adopting a host culture may increase risk when host culture normalizes substance use.

Strengths: Directly connects culture with health behaviour.

Weaknesses: Acculturation is complex; may be confounded with age, peer groups, socioeconomic status, or stress.

Use this study to explain: Acculturation, substance misuse, cultural differences, health behaviour, prevalence.

Aim: Investigate how self-construal and culture relate to impulsive consumption including alcohol use.

Research Method: Laboratory experiment.

Procedure: Participants’ independent or interdependent self-construal and alcohol-related attitudes/behaviours examined.

Findings: Individualistic self-construal linked to more impulsive alcohol consumption.

Conclusion: Cultural values can influence risky health behaviours.

Strengths: Connects culture with specific health behaviour.

Weaknesses: Consumer behaviour findings may not fully explain clinical addiction.

Use this study to explain: Individualism, collectivism, alcohol use, impulsive drinking, social norms, cultural differences in health.

Aim: Investigate impact of individualism, collectivism, and hopelessness on substance use.

Research Method: Correlational study.

Procedure: Participants completed measures of cultural orientation, hopelessness, and substance use.

Findings: Individualism and hopelessness linked to substance use.

Conclusion: Cultural orientation may influence substance misuse partly through psychological factors such as hopelessness.

Strengths: Integrates culture and cognition.

Weaknesses: Correlational data cannot prove causality.

Use this study to explain: Individualism, collectivism, hopelessness, substance misuse, cultural influences on health behaviour.

Aim: Test whether children learn aggressive behaviour through observation and imitation.

Research Method: Laboratory experiment.

Procedure: Children watched an adult model behave aggressively or non-aggressively toward a Bobo doll, then their own behaviour was observed.

Findings: Children who observed aggressive models were more likely to imitate aggressive behaviour.

Conclusion: Behaviour can be learned through modelling and vicarious reinforcement.

Strengths: Controlled experiment supports cause and effect.

Weaknesses: Hitting a doll in a lab may not fully represent real life aggression.

Use this study to explain: Social learning theory, modelling, imitation, vicarious reinforcement, vaping, substance use, eating habits, exercise, social media behaviour.

Hormone and Chemical Messenger Studies

Aim: Test whether oxytocin increases interpersonal trust.

Research Method: Laboratory experiment using economic trust game.

Procedure: Participants received oxytocin or placebo then made trust-based financial decisions.

Findings: Participants given oxytocin showed greater trust in others.

Conclusion: Oxytocin can influence social behaviour, especially trust.

Strengths: Controlled experimental design.

Weaknesses: Economic trust games may not fully represent real world relationships.

Use this study to explain: Oxytocin, trust, bonding, social behaviour, chemical messengers, biological explanations.

Aim: Investigate whether oxytocin influences fidelity or social distance from attractive strangers.

Research Method: Laboratory experiment.

Procedure: Men in monogamous relationships received oxytocin or placebo and interacted with/approached attractive women.

Findings: Oxytocin increased distance from attractive women among men in relationships.

Conclusion: Oxytocin may support pair bonding and relationship maintenance.

Strengths: Gives a specific behavioural link for oxytocin.

Weaknesses: Sample and artificial setting limit generalizability.

Use this study to explain: Oxytocin, fidelity, attachment, bonding, social behaviour.

Aim: Investigate whether testosterone affects neural responses to social threat.

Research Method: fMRI experiment.

Procedure: Participants’ testosterone levels or manipulation linked to brain responses while viewing threatening/angry faces.

Findings: Testosterone increased amygdala reactivity to social threat cues.

Conclusion: Testosterone may influence threat perception and aggression-related behaviour, but does not simply “cause aggression.”

Strengths: fMRI provides biological evidence.

Weaknesses: Brain activation does not directly prove real world aggressive behaviour.

Use this study to explain: Testosterone, dominance, aggression, social threat, amygdala reactivity.

Stress Theory Studies and Classic Models

Aim: Explain the biological stress response.

Research Method: Animal experiments.

Procedure: Animals exposed to stressors; physiological responses observed.

Findings: General adaptation syndrome — alarm, resistance, exhaustion.

Conclusion: Prolonged stress can wear down the body.

Strengths: Gave psychology a clear biological stress model.

Weaknesses: Animal stress responses may not fully represent human cognitive appraisal.

Use this study to explain: Acute stress, chronic stress, cortisol, burnout, allostatic load, physical health.

Aim: Explain stress as a transaction between person and environment.

Research Method: Literature review.

Procedure: Focused on how people appraise demands and coping resources.

Findings: Transactional model — primary appraisal and secondary appraisal.

Conclusion: Stress depends not only on the event but also on how the person interprets it.

Strengths: Explains individual differences in stress response.

Weaknesses: Appraisal can be difficult to measure objectively.

Use this study to explain: Stress, coping, appraisal, cognition, cortisol, health behaviour.
