What Is Psychology?

The scientific study of behaviour and mental processes.

Psychology is the scientific study of mental processes and behaviour. It explores everything from neurons firing in the brain to the collective behaviour of cultures and societies.

Psychologists use observation, experimentation, and analysis to explore the biological, cognitive, emotional, and social processes that shape what we think, feel, and do.

Consider these questions:

  • Why do people remember things that never happened?
  • Why do we obey people in authority?
  • Why can two people experience the same event differently?
  • How can childhood experiences influence later behaviour?
  • Why do people conform to groups?

Psychology investigates exactly these kinds of questions.

Click to see which area investigates each question:

  • Why do people remember things that never happened? → Cognitive psychology (memory, false memories)
  • Why do we obey authority? → Social psychology (conformity, obedience)
  • Why do two people experience the same event differently? → Cognitive psychology (perception, schemas)
  • How do childhood experiences influence later behaviour? → Developmental psychology (attachment, self)
  • Why do people conform? → Social psychology (group influence, social identity)
What makes psychology different from just "having opinions about people"?
Psychology uses systematic methods to test explanations. Rather than relying on intuition or anecdote, psychologists collect evidence, test hypotheses, and evaluate claims against data. This makes psychological knowledge more reliable than casual observation.

Is Psychology a Science?

Can something as complicated as human behaviour really be studied scientifically?

Psychology uses the scientific method:

  • Systematic observation: Carefully watching and recording behaviour
  • Hypotheses: Making testable predictions
  • Evidence: Collecting data to support or refute claims
  • Measurement: Quantifying behaviour using standardised tools
  • Replication: Repeating studies to check reliability
  • Testing explanations: Ruling out alternative accounts

Human behaviour is complex and influenced by many factors:

  • People are not identical — individual differences complicate measurement
  • Some variables cannot be controlled (ethics, culture, history)
  • Self-report data depends on honesty and self-awareness
  • Lab findings may not generalise to real life
  • Different researchers may interpret the same data differently

Psychology is not as precise as physics or chemistry — but this does not make it unscientific. It means psychology requires different methods and careful interpretation.

Some psychological questions are best answered with experiments. Others require observations, interviews, surveys, or case studies.

The key insight is that no single method suits every question. Psychologists choose methods that match what they are trying to find out.

To what extent can psychology be considered a science?
Psychology uses systematic, evidence-based methods to investigate behaviour. It may not achieve the precision of natural sciences, but it applies rigorous methods appropriate to its subject matter. Psychology is best understood as a science that requires careful, context-sensitive interpretation.

Psychology in Action

Demonstrations that reveal how our minds work — and how they can mislead us.

Read two descriptions of the same person:

Ken: intelligent, hard-working, impulsive, critical, stubborn, envious

Robin: envious, stubborn, critical, impulsive, hard-working, intelligent

Same traits, different order — but most people rate Ken more favourably. The order in which we receive information shapes our perception.

What happened? Why might it have happened?

Read this list: bed, rest, awake, tired, dream, wake, laydown, pillow, snore, snooze, nap.

Question: Was "sleep" on the list?

Many people say yes — but it wasn't. All words are related to sleep, so your brain fills in the gap. This demonstrates how memory is reconstructive, not a perfect recording.

How could a psychologist test this scientifically?

In a famous study, participants count how many times a ball is passed between players. While focusing on counting, a person in a gorilla suit walks through the scene — and about half don't notice it.

This demonstrates inattentional blindness: when we focus intently on one task, we miss unexpected stimuli right in front of us.

Two lines are exactly the same length, but one has outward arrowheads and the other inward. Most people perceive the inward-arrowhead line as longer.

Research shows people in Western cultures are more affected by this illusion, possibly because they are surrounded by buildings with straight lines and right angles (the "carpentered world" hypothesis).

This shows that even perception is shaped by culture.

What pattern do these demonstrations share?
Each demonstration shows that our minds do not work the way we assume. We think memory is accurate but it is reconstructive. We think we see everything but we miss obvious things. We think perception is objective but it is shaped by culture. Psychology reveals the gap between how we think we work and how we actually work.

The 6 Core Concepts

Ways of thinking about psychological knowledge — not topics to memorise.

The six core concepts are ways of thinking critically about psychological knowledge. They are NOT six topics you simply memorise. They are lenses you apply throughout the entire course.

You will return to these concepts in every unit, every assessment, and every research study you encounter.

  • Bias — How can personal beliefs or expectations distort interpretation?
  • Causality — Does X actually cause Y, or is something else going on?
  • Change — How and why does behaviour change over time?
  • Measurement — How do we know what we claim to know?
  • Perspective — How would a different approach explain this?
  • Responsibility — What are the ethical implications of this knowledge?
Why are these called "ways of thinking" rather than "topics"?
Because you do not study bias once and move on. You apply the concept of bias to every study you read, every argument you evaluate, and every piece of research you design. These concepts are tools for thinking, not content to memorise.

Core Concept — Bias

A systematic influence that can distort judgement, research, or interpretation.

Bias is a systematic influence that can distort judgement, research findings, or interpretation of behaviour.

Bias can occur in:

  • Participants: Demand characteristics, social desirability
  • Researchers: Expectations, interpretation
  • Measurements: Questionnaire design, observation methods
  • Samples: Who is studied and who is not
  • Interpretation: Selective reporting, confirmation bias

A researcher expects men to be more aggressive. During observation, they interpret a man's raised voice as "aggressive" but a woman's raised voice as "passionate."

The researcher's expectation created a systematic distortion in how identical behaviour was interpreted. This is researcher bias.

Where might bias enter this research? "A teacher rates students they taught as higher achieving."
Teacher expectancy bias: the teacher's knowledge of which students are "theirs" could systematically influence their ratings. The teacher may unconsciously rate their own students more favourably, regardless of actual performance. This is why blind assessment methods are used.

Core Concept — Causality

The difference between "X is associated with Y" and "X causes Y."

Consider this claim:

"Teenagers who use more social media report more anxiety."

Does social media cause anxiety?

Not necessarily. This finding could mean:

  • Reverse causality: Anxiety leads to more social media use (not the other way around)
  • Third variable: Something else (e.g., loneliness, sleep deprivation) causes both increased social media use and anxiety
  • Correlation: The two are related, but neither causes the other

To claim that X causes Y, researchers typically need:

  • Manipulation: Researchers control the independent variable
  • Control: A comparison group to rule out alternative explanations
  • Random assignment: Participants equally distributed across conditions
  • Replication: The finding is consistently reproduced

Correlation does not automatically establish causation. This is one of the most important principles in psychology.

Why is the distinction between correlation and causation so important?
Because acting on correlational findings as if they were causal can lead to ineffective or harmful interventions. If anxiety causes social media use (not the reverse), then restricting social media would not reduce anxiety. Getting the causal direction right matters for real-world applications.

Core Concept — Change

How and why human behaviour develops, adapts, and evolves.

Psychologists investigate:

  • Developmental change: How thinking develops from childhood to adulthood
  • Behavioural change: How therapy reduces anxiety symptoms
  • Cognitive change: How learning new information changes understanding
  • Biological change: How neuroplasticity allows the brain to rewire itself
  • Change over time: How attitudes, relationships, and identity evolve

Can psychotherapy change patterns of thinking?

Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) aims to identify and change unhelpful thinking patterns. If therapy is effective, we should see measurable change in both thinking and behaviour.

The key question becomes: What changed, how was it measured, and what might have caused the change?

What's the difference between saying "people change" and "change was measured"?
Saying "people change" is an opinion. Saying "change was measured" requires evidence: specific behaviours or mental processes that were measured before and after an intervention, using reliable and valid tools. Psychology requires measurable evidence of change, not just the assumption that it happens.

Core Concept — Measurement

How do we know what we claim to know?

A psychologist says: "Stress decreased."

Ask: How do they know?

Psychological constructs like intelligence, depression, stress, memory, prejudice, and attachment cannot be directly observed the way height or weight can. Psychologists must operationalize and measure them indirectly.

Operationalization means defining a construct in terms of how it will be measured.

Example: "Stress" could be measured as:

  • Cortisol levels (biological measure)
  • Self-reported anxiety on a scale (self-report)
  • Number of sleepless nights (behavioural measure)

Each measure captures something different. None captures "stress" perfectly. This is why psychologists use validity (does it measure what it claims?) and reliability (does it produce consistent results?) to evaluate their measures.

How would you measure "happiness"? Consider:

  • A self-report survey ("Rate your happiness 1-10")
  • Brain scan activity in reward regions
  • Frequency of smiling
  • Number of positive social interactions per day

Each method measures something different. The "best" measure depends on what aspect of happiness you are investigating.

Why does the way we measure something change what we conclude about it?
If you measure "stress" with cortisol, you get biological data. If you measure it with self-report, you get subjective experience. These may not agree. The measurement tool shapes the findings, which is why researchers must justify their measurement choices and use multiple methods.

Core Concept — Perspective

Different psychological approaches reveal different aspects of the same behaviour.

Psychological behaviour can often be understood from different perspectives. The same behaviour can have multiple valid explanations, each highlighting different causes.

Biological perspective: Genetic predisposition, neurochemical imbalance, brain structure differences.

Cognitive perspective: Persistent negative thinking patterns, catastrophising, rumination.

Sociocultural perspective: Social isolation, poverty, discrimination, lack of support.

Each explanation is valid. None alone tells the full story.

Ask: Does one explanation have to make the others wrong?

No. Different perspectives may explain different parts of behaviour. A complete understanding often requires drawing on multiple perspectives simultaneously.

If someone says "depression is purely biological," what perspective are they ignoring?
They are ignoring cognitive and sociocultural perspectives. While biology plays a role, thinking patterns and social circumstances also contribute. A purely biological explanation overlooks the role of learning, experience, and social context in shaping mental health.

Core Concept — Responsibility

The ethical and social implications of psychological knowledge.

Responsibility is not just about following ethics rules. Psychologists have broader responsibilities concerning:

  • Participant welfare: Protecting people who take part in research
  • Informed consent: Ensuring participants understand what they agree to
  • Privacy and confidentiality: Protecting personal information
  • Interpretation of evidence: Reporting findings accurately and honestly
  • Communicating findings: Making research accessible without distorting it
  • Applications of knowledge: Considering how findings might be used or misused
  • Vulnerable groups: Extra care when research involves children, patients, or marginalised populations

A researcher discovers that a particular parenting style is associated with lower academic achievement in children.

Before reading on: How should this finding be communicated?

Discussion points:

  • Should the researcher name the parenting style publicly?
  • Could this finding be used to judge or stigmatise parents?
  • Does the public's right to information outweigh potential harm?
  • How should researchers communicate uncertainty in their findings?
Why does responsibility matter beyond the research setting?
Psychological findings are used in policy, education, healthcare, and media. If findings are communicated irresponsibly, they can reinforce stereotypes, cause stigma, or lead to harmful interventions. Researchers must consider the real-world impact of their work.

How Psychological Content Works

The three levels of psychological explanation and research methodology.

Students study psychological content from three levels:

  • Biological: How brain, hormones, and genetics influence behaviour
  • Cognitive: How we think, remember, and make decisions
  • Sociocultural: How groups, culture, and social norms influence behaviour

Each level provides a different way of understanding the same behaviour.

In addition to the three content levels, students learn Research Methodology — how psychologists generate and evaluate psychological evidence.

Research Methodology is not a type of explanation. It is the toolkit psychologists use to investigate questions at all three levels.

What is the difference between content and methodology?
Content is the psychological knowledge (what we know). Methodology is how we generate and test that knowledge (how we know it). Biological, cognitive, and sociocultural content explain behaviour. Research methodology tells us how trustworthy those explanations are.

Biological Content

What kinds of questions does biological psychology ask?

Biological psychology explores:

  • Brain and behaviour: How different brain regions control different functions
  • Neurotransmission: How chemical messengers communicate between neurons
  • Genetics: How inherited traits influence behaviour
  • Neuroplasticity: How the brain changes in response to experience
  • Localization: Which brain areas are involved in specific functions
  • Hormones: How chemical messengers like cortisol and testosterone affect behaviour

Why do some people handle stress better than others?

A biological explanation might focus on differences in cortisol reactivity, genetic variations in stress-response genes, or brain structures involved in emotional regulation.

What kinds of questions does biological psychology ask?
Biological psychology asks: What is happening in the brain and body when people think, feel, and behave in certain ways? How do genes, hormones, and neural processes influence psychological functioning?

Cognitive Content

How we think, remember, decide, and sometimes get things wrong.

The cognitive approach examines:

  • Memory: How we encode, store, and retrieve information
  • Thinking and decision-making: How we process information and make judgements
  • Schemas: Mental frameworks that organise knowledge
  • Cognitive biases: Systematic errors in thinking
  • Learning: How experience changes knowledge and behaviour

How can processes we cannot directly see be studied scientifically?

Consider: you cannot see a memory. But you can measure recall accuracy, reaction times, and brain activity during remembering. Cognitive psychology uses indirect measures to study internal mental processes.

This connects directly to Measurement — how do we operationalize things we cannot observe?

Why is measurement particularly challenging for cognitive psychology?
Cognitive processes like thinking, remembering, and deciding happen inside the mind. We cannot observe them directly. Psychologists must rely on indirect measures — reaction times, accuracy scores, self-reports, brain scans — which each capture only part of the picture.

Sociocultural Content

How groups, culture, and social norms shape behaviour.

The sociocultural approach explores how behaviour is influenced by:

  • Groups: How group membership shapes identity and behaviour
  • Culture: How cultural values and norms influence thinking and feeling
  • Social norms: Unwritten rules that govern behaviour
  • Identity: How we define ourselves through social categories
  • Conformity and obedience: How social pressure changes behaviour

Would you behave differently if everyone around you behaved differently?

Research consistently shows: yes. Social norms powerfully shape behaviour, often without conscious awareness. Conformity studies, social identity research, and cross-cultural psychology all demonstrate this.

This connects to future material such as Social Identity Theory and Social Learning Theory.

What's the difference between a cognitive and a sociocultural explanation of the same behaviour?
A cognitive explanation focuses on internal mental processes (schemas, memory, thinking). A sociocultural explanation focuses on external social influences (groups, culture, norms). For example: a cognitive explanation of prejudice might focus on schemas; a sociocultural explanation might focus on group conflict and social identity.

Research Methodology

How psychologists generate and evaluate evidence.

Psychologists use different methods because different questions require different evidence.

Consider: if you wanted to know why teenagers use social media, would an experiment necessarily be the best method? Perhaps interviews or surveys would reveal more about motivations.

  • Experiments: Manipulating variables to establish causality
  • Observations: Systematically watching behaviour in natural or controlled settings
  • Interviews: In-depth exploration of individual experiences
  • Surveys and questionnaires: Gathering standardised data from large groups
  • Correlational research: Measuring relationships between variables
  • Case studies: Detailed investigation of individuals or small groups

Each method has strengths and limitations. The "best" method depends on the research question. A strength of experiments is establishing causality. A strength of interviews is depth. A strength of surveys is generalisability.

No single method is always superior. This is why psychologists often use multiple methods to investigate the same question.

If you wanted to understand why people conform, which method would you choose and why?
An experiment (like Asch's) could establish whether conformity occurs and under what conditions. Interviews could explore why individuals conform. Surveys could measure how common conformity is. Each method answers a different aspect of the same question.

The 4 Contexts

Content is the psychological knowledge. Context is where we apply that knowledge.

Content = the psychological knowledge you learn (biological, cognitive, sociocultural)

Context = the real-world area where that knowledge is applied

Content and context are different things. The same content can be applied within different contexts.

  • Health and Well-being
  • Human Development
  • Human Relationships
  • Learning and Cognition

Each context provides a different real-world area where psychological content is applied.

Consider biological content about neurotransmission.

In Health and Well-being, this might explain how antidepressants work.

In Human Development, this might explain how the adolescent brain develops.

Same content, different context.

What is the difference between content and context?
Content is the psychological knowledge (what we know). Context is where that knowledge is applied (the real-world situation). The same biological, cognitive, or sociocultural knowledge can be applied within any of the four contexts.

Context — Health and Well-being

How psychology helps us understand and improve mental and physical health.

  • Why do psychological disorders develop?
  • How can behaviour affect health?
  • Why do interventions work for some people and not others?
  • How can health-related behaviour be changed?

This context explores stress, therapy, resilience, emotional regulation, and prevention strategies.

Why do some people develop anxiety disorders while others facing similar stressors do not?

A biological explanation might focus on genetics and brain chemistry. A cognitive explanation might focus on thinking patterns. A sociocultural explanation might focus on social support and environment.

Can psychology actually improve health outcomes?
Yes. Cognitive-behavioural therapy, stress management techniques, mindfulness, and health behaviour interventions have all been shown to improve mental and physical health. Psychology provides evidence-based tools for improving well-being.

Context — Human Development

How people grow and change across the lifespan.

  • How does the brain mature?
  • How does thinking change as children develop?
  • How do early relationships shape who we become?
  • What role do peers and culture play in development?

This context explores brain maturation, neuroplasticity, Theory of Mind, attachment, self-concept, and cultural influences.

How much of the person you become comes from maturation, and how much comes from experience?

This nature-nurture question runs through the entire Human Development context. Biological maturation creates potential; experience and culture shape how that potential is realised.

Why is "change" particularly important in Human Development?
Because this context specifically studies how psychological processes change across the lifespan. Unlike other contexts that might study behaviour at a single point, development focuses on change over time — from childhood through adulthood.

Context — Human Relationships

How we interact with, influence, and connect to others.

  • Why do people conform to group pressure?
  • How do relationships form and break down?
  • What causes conflict between individuals and groups?
  • How do communication and culture shape relationships?

This context explores groups, social identity, conformity, relationships, conflict, and communication.

Consider: you are in a group where everyone expresses an opinion you disagree with. Would you speak up or go along with the group?

Research on conformity, social identity, and group dynamics helps explain why people often align with group opinions — even when they privately disagree.

How does Human Relationships differ from the sociocultural content area?
Sociocultural is a content area (the psychological knowledge). Human Relationships is a context (where that knowledge is applied). You use sociocultural theories to understand relationships, but the context provides the specific real-world focus.

Context — Learning and Cognition

How we learn, think, and adapt in different environments.

  • How do we learn new information?
  • Why do we forget things?
  • How do schemas shape what we notice and remember?
  • How do cognitive biases affect decision-making?

This context explores learning, memory, schemas, decision-making, cognitive biases, and conditioning.

Consider: you read a textbook chapter once and feel you understand it. A week later, you can barely remember it.

Cognitive psychology explains why: shallow processing leads to poor retention. Deep processing — connecting new information to what you already know — leads to stronger memories.

Is learning more about memorising or understanding?
Understanding is more durable and transferable than rote memorisation. Deep processing, connecting new information to existing knowledge, and applying concepts to new situations lead to better long-term learning.

Contexts and Their Class Practicals

Each context has an associated research method you will practise.

The interview method involves in-depth exploration of individual experiences, perspectives, and feelings.

Why it fits: Health and well-being often requires understanding personal experiences of stress, illness, or therapy that cannot be captured through numbers alone.

What you will do: Conduct structured or semi-structured interviews exploring health-related psychological questions.

The observation method involves systematically watching and recording behaviour in natural or controlled settings.

Why it fits: Development often requires watching how people behave at different ages or in different situations, rather than asking them to report on their own development.

What you will do: Conduct systematic observations of behaviour in developmental contexts.

The survey/questionnaire method involves gathering standardised data from large groups of people.

Why it fits: Relationships and social behaviour often need to be measured across large groups to identify patterns, norms, and cultural variations.

What you will do: Design and distribute surveys exploring relationship-related questions.

The experiment method involves manipulating variables to establish cause-and-effect relationships.

Why it fits: Learning and cognitive processes are well-suited to experimental manipulation — you can control conditions and measure effects on memory, attention, or decision-making.

What you will do: Design and conduct experiments investigating cognitive processes.

Why does each context use a different class practical?
Different psychological questions require different types of evidence. Interviews capture depth. Observations capture natural behaviour. Surveys capture breadth. Experiments capture causality. Each method is chosen because it best fits the kind of question the context explores.

Concepts x Content x Contexts

How the architecture of the 2027 IB Psychology course fits together.

The 2027 IB Psychology course has three dimensions:

  • CONCEPTS — How we think critically about psychological knowledge
  • CONTENT — The psychological knowledge we learn
  • CONTEXTS — Where that knowledge is applied

These three dimensions intersect. Every psychological question can be examined through all three.

Content: Cognitive (How does social media affect memory and attention?)

Context: Health and Well-being (How does social media affect mental health?)

Concept: Causality (Does social media actually cause poorer mental health, or is it just correlated?)

Now consider: what if we change the content?

  • Content: Biological → How does social media affect brain development?
  • Content: Sociocultural → How do social norms around social media shape behaviour?

Changing one dimension changes the psychological question.

How do concepts, content, and contexts work together?
Content provides the knowledge. Contexts provide the real-world application. Concepts provide the critical thinking tools. Together, they form a complete psychological investigation: you use concepts to critically evaluate content knowledge applied within specific contexts.

HL Psychology — What Is Different?

HL ONLY Additional content and skills for Higher Level students.

HL students study the same:

  • Concepts (all six)
  • Content (biological, cognitive, sociocultural)
  • Contexts (all four)
  • Class practicals (all four)

But they go further through three HL extensions:

  • Culture — How cultural factors influence psychological processes
  • Motivation — What drives human behaviour
  • Technology — How technology affects psychology

HL students also develop additional skills in:

  • Interpreting psychological research
  • Analysing quantitative and qualitative data
  • Applying HL extension understanding to unfamiliar material

These skills are assessed in Paper 3, which is exclusive to HL.

If you are studying HL, what additional commitment does this involve?
HL requires more study time, deeper engagement with research methods and data analysis, and understanding of three additional extension topics (culture, motivation, technology). The additional Paper 3 tests these deeper skills.

How IB Psychology Is Assessed

An overview of the assessment structure for SL and HL.

  • Paper 1: Understanding Behaviour (35%)
  • Paper 2: Applying Psychology in Contexts (35%)
  • Internal Assessment: Research Proposal (30%)
  • Paper 1: Understanding Behaviour (20%)
  • Paper 2: Applying Psychology in Contexts (25%)
  • Paper 3: Research Data Analysis (30%) — HL only
  • Internal Assessment: Research Proposal (25%)
What is the most important thing to notice about this structure?
Paper 1 and Paper 2 together account for 70% of the SL grade. For HL, Paper 3 adds another dimension. The Internal Assessment (research proposal) is significant for both. No single assessment dominates — you need to develop skills across all areas.

What does Paper 1 actually reward?
Paper 1 rewards understanding, application, and conceptual thinking — not simply memorising research studies. You need to understand content, apply it to new situations, and think critically using the six core concepts.

This is not about memorising studies. It is about thinking like a researcher.

Papers 1 and 2 test content knowledge and application. Paper 3 tests data analysis and research interpretation skills. It requires you to work with actual research findings rather than recalling knowledge from memory.

Internal Assessment

Your research proposal — connecting practical skills to independent research.

The IA is required for both SL and HL students. You produce a research proposal — you do not conduct the actual experiment.

Your proposal should include:

  • Aim: A clear research question
  • Method: Detailed description of how you would investigate the question
  • Ethical considerations: How you would protect participants
  • Expected results: What you would predict and why

HL students go into more depth in their analysis and methodology.

  • Designing interviews, observations, surveys, and experiments
  • Understanding ethical requirements
  • Evaluating research quality
  • Applying methodological knowledge

The IA is where you demonstrate that you can design psychological research independently.

Let's Get Started

A final challenge to see how well you understand the course architecture.

A school notices that students who sleep less tend to report more anxiety and perform worse on memory tests.

Consider this scenario from multiple angles:

  • Which content areas could help explain this? (biological: brain function; cognitive: memory processes; sociocultural: school environment)
  • Which context might it fit? (Health and Well-being: anxiety and sleep; Learning and Cognition: memory and academic performance)
  • Which IB concepts could help evaluate the claim? (causality: does sleep loss cause anxiety?; measurement: how were anxiety and memory measured?; bias: who was studied?)
  • What research method might help investigate it? (experiment: manipulate sleep and measure effects; survey: measure sleep and anxiety across a large group)

Multiple defensible answers exist — that is the point.

ASK QUESTIONS → GATHER EVIDENCE → EXPLAIN BEHAVIOUR → QUESTION THE EXPLANATION

Psychology is not about memorising facts. It is about learning how to investigate and explain human behaviour — and then questioning those explanations using the six core concepts.
