Human Relationships

Survey / Questionnaire Practical

In this practical, you will design and conduct a questionnaire investigating a safe question connected to human relationships. You will operationalize what you measure, design effective items and response formats, pilot and revise your questionnaire, collect standardized responses from a sample, analyse the results, evaluate your methodology and use your experience to prepare for Paper 2.

Context: Human Relationships Research Method: Survey / Questionnaire
Possible formats: Closed questions Open questions Multiple choice Rating / Likert-type scales Frequency responses

STEP 2

Write Your Research Question / Aim

A good questionnaire research question should:

  • be clear
  • be focused
  • relate to Human Relationships
  • be measurable using questionnaire responses
  • be ethically appropriate
  • be realistic for the sample available
  • be careful about causal claims
Strong example "What are DP students' attitudes toward collaborative group work?"

These weak examples need improving. Check each one to see the problem and a better version.

Weak: "Is group work good?"
Problem: vague and broad — what does "good" even mean?
Better: "What are DP students' attitudes toward collaborative group work?"
Weak: "Does working in groups cause students to make more friends?"
Problem: claims causation that a questionnaire alone cannot test.
Better: "How do students describe how group work affects getting to know classmates?"
Weak: "Have you ever been betrayed by a close friend?"
Problem: requests sensitive, private relationship information that is not needed.
Better: "What do students say they value in close friendships?"

4What Exactly Are You Measuring?

Operationalization matters in a questionnaire just as much as in an observation — but it is applied differently. Questionnaires measure constructs: things that cannot be seen directly, such as attitudes, beliefs or perceptions.

"Measure friendship" is too vague. "Measure perceived group belonging using responses to five rating-scale statements" is much clearer.

Strong example CONSTRUCT: Perceived group belonging
Possible items: "I feel included when working with my classmates." / "I feel comfortable contributing ideas during group tasks." / "I feel that other members listen to my ideas."
Response scale: 1 Strongly disagree — 2 Disagree — 3 Neither agree nor disagree — 4 Agree — 5 Strongly agree

From construct to measure

Construct Perceived group belonging
↓
Questionnaire Items "I feel included when working with my classmates." …
↓
Responses 1 Strongly disagree … 5 Strongly agree
↓
Measure A belonging score across the items

Measurable or Too Vague?

Classify each description as measurable (it turns a construct into specific items and responses) or too vague (the construct has no defined items or scale). You will get instant feedback.

"Measure friendship."
Too vague — "friendship" is a construct; you must decide how responses will represent it.
"Measure perceived group belonging using responses to five rating-scale statements."
Measurable — the construct is turned into specific items and a response scale.
"Find out whether students get along with each other."
Too vague — "get along" has no defined items or response options.
"Measure how often students collaborate using a frequency scale (never … very often)."
Measurable — behaviour is turned into a defined frequency question.
"Measure how supportive the school is."
Too vague — "supportive" needs items and a scale before it means anything.
"Measure attitudes toward teamwork using agreement responses to five statements."
Measurable — attitudes are defined with statements and a scale.
Your Practical Notes

In your own document, record what you want to measure and how your questionnaire responses will represent that construct — the items and the response format.

5Design Good Questionnaire Items

Well-designed items measure what you intend. Poorly designed items contaminate the data. Avoid these pitfalls:

  • Leading questions — wording that pushes a particular answer
  • Loaded questions — wording that assumes or implies a controversial judgement
  • Double-barrelled questions — two separate things asked as one
  • Vague questions — unclear what information is wanted
  • Assumptive questions — wording that assumes an answer (e.g. "why do you avoid…?")
  • Overly personal questions — requests for private information not needed for the aim
  • Ambiguous response options — options that overlap or could mean two things

Fix the question

Each item below has one of the problems above. Check it to see what is wrong and a better version.

"Don't you agree that group projects help students make friends?"
Problem: leading — it pushes participants towards "yes".
Better: "To what extent do you agree that group projects help students get to know classmates?"
"How satisfied are you with your friends and teachers?"
Problem: double-barrelled — it asks about two different relationships.
Better: split into two separate items — one about friends, one about teachers.
"How much do you enjoy your pointless group projects?"
Problem: loaded — it assumes the projects are pointless.
Better: "How much, if at all, do you enjoy group projects?"
"How often do you talk to your classmates?"
Problem: vague — which classmates, and about what?
Better: "How often do you talk about schoolwork with classmates outside lessons? (Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Very often)"
"Why do you prefer working alone?"
Problem: assumptive — it assumes they prefer working alone.
Better: "Do you prefer working in a group, alone, or neither?"
"How often do you help classmates? (Sometimes / Quite often / Very often)"
Problem: ambiguous response options — the options overlap and have no clear boundaries.
Better: "How often did you help a classmate with schoolwork last month? (Never / 1–2 times / 3–5 times / More than 5 times)"
Your Practical Notes

As you draft items in your own document, test every item against the pitfall list above. If an item needs a "don't you agree…", rephrase it.

6Choose the Right Response Format

The response format decides what kind of data each item produces. Match the format to what you need:

  • Closed questions — fixed options, quick to answer and count.
  • Open questions — free-text answers that give richer, individual responses.
  • Multiple choice — select one (or more) of the listed options.
  • Rating scale — a numbered scale such as 1–5, sometimes with labelled ends.
  • Likert-type scale — rate agreement with a statement (Strongly disagree … Strongly agree).
  • Frequency response — how often something happens (Never … Very often).

Which response format works best?

For each research need below, choose the better response format. You will get instant feedback.

You want to measure agreement with: "I enjoy collaborative learning."
Agreement with a statement is measured with a Likert-type agreement scale.
You want participants to explain one reason they prefer group or individual work.
A reason needs an open response — a frequency scale cannot capture it.
You want to know how often participants worked with classmates last week.
How often something happens is a frequency question, not an agreement question.
You want participants to pick the option that best describes them: work alone / in pairs / in a small group.
A fixed set of categories calls for multiple choice.
You want to rate how comfortable participants feel contributing ideas during group tasks.
Rating scales capture degrees, which a yes/no choice cannot.

7Design Rating Scales Carefully

The response scale itself can create problems. A balanced, clearly labelled scale should look like this:

Good balanced scale 1 = Strongly disagree  ·  2 = Disagree  ·  3 = Neither agree nor disagree  ·  4 = Agree  ·  5 = Strongly agree
What is wrong with this scale? — Excellent / Very good / Good / Okay
It is unbalanced toward positive responses — there is no negative half, and "Okay" sits at the bottom instead of the middle. Participants can only report positive-to-mildly-positive views.

Good scales have:

  • clearly labelled endpoints
  • logical ordering
  • consistent scale direction
  • balanced options
  • an appropriate neutral option where justified

Good scale or bad scale?

Classify each scale as good or bad. You will get instant feedback.

"1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neither agree nor disagree, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly agree"
Balanced, clearly labelled and logically ordered.
"1 = Agree, 2 = Strongly agree, 3 = Disagree, 4 = Strongly disagree"
The direction flips mid-scale — an option that reads as "bad" comes right after "strongly agree".
"1 = Brilliant, 2 = Great, 3 = Fine, 4 = Not great"
Unbalanced toward positive wording — there is no real negative range.
"1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Sometimes, 5 = Always"
Two options mean the same thing ("Sometimes") — ambiguous and unbalanced.
"1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Often, 5 = Very often"
Logically ordered, each option distinct, single direction.

9Pilot Your QuestionnaireOUR CLASS REQUIREMENT

A pilot is a small trial run before collecting the final data. Its purpose is to find problems — before the problems reach your real sample.

Ask your pilot participants to test:

  • Are the instructions clear?
  • Are any questions confusing?
  • Are any response options missing?
  • Does the scale make sense?
  • Is anything ambiguous?
  • Does the questionnaire take a reasonable amount of time?
  • Does any question make participants uncomfortable?
  • Are the responses likely to provide useful data?

Pilot Detective

Each pilot below has four possible actions. Judge each action as the best action or not the best. You will get instant feedback.

Pilot 1: Three pilot participants ask what "regularly" means in one item.

Option B: Clarify / operationalize the wording.
Correct — ambiguity found in the pilot should be fixed before the real run.
Option A: Ignore them.
Not the best — the confusion is real data and will confuse others too.
Option C: Remove those participants.
Not the best — the problem is with the item, not with the participants.
Option D: Tell participants how to answer the item on the day.
Not the best — explaining on the day makes answers inconsistent and biased.

Pilot 2: Most pilot participants take 12 minutes, even though the class setting allows about 8.

Option A: Shorten or simplify the questionnaire.
Correct — the pilot shows the questionnaire is too long for the setting.
Option B: Ignore the timing — it does not matter.
Not the best — if it cannot be completed in the setting, the real run will fail.
Option C: Tell participants to hurry up.
Not the best — rushing changes how carefully people answer.
Option D: Drop the items that take the longest, regardless of their importance to the aim.
Not the best — cut items that are weak or unnecessary, not just the slow ones.

Pilot 3: Two pilot participants write a comment that does not fit any of the response options.

Option A: Add or adjust the response options so the comments fit.
Correct — the pilot reveals what the response options are missing.
Option B: Ignore the comments — the options were planned in advance.
Not the best — the options clearly do not match how participants think.
Option C: Tell those participants they used the wrong option.
Not the best — if the option does not fit, the problem is the option.
Option D: Turn that item into an open question for everyone.
Not the best — making everything open weakens the standardization a questionnaire provides.

Pilot 4: Pilot participants find one question uncomfortable.

Option A: Remove or reword the question.
Correct — anything that makes participants uncomfortable has no place in a school questionnaire.
Option B: Keep it anyway — it is only one question.
Not the best — discomfort matters even for a single question.
Option C: Make the questionnaire anonymous and keep the question anyway.
Not the best — anonymity does not make an uncomfortable question appropriate.
Option D: Ask only the most outgoing participants to answer it.
Not the best — choosing who answers biases the data and removes the question from proper review.
Your Practical Notes

After piloting, record in your own document: what problem did you find? what did you change? why did you change it?

10Participants and Sampling

IB

IB Sample Guidance

  • Minimum sample: 10 participants.

Apply what you already know about sampling. Make these decisions in order:

  1. Target population — the wider group your question is about (e.g. all DP students at your school).
  2. Actual sample — who you will actually ask (minimum 10 participants).
  3. Sampling technique — how those participants were chosen (e.g. opportunity, volunteer, convenience).
  4. Possible sampling bias — what this choice could miss or over-represent.

Sampling decision

Classify each sampling decision as a good choice or poor choice. You will get instant feedback.

A student distributes the questionnaire to two full classes that are easiest to reach, and records this as an opportunity sample.
Good — pragmatic and clearly labelled as an opportunity sample.
A student claims the opportunity sample represents all DP students at the school.
Overclaiming — a convenience sample rarely represents a whole population.
A student invites volunteers from three grade levels and notes that the sample is volunteer-based.
Good — the technique is stated honestly, including its bias.
A student keeps only the responses they "feel like" using and drops the rest.
Selection bias — dropping responses without a rule distorts the data.
A student posts the questionnaire link in a public group chat and lets anyone respond more than once.
Uncontrolled administration — the sample is unidentifiable and duplicates are possible.
Your Practical Notes

In your own document, record:

  • your target population
  • your actual sample (minimum 10 participants)
  • your sampling technique
  • why it is practical / appropriate
  • one likely limitation or bias

11STOP: Ethics & ApprovalIB GUIDANCE

Complete this before collecting data. The IB ethical guidance below applies to every practical — including questionnaires.

Informed Consent Participants must explicitly agree to participate after receiving appropriate information about the investigation.
Right to Withdraw Participants may stop participating and withdraw their data.
Confidentiality & Anonymity Protect identifying and personal information.
Protection From Harm The investigation must not create unnecessary anxiety, stress, pain or discomfort.
Data Protection Research data must be securely handled and should not be retained unnecessarily.
Debriefing Participants should receive appropriate information following participation, particularly where information was withheld beforehand.

A questionnaire does not change the ethics rules. Students must not assume: "It's anonymous, so consent doesn't matter."

Ethical or Not?

Classify each scenario as ethical or not ethical. You will get instant feedback.

The questionnaire is anonymous, so the researcher distributes it without telling participants what the study involves.
Not ethical — anonymous data does not remove the need for appropriate informed consent.
Participants are told the topic, that participation is voluntary, and that they can skip items or stop at any time.
Ethical — informed consent and voluntary participation, with the right to skip and withdraw.
Participants write their full name at the top so the researcher can "follow up later", even though names are not needed for the aim.
Not ethical — collecting unnecessary identifying information.
Responses are collected without names and stored where only the researcher can access them.
Ethical — confidentiality, anonymity and secure data handling.
The researcher announces "everyone has to complete this — it is part of the lesson" and watches as it is filled in.
Not ethical — coercion and the pressure of being watched.
The questionnaire asks nothing sensitive or personal, and consent is obtained before distribution.
Ethical — a safe topic with consent handled properly.

16Do Not Confuse Description With Causation

Questionnaire data can describe reported attitudes, beliefs, perceptions, experiences and behaviours. But a questionnaire alone does not normally establish cause and effect.

Supported or Overclaim?

Classify each statement as supported (a fair summary of the questionnaire data) or an overclaim (it goes beyond what the data can show). You will get instant feedback.

"Seven of the twelve participants selected Agree or Strongly Agree."
A faithful, specific summary of the collected responses.
"Collaborative work causes students to form better relationships."
A questionnaire describes what participants reported — it cannot demonstrate causation.
"Most participants in this sample reported positive attitudes toward collaborative work."
Describes this sample's reports without generalising beyond it.
"All teenagers prefer collaborative work."
Generalizes beyond the sample and beyond what a questionnaire can show.
A questionnaire can describe reported attitudes, beliefs, perceptions, experiences and behaviours. It does not, on its own, show what caused them.

17Evaluate Your Questionnaire

Evaluate your questionnaire against considerations specific to this method:

  • Question wording — could the wording have influenced answers?
  • Response bias — could participants have answered in a way that does not reflect their true views?
  • Social desirability bias — could participants have given answers they think are more acceptable?
  • Sampling bias — does the sample represent the target population?
  • Measurement — did the questions actually measure the intended construct?
  • Reliability — would similar questions / procedures produce reasonably consistent responses?
  • Validity — are the responses a meaningful measure of what the investigation claims to investigate?
  • Ethics — were consent, privacy and participant rights protected?

Spot the Methodological Problem

Each scenario below is either a methodological problem or not a problem. You will get instant feedback.

Item: "Don't you agree that group work helps you make friends?"
Problem — leading wording pushes participants toward agreement.
A "how helpful are you?" item produces almost all "extremely helpful" answers.
Problem — responses look shaped by social desirability bias.
The researcher distributes the questionnaire only to friends in their own class, then generalizes to all DP students.
Problem — sampling bias; the conclusions go beyond the sample.
The scale offers "Sometimes" twice, with different meanings in each place.
Problem — ambiguous response options that participants cannot distinguish.
To measure "group belonging", the researcher asks whether participants like the school buildings.
Problem — questionable construct validity; the item does not measure the construct.
The researcher pilots the questionnaire, finds the confusing items, and fixes them before the final distribution.
Not a problem — piloting and revising is exactly right.
