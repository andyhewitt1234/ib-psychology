Human Relationships

Survey / Questionnaire Practical

In this practical you will investigate a safe Human Relationships topic using a questionnaire, analyze your responses, and reflect on your methodology for Paper 2.

1. Overview & Research Question 2. Design Your Questionnaire 3. Pilot, Revise & Sample 4. Ethics & Data Collection 5. Analyze Your Results 6. Reflect & Paper 2 7. Final Submission

1Overview & Research Question

Teacher-created requirements are never presented as official IB requirements.

From the IB

  • Complete a survey / questionnaire class practical as one of the four required research methods.
  • Human Relationships is the recommended context for the survey / questionnaire practical.
  • In this context, "survey" and "questionnaire" may be used interchangeably.
  • For a student-led survey/questionnaire practical: minimum sample size of 10 participants.
  • Appropriate ethical requirements must be followed: informed consent, voluntary participation, the right to withdraw, confidentiality/anonymity, and protection from harm.
  • Keep a brief record of the practical — aim, procedure, sampling, findings and ethical considerations.

Our class requirements

  • Write a research question connected to Human Relationships and operationalize what you are measuring.
  • Design your questionnaire, pilot it, record the problems you found, and revise it.
  • Collect anonymized responses from your sample and build a results table.
  • Complete the practical reflection and submit the required evidence to the teacher.

Choose a safe topic

Safe topics for a school questionnaire include:

  • how students perceive their sense of belonging in groups
  • whether students prefer working in a group or individually
  • how often students collaborate with classmates outside lessons
  • students' perceptions of teamwork
  • students' attitudes toward cooperation
  • how students describe everyday experiences of social support

Avoid abuse, bullying, sexual relationships, or private family information. These are sensitive or safeguarding matters — never school questionnaire topics.

Write your research question

Strong exampleResearch question: What are DP students' attitudes toward collaborative group work? This works because the topic is safe, focused, and can be investigated using questionnaire responses.
Weak example → better versionWeak: Does working in groups cause students to make more friends? Problem: A questionnaire cannot establish whether group work causes friendships to develop. Better: What do DP students report about getting to know classmates during group activities?
Your taskWrite your research question and explain what information your questionnaire needs to collect to answer it.

2Design Your Questionnaire

A. Operationalization

Operationalization means deciding how questionnaire responses will represent the concept you want to investigate. For example, perceived group belonging could be measured using several statements about inclusion, participation, and feeling listened to.

ExampleConstruct: Perceived group belonging
Possible items: "I feel included when working with my classmates." / "I feel comfortable contributing ideas during group tasks." / "I feel that other members listen to my ideas."
Response scale: 1 Strongly disagree — 2 Disagree — 3 Neither agree nor disagree — 4 Agree — 5 Strongly agree

One participant responds 4, 3, and 5 to the three belonging items above.

Step 1 — Add the scores: 4 + 3 + 5 = 12.

Step 2 — Calculate the average: 12 ÷ 3 = 4.

Interpretation: This participant's average belonging score is 4 out of 5 across these three items. A higher score indicates stronger reported belonging as measured by these items. It does not prove that the participant objectively belongs more than another participant.

Items should measure the same construct and use compatible response scales before being combined. If negatively worded items are included, they may require reverse scoring. A classroom-created score is not automatically a validated psychological measure, so only combine items that are suitable, and explain whether you will analyse individual questions or combine responses into a score.

B. Writing effective questions

Check every item against these six problems:

Problem Example Improvement
Leading Don't you agree that group work is helpful? How helpful, if at all, do you find group work?
Loaded How much do you enjoy pointless group projects? How much, if at all, do you enjoy group projects?
Double-barreled How satisfied are you with your friends and teachers? Ask about friends and teachers separately.
Vague How often do you talk to classmates? How often did you discuss schoolwork with classmates outside lessons last week?
Assumptive Why do you prefer working alone? Do you prefer working alone, in a group, or neither?
Overlapping options 1–3 times / 3–5 times 1–2 times / 3–5 times

Avoid unnecessarily personal questions. If an item is not needed to answer your research question, remove it.

C. Response formats

Format Best used for
Multiple choice Selecting from defined categories
Agreement scale Measuring agreement with a statement
Frequency scale Reporting how often something happens
Open response Explaining an experience or opinion in the participant's own words

Response options must be clear, appropriately balanced, and non-overlapping — each participant should find exactly one option that fits their answer.

Example — five-point agreement scale1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neither agree nor disagree, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly agree

D. Worked questionnaire

Teacher-created classroom example: not an official IB questionnaire or a validated psychological scale.

Research question: What are DP students' attitudes toward collaborative group work?

Participant instructions

This short questionnaire asks about your experiences of classroom group work. Participation is voluntary. You may skip any question or stop participating. Do not write your name. Your answers will be used only for this classroom investigation and reported in summary form. Please read each statement and select the response that best represents your view.

Consent

Before beginning, participants should receive the relevant study information and explicitly agree to participate. The researcher must follow the teacher-approved consent procedure.

Response options for Questions 1–5

1 = Strongly disagree
2 = Disagree
3 = Neither agree nor disagree
4 = Agree
5 = Strongly agree

Questionnaire items

  1. I enjoy working collaboratively with classmates.
  2. I feel comfortable sharing my ideas during group activities.
  3. I feel included when working in a group.
  4. Working with classmates helps me understand lesson content.
  5. I would like more opportunities for collaborative classroom activities.

These items examine several aspects of students' attitudes toward group work. They should initially be analysed individually because enjoyment, participation, inclusion, perceived usefulness, and preference are related but not identical constructs.

The questionnaire does not establish whether group work actually improves learning or causes stronger relationships.

After the questionnaire

Participants should receive the teacher-approved debrief information and know whom to contact if they have questions about the investigation.

Your taskCreate your questionnaire with participant instructions, relevant questions, and clearly labeled response options. Every question must help answer your research question.

3Pilot, Revise & Choose Your Sample

A pilot is a small trial of your questionnaire before the main investigation. It helps you identify confusing wording, unsuitable response options, and practical problems. You need teacher approval and appropriate consent before involving pilot participants.

Worked examplePilot problem: Two participants do not understand what "regularly" means. Revision: Replace "regularly" with a defined time period and clear response options. Reason: Participants are more likely to interpret the question consistently.
Your taskRecord at least one problem identified during your pilot, the change you made, and why you made it. If no problem was identified, explain what you checked and why you retained the questionnaire.

Choose your sample

Opportunity sampling: approaching people who are readily available and eligible, such as students accessible during an approved classroom session. Volunteer sampling: participants choose to take part after seeing an invitation. Both can introduce sampling bias: an opportunity sample may over-represent easily accessible students, while a volunteer sample may over-represent students particularly interested in the topic. Asking available classmates during an approved lesson is opportunity sampling; recruiting only students who respond to a posted invitation is volunteer sampling.

IB guidance for a student-led questionnaire practical: minimum sample size of 10 participants.

Your taskIdentify your target population, intended sample, sampling technique, and one limitation of your sampling decision.

Before distributing the final questionnaire, show your teacher your research question, revised questionnaire, pilot notes, sampling plan, and ethical arrangements. Do not collect final data until approved.

4Ethics & Data Collection

Open Debrief Form → REQUEST FOR PERMISSION Used where teacher / guardian permission is needed. Open Permission Form →

Meet these ethical requirements before collecting data:

  • informed consent after receiving appropriate study information
  • voluntary participation and the right to withdraw
  • confidentiality and anonymity of responses
  • protection from harm
  • secure handling of data, deleted when no longer needed

If questionnaire responses are genuinely anonymous, participants may not be able to withdraw an individual response after submitting it because the researcher cannot identify which response belongs to them. Explain this limitation before participants agree to take part, and follow your teacher-approved consent procedure.

Collecting your data

Before

Obtain approval and consent, prepare the final questionnaire, and confirm the sampling plan.

During

Give consistent instructions, avoid influencing responses, and respect participants' right to decline or skip questions.

After

Debrief participants, secure the responses, and remove unnecessary identifying information.

5Analyze Your Results

Step 1 — Organize your responses

Create a results table for each closed question. Count how many participants selected each response option.

Step 2 — Calculate percentages

Divide the number choosing an option by the total number of valid responses to that question, then multiply by 100.

Worked exampleItem: "I enjoy working collaboratively with classmates."
Response scale: 1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neither agree nor disagree, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly agree
Results (12 participants): 1 → 1 participant; 2 → 2; 3 → 2; 4 → 4; 5 → 3
Response Scale Frequency (n = 12) Percentage
1 — Strongly disagree 1 1 8%
2 — Disagree 2 2 17%
3 — Neither agree nor disagree 3 2 17%
4 — Agree 4 4 33%
5 — Strongly agree 5 3 25%
Agreement (4 + 5) — 7 58%

Step 3 — Identify a pattern

Look for the most common responses, differences between response categories, or other patterns that directly answer your research question.

Model statementSeven of the twelve participants (approximately 58%) agreed or strongly agreed that they enjoyed collaborative group work.

Step 4 — Interpret cautiously

Explain what the pattern suggests about your sample. Do not claim that the questionnaire proves causation or represents everyone.

Model interpretationPositive attitudes toward collaborative group work were more common than negative attitudes in this sample. However, the questionnaire measures reported enjoyment, not whether group work improves learning. The sample is small and its representativeness has not been established, so the findings should not be generalized to all DP students.

If you included open questions, group similar responses into meaningful categories and use short anonymized quotations as evidence.

Your taskCreate results tables for your questionnaire, identify the main patterns, and explain what they suggest about your research question.

6Reflect & Prepare for Paper 2

REFLECTION WORKSHEET The IB reflection worksheet for this class practical. Open Reflection Worksheet → RESEARCH COMPARISON WORKSHEET The IB research comparison worksheet. Open Research Comparison Worksheet →

Complete the reflection worksheet using your actual investigation: research question, procedure, sample, findings, strengths, limitations, ethics, and what you learned.

One limitation of the questionnaire is that participants may have exaggerated how much they enjoy collaborative group work because they believed positive attitudes would be viewed favourably by their teacher or classmates. This could have increased the number of agreement responses, making students' attitudes appear more positive than they actually were. To reduce this risk, the researcher could emphasize that there are no correct answers, ensure responses are not identifiable, and arrange for questionnaires to be completed privately. These measures may reduce social desirability bias, although they cannot eliminate it completely.

  • Limitation: Participants may give socially desirable answers.
  • Consequence: Positive attitudes may be overestimated.
  • Improvement: Strengthen privacy, neutrality and reassurance about honest responses.
  • Remaining uncertainty: The improvement reduces the risk but does not guarantee truthful responses.

Base your evaluation on what actually happened in your investigation.
