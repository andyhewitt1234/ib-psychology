Health & Well-being

Interview Practical

In this practical, you will design and conduct an interview on a safe Health & Well-being question, analyse the responses, evaluate your methodology and use the experience to prepare for Paper 2.

1. Overview 2. Plan Your Interview 3. Ethics & Data Collection 4. Analyze Your Data 5. Reflect & Compare 6. Submit & Paper 2

1Overview

Context: Health & Well-being. Method: interview (structured, semi-structured or focus group). Your goal is to explore people's experiences, views or behaviours relating to a safe Health & Well-being topic, then analyse what the responses suggest.

It matters which requirements come from the IB and which are our classroom procedures. Teacher-created requirements are never presented as official IB requirements.

From the IB

  • Complete an interview / focus-group class practical as one of the four required research methods.
  • Health & Well-being is the recommended context for the interview practical.
  • Interviews may be structured, semi-structured or focus groups.
  • For student-led practicals: an individual interview needs a minimum of 1 participant; a focus group needs approximately 3–8 participants.
  • Ethical guidelines must be followed: explicit informed consent, participants informed about the study, voluntary participation, the right to withdraw, confidentiality/anonymity, and protection from harm.
  • Keep a brief record of the practical — aim, procedure, sampling, findings and ethical considerations.

Our class requirements

  • Individual interviews should normally last approximately 15-20 minutes.
  • Produce an anonymized working transcript or a detailed interview record.
  • Code / highlight relevant responses and identify key themes.
  • Complete the practical reflection and submit the required evidence to the teacher.

By the end of this practical you should be able to

  • formulate an appropriate research question and choose a fitting interview format
  • create an interview schedule and make ethical research decisions
  • select participant(s), conduct the interview and create an anonymized record
  • identify themes in qualitative data, evaluate your decisions and connect the experience to Paper 2

One rule never changes: research questions here must stay safe, ethical and appropriate for a school practical. If a topic would need a professional or clinical setting, it is not right for this practical.

2Plan Your Interview

Work through the steps below in order. Do not collect any data until your teacher has checked and approved your plan.

  1. Choose a safe topic. It must relate to Health & Well-being and be explorable through interview data. Safe examples: how students manage everyday academic stress; strategies students use to improve sleep; how students describe the relationship between exercise and mood; how students use mindfulness or relaxation strategies; habits students believe support well-being. Not appropriate: diagnoses, self-harm, bullying, trauma, or private family matters.
  2. Write your research question. It should be clear, relate to Health & Well-being, explore experiences or perceptions, and be answerable with interview data. Strong example: "How do DP students describe the strategies they use to manage everyday academic stress?" Weak questions and fixes: "How bad is the stress in our school?" assumes everyone is stressed — better: "How do students describe the stress they experience during the school year?" "Do students who exercise sleep better than students who don't?" implies a comparison interviews cannot test — better: "How do students describe the relationship between exercise and their sleep?"
  3. Choose your interview type. Structured: the same predetermined questions in the same order, best when answers must be comparable. Semi-structured: prepared key questions with relevant follow-ups, best for exploring unexpected ideas. Focus group: several participants discuss the topic while you guide the conversation, best when you want participants to build on each other's ideas. Choose the format that best answers your question, and record one reason why it fits.
  4. Choose your participant(s). Decide in order: target population (e.g. DP students); participant / sample (an individual interview needs a minimum of 1 participant; a focus group needs approximately 3–8); sampling technique (e.g. opportunity, volunteer, snowball); and why this sample fits, including one limitation (e.g. it is not representative). More participants does not automatically make qualitative research better — depth comes from exploring a suitable sample thoroughly.
  5. Create your interview schedule. Prepare around five main questions plus neutral follow-ups such as "Can you tell me more about that?", "Can you give me an example?" and "Why do you think that is?" Avoid leading questions ("Don't you think exercise obviously makes students feel less stressed?" — better: "How, if at all, does exercise affect how you feel when you are stressed?"), loaded questions (drop the assumed judgment — "How, if at all, do you think social media affects young people's well-being?"), double-barrelled questions (split them in two), vague questions ("How do you cope?" — better: "What, if anything, do you do to manage stress in your daily routine?"), and anything requesting highly sensitive information. Open with something comfortable and easy to answer.
  6. Plan how you will record the interview. Either audio-record (only with the participant's explicit permission) or take detailed written notes during or immediately after. Never secretly record a participant. Use identifiers such as Participant A or P1 — never real names. If you record audio: explain what it is for, get permission before starting, use it only for the agreed purpose, store it securely and delete it when no longer needed.

Before you continue, your own document should contain: your topic, research question, interview type and why it fits, target population, intended participant(s), sampling technique with one limitation, and your interview schedule with follow-up prompts. Do not collect any data yet.

3Ethics & Data Collection

Complete the ethics requirements before collecting data. The IB ethical guidance below applies to every practical: participants must explicitly agree to participate after receiving appropriate information; participation is voluntary and they may withdraw their data; protect identifying and personal information (confidentiality/anonymity); the investigation must not create unnecessary anxiety, stress, pain or discomfort; handle data securely and do not retain it unnecessarily; and give participants appropriate information afterwards (debriefing), particularly where information was withheld beforehand.

Conducting the interview

Our class target is an individual interview of approximately 15-20 minutes. The IB does not prescribe an interview length.

Before: confirm consent is obtained, your schedule is ready, your recording or note-taking method is ready, the participant understands the right to withdraw, and the setting is appropriate. During: follow the schedule, listen carefully, remain neutral, use appropriate follow-ups, do not pressure the participant, respect the right not to answer, and protect confidentiality. After: thank and debrief the participant, secure the data, remove identifying information, and note important observations about how the interview went.

If you created an audio recording, use it only for producing or checking the interview record. Do not upload it, do not submit it, and delete it when it is no longer required.

4Analyze Your Data

Analysis turns raw responses into themes. Work through these four steps in order. Interview data shows what people report about their experiences — it does not demonstrate what causes what, and findings from a small school sample cannot be generalized.

  1. Prepare an anonymized transcript or detailed record. A working transcript does not need professional conventions — use a simple format and identifiers, leaving out anything unrelated to your research question. Example: INTERVIEWER: What strategies do you use when you feel stressed about school? PARTICIPANT A: I usually exercise or go for a walk because it helps me clear my head. Never include the participant's real name, unnecessary identifying information, or private information unrelated to the research question. You do not need every filler or pause, and you must not change the meaning of what was said.
  2. Highlight responses and assign descriptive codes. Read the responses carefully, more than once, mark the important or repeated ideas, and give each a short code. Example: responses "I go for a run." / "Exercise helps clear my head." / "I usually walk when I'm stressed." give the codes exercise, walking and stress relief.
  3. Group related codes into themes. Bring related codes together and name the broader pattern they point to. Example: the codes exercise and walking group into the theme PHYSICAL ACTIVITY AS A COPING STRATEGY. A second example: "I talk to my friend about it." / "Calling my mum helps me feel better." give codes such as talking and social support, grouping into the theme SOCIAL SUPPORT AS A STRESS-MANAGEMENT STRATEGY.
  4. Produce a findings table with evidence and cautious interpretation. Record each theme with real evidence and an interpretation that stays close to the data. Model findings table:
    Theme / Pattern Evidence From Responses What Might This Suggest?
    PHYSICAL ACTIVITY AS A COPING STRATEGY "I go for a run." / "Exercise helps clear my head." Participants report using movement to manage stress — a self-reported strategy, not evidence of a causal effect.
    SOCIAL SUPPORT AS A STRESS-MANAGEMENT STRATEGY "I talk to my friend about it." / "Calling my mum helps me feel better." Participants describe turning to others when stressed — a self-reported coping strategy.
    Build your own table with the same three columns. Base every theme on real evidence, let the data decide how many themes you report, and phrase interpretations cautiously: "participants reported…" rather than claims about all students or about causes.

5Reflect & Compare

REFLECTION WORKSHEET The IB reflection worksheet for this class practical. Open Reflection Worksheet → RESEARCH COMPARISON WORKSHEET The IB research comparison worksheet. Open Research Comparison Worksheet →

Reflection worksheet

IB recommends keeping a brief record of each class practical — it later helps with Paper 2 and with understanding research methodology. Complete the worksheet using your actual practical, covering its eight areas: context explored; aim; procedure; sample and sampling technique; findings; research considerations (strengths and limitations); ethical considerations; and reflection on what you learned about research and about yourself as a researcher. Be specific: name your real question, your real sampling decision and one real limitation, rather than writing in general terms. Submit your answers with your other evidence.

Research comparison worksheet

Consider how investigating the same question with another research method would change the evidence you obtain. Choose one alternative — experiment (controlled conditions and measured variables), observation (recording behaviour as it happens), or survey / questionnaire (self-report data from a sample) — and compare it with your interview across as many of these six aspects as you can: aim; procedure; sample / sampling; findings / data (e.g. rich qualitative answers versus countable scores); research considerations (strengths and limitations of each); and ethical considerations for each. You do not conduct another investigation; the comparison is written, and it directly prepares the Paper 2 skill of comparing research methods.

6Submit & Prepare for Paper 2

What to hand in

Submit these three items to your teacher according to the teacher's instructions: (1) completed reflection worksheet containing the key details of the investigation; (2) anonymized interview record — working transcript or detailed notes, with no real participant names and relevant highlighting / coding visible; (3) findings / themes table with the identified themes and supporting evidence. Do not submit raw audio recordings or unnecessary personal participant information. Name your files clearly.

Paper 2 preparation

Paper 2 Section A focuses on the research method used in the practical — not on testing the Health & Well-being topic itself. Prepare these four skills using your own practical:

  1. Describe your practical: state the aim, name the interview format you used, and briefly describe the procedure (participant, sampling, recordings or notes).
  2. Apply a concept: explain how a concept such as bias, measurement, perspective or responsibility relates to a real decision or moment in your practical — for example, interviewer bias in how a follow-up question was worded, or perspective in that self-reports capture only the participant's point of view.
  3. Compare methods: compare your interview with one other research method — for example, interviews give rich, detailed answers but are time-consuming per participant, while surveys reach more people quickly but with less depth.
  4. Design an alternative: outline another method for the same topic by naming the method, stating what you would measure, and noting how you would keep it ethical (informed consent, voluntary participation, anonymity).

Before you consider yourself ready, check that you can do all four from memory using your own practical as the example.
