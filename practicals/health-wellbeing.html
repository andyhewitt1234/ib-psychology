Health & Well-being

Interview Practical

In this practical, you will design and conduct an interview investigating a safe question connected to health and well-being. You will make real research decisions, collect qualitative data, analyse your findings, evaluate your methodology and use your experience to prepare for Paper 2.

Context: Health & Well-being Research Method: Interview
Possible formats: Structured Semi-structured Focus Group
IB vs Class 1. Overview 2. Plan 3. Ethics 4. Conduct 5. Workflow 6. Record 7. Analyse 8. Reflect 9. Compare 10. Submit 11. Paper 2

!IB Requirements vs Our Class Requirements

It matters which requirements come from the IB and which are our classroom procedures. Teacher-created requirements are never presented as official IB requirements.

From the IB

IB Requirements / Guidance

  • Complete an interview / focus-group class practical as one of the four required research methods.
  • Health & Well-being is the recommended context for the interview practical.
  • Interviews may be structured, semi-structured or focus groups.
  • For student-led practicals: an individual interview needs a minimum of 1 participant; a focus group needs approximately 3–8 participants.
  • Ethical guidelines must be followed: explicit informed consent, participants informed about the study, voluntary participation, the right to withdraw, confidentiality/anonymity, and protection from harm.
  • Keep a brief record of the practical — aim, procedure, sampling, findings and ethical considerations.
Our class

Our Class Requirements

  • Individual interviews should normally last approximately 8–12 minutes.
  • Produce an anonymized working transcript or a detailed interview record.
  • Code / highlight relevant responses.
  • Identify key themes / patterns in the responses.
  • Complete the practical reflection.
  • Submit the required practical evidence to the teacher.

STEP 1

Choose Your Topic

Your investigation must relate to Health & Well-being and must be ethically appropriate for a school practical. The five ideas below are examples — not compulsory questions. Click one (or more) to mark your interest.

Would this be appropriate?

Sort the example topics below: appropriate or not appropriate for a school interview practical. You will get instant feedback.

"How do students manage everyday academic stress?"
Managing everyday stress is a normal, low-risk experience and can be discussed safely in a school practical.
"What strategies do students use to improve their sleep?"
Sleep habits are everyday behaviour that students can describe safely, without any need for sensitive information.
"How do students describe the relationship between exercise and their mood?"
Broad, open and about everyday experience — phrased as an open question, this is safe to explore in interviews.
"How do students use mindfulness or relaxation strategies?"
Describing wellbeing strategies is everyday experience, well within the scope of a school practical.
"Have you ever been diagnosed with a mental health condition?"
This asks for a diagnosis — sensitive clinical information. Diagnosing people is not part of a school practical.
"Have you ever self-harmed?"
Self-harm is highly sensitive and a safeguarding matter. It must not be investigated in a school practical.
"Describe a time you were bullied and how it affected you."
Experiences of bullying are personal and potentially distressing — not an appropriate school practical topic.
"Describe a traumatic event you have experienced."
Trauma is highly sensitive and potentially re-traumatising. It is not appropriate for a school practical.
"Tell me about your most private worries about your family."
This requests highly sensitive personal information that is not needed to answer any school research question.

STEP 2

Write Your Research Question

A good interview research question should:

  • be clear
  • relate to Health & Well-being
  • explore experiences, perceptions, beliefs or behaviours
  • be answerable using interview data
  • avoid diagnosing people
  • be ethically appropriate
Strong example "How do DP students describe the strategies they use to manage everyday academic stress?"

These weak examples need improving. Check each one to see the problem and a better version.

Weak: "How bad is the stress in our school?"
Problem: vague, and it assumes everyone is stressed.
Better: "How do students describe the stress they experience during the school year?"
Weak: "Do students who exercise sleep better than students who don't?"
Problem: implies a comparison or cause-and-effect that interviews cannot test.
Better: "How do students describe the relationship between exercise and their sleep?"
Weak: "Why is the school making everyone so stressed?"
Problem: leading (it assumes the school is the cause) and very broad.
Better: "How do students describe the things that contribute to stress at school?"

Write your research question in your own document, then run it through this final check before you plan your participants.

STEP 3

Choose Your Interview Type

Select the format that fits your research question.

Pick the best format

Check each scenario and decide which format fits best.

Sana wants everyone to answer exactly the same questions in the same order so she can compare answers.
Structured interview — identical questions in a fixed order keep the answers comparable across participants.
Ben wants to explore unexpected ideas using open questions and follow-ups.
Semi-structured interview — prepared key questions plus the freedom to probe interesting answers.
Priya wants to hear a small group build on each other's ideas about wellbeing.
Focus group — participants respond to each other while the researcher guides the discussion.
Your Practical Notes

In your own document, record which format you will use and one reason why it fits your research question.

Do not just choose the format you think is easiest — choose the format that best answers your question, then justify it.

STEP 4

Choose Your Participant(s)

Make these decisions in order

Work through these steps in order — each decision shapes the next.

  1. Target population — the wider group your question is about (e.g. DP students).
  2. Participant / sample — who you will actually interview (e.g. one DP student, or a small focus group).
  3. Sampling technique — how those participants were chosen (e.g. opportunity, volunteer, snowball).
  4. Why this sample, considering bias — explain your decision and a sampling limitation it creates.
Your Practical Notes

In your own document, write down:

  • your target population
  • your actual participant(s) — an individual interview needs a minimum of 1 participant; a focus group needs approximately 3–8
  • your sampling technique
  • why that technique is appropriate for your research question
  • one limitation of your sample (e.g. it is not representative)
IB

IB Sample Guidance

  • Individual interview: minimum 1 participant.
  • Focus group: approximately 3–8 participants.

Careful: more participants does not automatically make qualitative research better. Qualitative depth comes from exploring a suitable sample thoroughly — not from counting responses.

STEP 5

Create Your Interview Schedule

An interview schedule is the prepared set of questions/prompts used to guide the interview. Your questions should avoid:

  • leading questions
  • loaded questions
  • double-barrelled questions (two questions in one)
  • confusing / vague questions
  • unnecessarily personal questions
  • questions requesting highly sensitive information

Fix the question

Each bad question has one of the problems above. Check it to see the problem and a better version.

Bad: "Don't you think exercise obviously makes students feel less stressed?"
Problem: leading question — it pushes the participant towards a "yes".
Better: "How, if at all, does exercise affect how you feel when you are stressed?"
Bad: "Why do you think social media is damaging young people's well-being?"
Problem: loaded question — it assumes social media is damaging.
Better: "How, if at all, do you think social media affects young people's well-being?"
Bad: "Do you feel stressed by schoolwork and get enough sleep at night?"
Problem: double-barrelled — it asks two separate things at once.
Better: ask two questions: "How much stress, if any, does schoolwork cause you?" and "How well, on average, do you sleep?"
Bad: "How do you cope?"
Problem: vague — the participant does not know what kind of answer is wanted.
Better: "What, if anything, do you do to manage stress in your daily routine?"
Bad: "Have you ever been diagnosed with a mental health condition?"
Problem: requests highly sensitive / clinical information that is not needed.
Better: keep the question on everyday experience — "How, if at all, do you manage everyday stress?"

Your interview schedule

Your Practical Notes

Draft your schedule in your own document: your main questions (around five is a sensible number) plus neutral follow-up prompts ready, such as:

  • "Can you tell me more about that?"
  • "Why do you think that is?"
  • "Can you give me an example?"
  • "How did that affect you?"

Follow-up questions should never pressure participants. Always respect the right not to answer.

STEP 6

Plan How You Will Record the Interview

Decide before the interview how responses will be recorded.

Never secretly record a participant.

Do not use participant names in your working data. Use identifiers such as Participant A, Participant B, P1, P2.

If you are using audio recording: follow this procedure.

  1. Explain clearly what the recording will be used for.
  2. Get the participant’s explicit permission before recording.
  3. Never start recording before you have that permission.
  4. Use the recording only for the research purpose agreed.
  5. Store the recording securely.
  6. Delete the recording when it is no longer needed.
Your Practical Notes

In your own document, record how you will record the interview and why that choice suits your research question.

6Create Your Interview RecordOUR CLASS REQUIREMENT

For this class practical you create either:

  • an anonymized working transcript, or, where appropriate,
  • a detailed anonymized interview record based on your notes.

A working transcript does not need professional transcription conventions. Use a simple format like this:

INTERVIEWER: What strategies do you use when you feel stressed about school? PARTICIPANT A: I usually exercise or go for a walk because it helps me clear my head. INTERVIEWER: Can you tell me more about that? PARTICIPANT A: …

Never include:

  • the participant's real name
  • unnecessary identifying information
  • private information unrelated to the research question
If you created an audio recording: use it only for producing or checking the interview record. Do not upload the recording to the website, do not submit it to the teacher, and delete it when it is no longer required, following appropriate data-protection procedures.

How much detail do you need? You need a working transcript you can code and quote from — not professional transcription. You do not need every filler ("um", "uh"), measured pauses, or speech notation. You must not change the meaning of what the participant said.

Your Practical Work

Build your anonymized working transcript / interview record in your own document (or your teacher’s template). Use identifiers like PARTICIPANT A, P1. Leave out content that does not relate to your research question.

7Analyse Your Findings

Analysis turns raw responses into themes. Work through these steps:

  1. Read the interview responses carefully.
  2. Highlight important or repeated ideas.
  3. Create short codes describing those ideas.
  4. Group related codes.
  5. Identify broader themes / patterns.
  6. Select brief evidence from the interview.
  7. Explain what the findings might suggest about the research question.
Worked example Responses: "I go for a run." / "Exercise helps clear my head." / "I usually walk when I'm stressed."
Possible codes: exercise walking stress relief
Possible theme: PHYSICAL ACTIVITY AS A COPING STRATEGY
Another worked example Responses: "I talk to my friend about it." / "Calling my mum helps me feel better." / "We have a family dinner and I feel calmer."
Possible codes: talking social support comfort
Possible theme: SOCIAL SUPPORT AS A STRESS-MANAGEMENT STRATEGY

This is the coding process in one chain:

Raw Response "I go for a run."
↓
Short Code exercise
↓
Similar Codes Grouped exercise + walking
↓
Broader Theme PHYSICAL ACTIVITY AS A COPING STRATEGY

Model findings table

This is what a complete findings table looks like. Yours should follow the same shape: each theme named, real evidence attached, and an interpretation that stays close to the data.

Theme / Pattern Evidence From Responses What Might This Suggest?
PHYSICAL ACTIVITY AS A COPING STRATEGY "I go for a run." / "Exercise helps clear my head." Participants report using movement to manage stress — a self-reported strategy, not evidence of a causal effect.
SOCIAL SUPPORT AS A STRESS-MANAGEMENT STRATEGY "I talk to my friend about it." / "Calling my mum helps me feel better." Participants describe turning to others when stressed — a self-reported coping strategy.
Your Practical Work

Build your own findings table in your own document with columns for Theme / Pattern, Evidence From Responses and What Might This Suggest?. Base every theme on real evidence from your interview, and let the data, not a fixed target number, decide how many themes you report.

What can we conclude?

Distinguish between reasonable interpretations and overclaims. Classify each statement as Supported (a fair interpretation of interview evidence) or Overclaim (it goes beyond what the evidence can show). You will get instant feedback.

"Participants in this interview reported using physical activity to manage stress."
An accurate, evidence-based summary of what participants reported.
"Exercise reduces stress for all students."
This generalises beyond the sample and claims a causal effect — far more than a small interview can show.
"Participants described several strategies, including physical activity and talking to others."
Fits the responses directly and stays at the level of what was described.
"Because participants reported feeling calmer, the interview shows exercise causes lower stress."
Interview self-report cannot demonstrate cause and effect — this is the overclaim to avoid.
Finding a theme or pattern does NOT demonstrate a causal relationship. Interview data shows what people report about their experiences — not what causes what.

Think about your interview and your chosen alternative method across each aspect. Here is a guide to the six aspects:

  • Aim — what each method would try to investigate.
  • Procedure — how each method would actually be carried out.
  • Sample / sampling — who would take part and how they would be chosen.
