Health & Well-being

Interview Practical

Design and conduct an interview on a safe Health & Well-being question, analyse the responses and prepare for Paper 2.

1. Overview 2. Plan Your Interview 3. Ethics & Data Collection 4. Analyze Your Data 5. Reflect & Compare 6. Prepare for Paper 2 7. ManageBac

1Overview

In this practical you will explore people's experiences, views or behaviours relating to a safe Health & Well-being topic, then analyse what the responses suggest.

Teacher-created requirements are never presented as official IB requirements.

From the IB

  • Complete an interview / focus-group class practical as one of the four required research methods.
  • Health & Well-being is the recommended context for the interview practical.
  • Interviews may be structured, semi-structured or focus groups.
  • For student-led practicals: an individual interview needs a minimum of 1 participant; a focus group needs approximately 3–8 participants.
  • Ethical guidelines must be followed: explicit informed consent, voluntary participation, the right to withdraw, confidentiality/anonymity, and protection from harm.
  • Keep a brief record of the practical — aim, procedure, sampling, findings and ethical considerations.

Our class requirements

  • Individual interviews should normally last approximately 15-20 minutes.
  • Produce an anonymized working transcript or a detailed interview record.
  • Code / highlight relevant responses and identify key themes.
  • Complete the practical reflection and submit the required evidence to the teacher.

Choose a Safe Topic

Keep research questions safe and ethical — not topics needing a professional or clinical setting. Suitable examples appear in Stage 2, Step 1 — such as how students manage everyday academic stress.

2Plan Your Interview

Work through the steps below in order, recording each action in your own document. Do not collect any data until your teacher has checked and approved your plan.

Step 1 — Choose a safe topic

Choose a Health & Well-being topic you can explore safely through interview data — for example, how students manage everyday academic stress. Avoid diagnoses, self-harm, bullying, trauma or private family matters.

Your taskRecord your chosen topic.

Step 2 — Write your research question

Write one clear question about experiences or perceptions that interview data can answer.

Example"How do DP students describe the strategies they use to manage everyday academic stress?" "Do students who exercise sleep better than students who don't?" implies a comparison interviews cannot test. Better: "How do students describe the relationship between exercise and their sleep?"
Your taskWrite your final research question.

Step 3 — Choose your interview type

  • Structured: identical questions in a fixed order, best for comparable answers.
  • Semi-structured: key questions plus follow-ups, best for exploring ideas.
  • Focus group: several participants discuss while you guide, best for shared ideas.
Your taskRecord your format and one reason it fits.

Step 4 — Choose your participant(s)

Decide population (e.g. DP students), participant(s) (minimum 1; about 3–8 for a focus group), sampling technique (e.g. opportunity sampling) and one limitation.

Your taskRecord these four decisions.

Step 5 — Create your interview schedule

Prepare around five main questions plus a neutral follow-up such as "Can you tell me more about that?". Avoid leading, loaded, double-barrelled or vague questions, and anything requesting highly sensitive information.

ExampleLeading: "Don't you think exercise obviously makes students feel less stressed?" Better: "How, if at all, does exercise affect how you feel when you are stressed?" Vague: "How do you cope?" Better: "What, if anything, do you do to manage stress in your daily routine?"
Your taskDraft five questions plus follow-ups, checking each against the list above.

Step 6 — Plan how you will record the interview

Audio-record only with explicit permission, or take detailed notes. Use identifiers such as Participant A — never real names. For audio, explain its purpose, get permission first, store it securely and delete it when unneeded.

Never secretly record a participant.

Your taskRecord your method and why it suits your question.

3Ethics & Data Collection

Meet these IB ethical requirements before collecting data:

  • participants must explicitly agree to participate after receiving appropriate information
  • participation is voluntary and they may withdraw their data
  • protect identifying and personal information (confidentiality/anonymity)
  • avoid causing unnecessary anxiety, stress, pain or discomfort
  • handle data securely and do not retain it unnecessarily
  • debrief participants afterwards, particularly where information was withheld beforehand

Conducting the interview

Class target: about 15–20 minutes per interview (the IB prescribes no length).

Before

Confirm consent, prepare your schedule and recording method, confirm the right to withdraw, and choose a suitable setting.

During

Follow the schedule, listen, stay neutral, use gentle follow-ups, never pressure the participant, and protect confidentiality.

After

Thank and debrief the participant, secure and anonymize the data, and record your observations.

If you created an audio recording, use it only for producing or checking the interview record. Do not upload it, do not submit it, and delete it when it is no longer required.

4Analyze Your Data

Analysis turns raw responses into themes. Work through these four steps in order. Interview data shows what people report about their experiences — it does not demonstrate what causes what, and findings from a small school sample cannot be generalized.

Step 1 — Prepare an anonymized transcript or detailed record

Use a simple format with identifiers, leaving out anything unrelated to your question.

Transcript exampleINTERVIEWER: What strategies do you use when you feel stressed about school?
PARTICIPANT A: I usually exercise or go for a walk because it helps me clear my head.

Never include real names, identifying details or private information unrelated to your question. You do not need every filler or pause, and you must not change the participant's meaning.

Your taskProduce your anonymized transcript or record.

Step 2 — Highlight Responses and Assign Codes

Read your interview responses carefully. Highlight relevant ideas and give each one a short descriptive label called a code.

Worked example
Interview Response Code
"I go for a run." Running
"Exercise helps clear my head." Exercise for stress relief
"I usually walk when I'm stressed." Walking to manage stress

A code labels an idea in one response; a theme brings related codes together into a broader pattern.

Your taskAssign a short code to each relevant idea.

Step 3 — Group Codes Into Themes

Use the same three codes to form one broader pattern.

Worked exampleTheme: Physical activity as a coping strategy. Running, exercising and walking are all physical activities used to manage stress, so the three codes group into this single theme.
Your taskGroup your codes into themes describing broader patterns.

Step 4 — Produce a findings table with evidence and cautious interpretation

Record each theme with real evidence and an interpretation that stays close to the data. Model findings table:

Theme / Pattern Evidence From Responses What Might This Suggest?
Physical activity as a coping strategy "I go for a run." / "Exercise helps clear my head." Participants report using movement to manage stress — a self-reported strategy, not evidence of a causal effect.
Social Support as a Stress-Management Strategy "I talk to my friend about it." / "Calling my mum helps me feel better." Participants describe turning to others when stressed — a self-reported coping strategy.
Your taskBuild the same three-column table from your own evidence, phrased cautiously.

5Reflect & Compare

REFLECTION WORKSHEET The IB reflection worksheet for this class practical. Open Reflection Worksheet → RESEARCH COMPARISON WORKSHEET The IB research comparison worksheet. Open Research Comparison Worksheet →

Reflection worksheet

Complete both downloadable worksheets above. Use your actual interview for the reflection, and compare your interview with one alternative research method for the comparison worksheet, considering the aim, procedure, sampling, data, methodological strengths and limitations, and ethical considerations. For the reflection, cover:

  • context explored
  • aim
  • procedure
  • sample and sampling technique
  • findings
  • research considerations (strengths and limitations)
  • ethical considerations
  • reflection on what you learned about research and about yourself as a researcher

Use your real question, sampling decision and one real limitation.

Both completed worksheets must be included in your final ManageBac submission.

Worked exampleMethodological limitation: "I asked a follow-up question that suggested a particular answer. This may have introduced interviewer bias because the participant could have been influenced by my wording."
Your taskIdentify one genuine strength or limitation and explain its effect on your findings. Do not invent problems.

Research comparison worksheet

Choose experiment, observation or survey / questionnaire as your alternative, and consider how a different method would change your evidence.

Compare the two on as many of these as you can: aim, procedure, sampling, data, strengths and limitations, ethics.

You do not conduct another investigation; the comparison is written, and it directly prepares the Paper 2 skill of comparing research methods.

6Prepare for Paper 2

Paper 2 Section A examines the method, not the topic. Prepare these four skills using your own practical:

Describe your practical

State the aim, name the interview format, and outline the procedure: participant, sampling and how responses were recorded.

Apply a concept

Link a concept such as bias to a real moment — for example, interviewer bias in a follow-up you asked.

Compare methods

Compare your interview with one other method — for example, interviews give rich detail but take time, while surveys reach more people with less depth.

Design an alternative

Outline another method: name it, state what you would measure, and note how you would keep it ethical.

Check you can do all four from memory using your own practical.

7Final Submission — ManageBac

Upload ONE Word document (.docx) containing these four sections:

1. Practical Reflection

Include your completed Practical Reflection Worksheet, covering your research aim, procedure, participants, sampling technique, findings, methodological and ethical considerations, and what you learned.

2. Research Methods Comparison

Include your completed Research Methods Comparison Worksheet. Compare your interview with one other research method, considering the aim, procedure, sampling, type of data collected, methodological strengths and limitations, and ethical considerations.

3. Interview Record and Coding

Include your anonymized interview transcript or detailed interview notes.

  • Use Participant A, B, etc. — never real names.
  • Highlight or label important responses so your coding is visible.
  • Make sure the codes relate to your research question.

4. Findings and Themes

Include your completed findings table with these three columns:

  • Theme / Pattern
  • Evidence From Responses
  • What Might This Suggest?

Support each theme with actual evidence from your interview and explain what that evidence suggests. Avoid making claims that go beyond your data.

Before uploading

  • Combine all four sections into ONE Word document.
  • Use clear headings to separate the sections.
  • Make sure both worksheets are completed.
  • Check that participants' identities are protected.
  • Make sure your coding and findings are clearly presented.
  • Upload the completed document to ManageBac.

Important: Do not upload audio recordings, participants' real names, or unnecessary personal information.
