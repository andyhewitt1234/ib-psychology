Human Development

Observation Practical

In this practical, you will design and conduct an observation investigating behaviour connected to human development. You will build a coding scheme, record behaviour as it happens, check the reliability of your data, analyse patterns, evaluate your methodology and use your experience to prepare for Paper 2.

Context: Human Development Research Method: Observation
Possible formats: Naturalistic Structured Overt / Covert Participant / Non-participant
IB vs Class 1. Overview 2. Plan 3. Operationalize 4. Record 5. Sample 6. Reliability 7. Ethics 8. Pre-check 9. Conduct 10. Workflow 11. Analyse 12. Evaluate 13. Reflect 14. Compare 15. Submit 16. Paper 2

!IB Requirements vs Our Class Requirements

It matters which requirements come from the IB and which are our classroom procedures. Teacher-created requirements are never presented as official IB requirements.

From the IB

IB Requirements / Guidance

  • Complete an observation class practical as one of the four required research methods.
  • Human Development is the recommended context for the observation practical.
  • Observations may be naturalistic or structured, and may be overt or covert where appropriate and ethically justified.
  • For student-led practicals: observing behaviour requires a minimum of 1 participant.
  • Ethical guidelines must be followed: explicit informed consent (or an approved alternative consistent with the nature of the study), participants informed about the study, voluntary participation, the right to withdraw, confidentiality/anonymity, and protection from harm.
  • Keep a brief record of the practical — aim, procedure, sampling, findings and ethical considerations.
Our class

Our Class Requirements

  • Choose a safe, observable topic connected to Human Development.
  • Build your coding scheme before you observe.
  • Decide event or time sampling and how long you will observe.
  • Record behaviour in real time using your observation sheet.
  • Check inter-observer reliability with a second observer.
  • Identify patterns in the recorded behaviour.
  • Complete the practical reflection.
  • Submit the required practical evidence to the teacher.

1Practical Overview

Context Human Development
Method Observation
Your Goal Study behaviour as it actually happens — build a coding scheme, observe and record behaviour in real time, and check the reliability of your data.
Final Product A completed observation practical and evidence package that can later be used for Paper 2 revision.

By the end of this practical you should be able to:

  • choose an appropriate observation type
  • build a coding scheme with clear behaviour categories
  • record behaviour systematically (event or time sampling)
  • make ethical research decisions
  • check inter-observer reliability
  • observe without influencing the behaviour
  • create an anonymized observation record
  • identify patterns in observational data
  • evaluate methodological decisions
  • connect your experience to Paper 2
One rule that never changes: here we record only behaviour we can SEE or HEAR. We observe what people do — never private, sensitive or intrusive data. If a topic would require that, it is not right for this practical.

STEP 1

Choose Your Topic

Your investigation must relate to Human Development and must be ethically appropriate for a school practical. The five ideas below are examples — not compulsory topics. Click one (or more) to mark your interest.

Can you observe it directly?

Sort the behaviours below: directly observable or not directly observable. You will get instant feedback.

"A toddler building a tower of blocks."
A visible action — you can see it happen.
"A child raising a hand in class."
A visible action — you can count it.
"A student tapping a pen during a lesson."
A visible and audible action — you can see and hear it.
"A caregiver reading aloud to a child."
Visible and audible behaviour — you can record it in real time.
"A toddler feeling frustrated."
An internal state — you cannot observe a feeling directly, only behaviour that might accompany it.
"A child being bored in class."
Boredom is inside the child. You can observe behaviour, not the feeling itself.
"A student wanting to answer a question."
A desire is internal — you can observe a raised hand, not the wanting behind it.
"A caregiver loving their child."
A long-term internal experience — not something you can watch for during an observation.
Your Practical Notes

In your own document, note which topic interests you (or write your own safe idea) and why it suits an observation. Make sure the behaviour you want to record can be seen or heard.

STEP 2

Write Your Research Question and Aim

A good observation research question should:

  • be clear
  • relate to Human Development
  • focus on behaviour you can see or hear
  • describe behaviour rather than claiming a cause
  • be answerable using observational data
  • be ethically appropriate
Strong example "What types of cooperative behaviours do primary-school children show during group tasks?"

These weak examples need improving. Check each one to see the problem and a better version.

Weak: "How do children feel about playing together?"
Problem: it asks about feelings — internal states that cannot be observed directly.
Better: "What types of play behaviours do toddlers show during free play?"
Weak: "Why do some students lose focus during lessons?"
Problem: it asks "why" — a single observation cannot establish causes.
Better: "What types of off-task behaviours do students show during group work?"
Weak: "Are young children naturally kinder than older children?"
Problem: it compares groups and claims a fixed trait that one observation cannot show.
Better: "What types of helping behaviours do children show during lunch break?"

Write your research question in your own document, then run it through this final check before you plan your observation.

Build the Observation

Each researcher below has made three decisions. Judge each decision: good choice or poor choice. You will get instant feedback.

Riya has permission to observe 4-year-olds during free play at a nursery. She wants to study how much children talk to each other compared to adults.

Riya observes in the normal free-play setting without changing it.
Naturalistic observation in the real setting — the behaviour is not disturbed.
Riya chats to the children during the observation to "help them open up".
Talking to participants mid-observation changes the very behaviour you are recording.
Riya records talk using the categories "talk to a child" and "talk to an adult".
Clear, observable categories — she can tally them in real time.

Ben wants to study off-task behaviour during group work in his own class. He plans to observe quietly from the side of the room.

Ben defines "off-task" before observing (talking off-task, phone use, staring away from the task).
A working definition before observing is exactly right — categories must be fixed in advance.
Ben adds new categories during the observation whenever he sees something new.
Changing categories mid-observation breaks consistency — refine them before you start, not during.
Ben tells the class the broad purpose of the observation and gets the teacher's approval first.
Overt observation with prior agreement — transparent and ethical.

Priya is observing caregiver–child reading in a waiting room where caregivers read to children voluntarily.

Priya records only visible reading behaviours: book held, pointing, child looks at book, question asked.
Bounded, observable behaviours she can record reliably.
Priya writes down what she thinks the child is feeling inside.
Internal states cannot be seen — she would be guessing, not observing.
Priya uses a fixed observation window for each pair so she records consistently.
Consistent recording windows make her data comparable.

Tom wants to compare how children in two age groups cooperate at lunch break.

Tom observes each age group for the same total time.
Equal observation time keeps the groups comparable.
Tom records mostly the children nearest to him because they are easiest to see.
Recording only who is convenient biases the sample and the comparison.
Tom records "who started the cooperation" by guessing each child's intention.
Intentions and motives cannot be observed — only the visible behaviour can be recorded.
Your Practical Notes

In your own document, record your three observation choices (setting, visibility, role) and one reason for each. Then move to Section 3 to operationalize the behaviours you will record.

3Operationalize Your Behaviours

A coding scheme turns raw behaviour into clear, countable categories. To build one, operationalize every category: give it a definition, say what counts as an instance, and say what does not count. This boundary is what makes the category reliable.

Worked example CATEGORY: HELPS ANOTHER
Definition: gives or retrieves an object, or offers assistance, after another person shows a need.
What counts: handing back a dropped item, holding a door, passing a requested toy.
What does not count: friendly conversation, touching, or any action without a clear visible trigger.
Another worked example CATEGORY: OFF-TASK
Definition: eyes away from the task material for five or more consecutive seconds, or engaging in non-task talk.
What counts: staring out of the window, unrelated chat, phone use.
What does not count: brief glances, thinking pauses with eyes on the material, asking a question related to the task.

Too Vague or Operationalized?

Each description below is either operationalized (clear, observable and bounded) or too vague (unclear, internal or unbounded). Classify each one. You will get instant feedback.

"Helping others."
Too vague — which exact actions count? A category needs visible, bounded acts.
"Gives a dropped item back to another child."
Operationalized — a specific visible act with a clear boundary.
"Being distracted."
Too vague — distraction is invisible; you need visible indicators and a time limit.
"Looks away from the task material for five or more seconds."
Operationalized — visible, timed and countable.
"Showing aggression."
Too vague — "aggression" covers many actions; it needs examples and exclusions.
"Pushes, hits, kicks or throws an object at another person."
Operationalized — specific visible actions listed in advance.
Your Practical Work

Build your coding scheme in your own document. For every category write: the category name, a definition, what counts, and what does not count. Around four to eight categories is a sensible number.

Operationalized categories describe behaviour you can see or hear — not personalities, feelings or intentions.

4Plan How You Will Record

Decide before the observation how you will turn behaviour into data. Two main approaches.

Event sampling

Tally every time the behaviour occurs across the whole observation. It suits frequent, clearly bounded behaviours that are quick enough to catch in real time.

Time sampling

Divide the observation into fixed intervals (for example, every 20 seconds) and record whether the behaviour is present during each interval. It suits ongoing behaviours that last for a while. Every interval gets a mark — even a zero.

Event or time?

Classify each recording plan as event sampling or time sampling. You will get instant feedback.

Tallying every time a child raises their hand during a 10-minute lesson.
Event sampling — a short, clearly bounded behaviour counted every time it happens.
Recording whether a student is on-task during each 20-second interval.
Time sampling — an ongoing state checked in fixed intervals.
Counting how many times a toddler says a new word during play.
Event sampling — each occurrence is a discrete, countable event.
Recording whether any cooperative behaviour occurs in each 30-second window.
Time sampling — presence or absence is checked per interval.

In your own document, record: event or time sampling, why it fits the behaviour you chose, how long you will observe, and how you will time the intervals.

Decide your sampling method before you observe and record in real time. Do not rely on memory afterwards.

5Choose Who You Will Observe

Make these decisions in order

Work through these steps in order — each decision shapes the next.

  1. Target population — the wider group your question is about (e.g. toddlers, DP students).
  2. Setting — the real place the behaviour will occur (e.g. nursery free-play area, classroom, corridor before lessons).
  3. Participant(s) — who you will actually observe (a minimum of 1 participant).
  4. Sampling technique — how those participants were chosen (e.g. opportunity, volunteer).
  5. Why this sample, considering bias — explain your decision and a limitation it creates.

Your Practical Notes

In your own document, write down:

  • your target population
  • your setting
  • your participant(s)
  • your sampling technique
  • why that technique is appropriate for your research question
  • one limitation of your sample (e.g. it is not representative)

Careful: a bigger group does not automatically make an observation better. What matters is systematic recording and consistent sampling — not counting more people.

6Check Inter-Observer ReliabilityOUR CLASS REQUIREMENT

One observer simply cannot see everything correctly. Inter-observer reliability checks whether two observers using the same coding scheme record the same behaviour the same way. The higher the agreement, the more reliable the data.

Run the reliability check

  1. Both observers use the same written coding scheme.
  2. Both observe the same behaviour at the same time (or from the same recording).
  3. Record independently — do not watch each other's sheets.
  4. Compare the records and count how often you agreed.
  5. Work out the agreement — for example, agreement on 8 of 10 events is 80% — and then discuss the disagreements.
Why isn't one observer enough?
Observers can miss behaviour, interpret a category differently, or drift as the observation goes on. A second observer shows how consistently the coding scheme is being applied — that is inter-observer reliability.
0 / 5 checked
High agreement does not prove the coding scheme is "true" — it shows the observers are consistent. A coding scheme can be reliably wrong if the categories do not match the real behaviour.
Your Practical Work

In your own document, record your agreement calculation (agreements ÷ total events) and one likely reason for any disagreements.

11Analyse Your Findings

Analysis turns tallies into patterns. Work through these steps:

  1. Read your observation records carefully.
  2. Check you used the same categories throughout.
  3. Add the frequencies for each category.
  4. Note which categories occurred most and least often.
  5. Look for patterns over time (early vs late in the observation).
  6. Select specific evidence (tallies or noted behaviours).
  7. Interpret the pattern in relation to your research question.
Worked example Observed: over 15 minutes, "HELPS ANOTHER" = 12, "REQUESTS HELP" = 3, "SOLO PLAY" = 4 intervals.
Pattern: helping others was the most frequent category; requests for help were rare.
Evidence: tallies from the observation sheet.

Model findings table

This is what a complete findings table looks like. Yours should follow the same shape: each pattern named, real evidence attached, and an interpretation that stays close to the data.

Pattern Evidence From Observation What Might This Suggest?
HELPING WAS THE MOST FREQUENT CATEGORY 12 tallies for "HELPS ANOTHER" across 15 minutes In this group, helping was common — an observation of one session, not a claim about children in general.
REQUESTS FOR HELP WERE RARE 3 tallies for "REQUESTS HELP" across 15 minutes Children rarely asked for help in this session — this does not tell us why.
Your Practical Work

Build your own frequency table and pattern notes in your own document. Base every pattern on real tallies, and let the data decide how many patterns you report.

What can we conclude?

Distinguish between reasonable interpretations and overclaims. Classify each statement as Supported (a fair interpretation of observation evidence) or Overclaim (it goes beyond what the evidence can show). You will get instant feedback.

"In this observation, 'HELPS ANOTHER' was the most frequently tallied category."
A faithful summary of the observation records.
"Children are naturally cooperative."
Generalising a trait from one observation — the observation shows one session, not an inherent quality.
"During the session, the toddler talked to adults more often than to other children."
A supported statement — it reports repeated, recorded events from the observation.
"Because children cooperated, group play causes kindness."
Observational data cannot demonstrate cause and effect — this goes beyond what was recorded.
An observation shows what people did on one occasion — not what they are like, and not what causes their behaviour.

12Evaluate Your Observation

Evaluation means honestly weighing the strengths and limitations of your observation, tied to what actually happened.

Spot the Methodological Problem

Each description below describes either a methodological problem or correct practice. You will get instant feedback.

The observer changed the length of the recording windows halfway through.
Problem — inconsistent sampling makes the data hard to compare.
The observer used the same sampling method for the whole session.
No problem — consistent recording is exactly right.
The two observers counted different things for the same category.
Problem — the category was not applied the same way, so inter-observer reliability is low.
The observer recorded behaviour live, on the spot.
No problem — recording in real time avoids relying on memory.
Example strength Naturalistic observation captures behaviour as it happens, with no self-report — for example, real play or classroom behaviour rather than what people say they do.
Example limitation Reactivity: behaviour may change when people know they are watched, and a single session rarely represents a person's typical behaviour.

Evaluation guide

  1. Strength to acknowledge — what did the observation let you see that another method could not?
  2. Limitation to acknowledge — what could have distorted the data (reactivity, sampling, category boundaries, observer error)?
  3. Ethical considerations — what ethical issues did the observation raise, and how were they managed?
  4. Improvement — what would you change if you repeated the observation?
Your Practical Notes

Write your evaluation in your own document using the guide above.
