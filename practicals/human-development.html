Human Development

Observation Practical

In this practical you will observe behavior in a Human Development context, record it systematically, check your reliability, and reflect on your methodology for Paper 2.

1. Overview & Research Question 2. Design Your Observation 3. Coding Scheme & Reliability 4. Ethics & Data Collection 5. Analyze Your Results 6. Reflect & Paper 2 7. Final Submission

1Overview & Research Question

Teacher-created requirements are never presented as official IB requirements.

From the IB

  • Complete an observation class practical as one of the four required research methods.
  • Human Development is the recommended context for the observation practical.
  • For student-led practicals: observing behavior requires a minimum of 1 participant.
  • Observations may be naturalistic or structured, and may be overt or covert where appropriate and ethically justified.
  • Ethical guidelines must be followed, including explicit informed consent (or an approved alternative consistent with the nature of the study).
  • Keep a brief record of the practical — aim, procedure, sampling, findings and ethical considerations.

Our class requirements

  • Prepare a coding scheme before observing.
  • Choose a recording method.
  • Record behavior systematically.
  • Check inter-observer reliability with a second observer.
  • Analyze recorded patterns.
  • Complete both worksheets.
  • Submit the required practical evidence.

Choose a Safe Topic

Safe topics include play, caregiver–child interaction, cooperation, attention, and queuing. You do not have to observe young children — observations of appropriately approved student behavior are also suitable. Avoid sensitive or safeguarding matters, and keep every topic ethically appropriate for a school practical.

Write your research question

An observation research question should focus on behavior you can see or hear. It should describe what happens rather than assume why it happens.

Strong example"What types of cooperative behaviors do primary-school children show during group tasks?"
Weak example → better versionToo vague: "Why do students lose focus during lessons?" Improved: "What types of off-task behaviors do students show during group work?" Observation can record behavior but cannot directly establish thoughts, feelings, motives, or causes.
Your taskWrite your research question and explain which observable behaviors will help answer it.

2Design Your Observation

A. Choose your observation type

Make three independent decisions:

Decision Options
Setting Naturalistic or structured
Visibility Overt or covert
Researcher role Participant or non-participant
  • Naturalistic: observing behavior in a real, everyday setting without arranging anything.
  • Structured: arranging a situation so the behavior of interest has a chance to occur.
  • Overt: participants know they are being observed and have agreed to it.
  • Covert: participants are not told they are being observed.
  • Participant: the researcher takes part in the situation while observing.
  • Non-participant: the researcher stays outside the situation and only watches and records.

Covert observation is not a default option for this class practical. It requires explicit teacher approval and appropriate ethical justification. Never conduct covert observation without approval.

B. Build your coding scheme

A coding scheme is a list of clearly defined behaviors that you will record. Each category must explain exactly what counts and what does not count.

Category Definition Counts Does not count
HELPS ANOTHER Gives or retrieves an object, or offers assistance, after another person shows a need. Handing back a dropped item, holding a door, or passing a requested object. Friendly conversation or unrelated touching.

Around four to eight categories is usually manageable, depending on the investigation. Avoid overlapping categories unless you have clearly decided that multiple codes can be recorded for the same behavior.

C. Choose your recording method

Method How it works Suitable example
Event sampling Count each occurrence of a defined behavior. Number of times a student raises a hand.
Time sampling Divide the observation into fixed intervals and record behavior according to a predefined rule. Whether a student is on-task at the end of each 20-second interval.

Time sampling needs a precise rule, such as momentary recording or whether a behavior occurred at any point during an interval. These are not interchangeable, so decide your recording rule before observing.

Model observation sheetRecord every category using its planned sampling method. Event counts and interval counts use different units, so report each type separately and never combine them into one total.
Category Sampling Record
HELPS ANOTHER Event 12 events
TALKS TO ADULT Time (20 s intervals) Present in 8 of 15 intervals
SOLO PLAY Time (20 s intervals) Present in 4 of 15 intervals

D. Choose your participants and setting

Decide your target population, observation setting, actual participant(s), sampling technique, one sampling limitation, and observation duration. Our class target is approximately 10–15 minutes of observation — a class guideline, not an IB requirement.

Your taskPrepare your observation design, operationalized coding scheme, recording sheet, sample, observation duration, and sampling decisions. Show your plan to your teacher before collecting data.

3Check Your Coding Scheme & Reliability

Inter-observer reliability examines whether two observers independently record the same behavior in a consistent way:

  1. Both observers use the same coding scheme and recording rules.
  2. Both observe the same session or approved recording.
  3. Each records independently.
  4. Compare matching observation units.
  5. Calculate agreement and identify disagreements.
  6. Revise unclear category definitions before the main observation if necessary.
Worked exampleTwo observers independently recorded whether a student was on-task at the end of each of 10 observation intervals. They agreed on 8 intervals and disagreed on 2.
Percentage agreement = 8 ÷ 10 × 100 = 80%. The observers agreed on 80% of the intervals. This provides a simple measure of consistency, although percentage agreement does not account for agreement occurring by chance.

The calculation uses matching intervals, not an arbitrary total of recorded events. For event sampling, establish a consistent way to match and compare recorded events; the interval example should not be copied mechanically. High agreement suggests consistent recording. It does not automatically prove that the coding scheme measures the intended behavior accurately. Percentage agreement is sufficient — complex reliability statistics are not required.

Your taskRecord how you checked inter-observer reliability, your agreement result, any disagreements, and what you learned about your coding scheme.

4Ethics & Data Collection

PARTICIPANT CONSENT FORM For participants to give their explicit, informed consent. Open Consent Form → PARTICIPANT DEBRIEF FORM To give participants appropriate information after the practical. Open Debrief Form → REQUEST FOR PERMISSION Used where teacher / guardian permission is needed. Open Permission Form →

Meet these ethical requirements before collecting data:

  • informed consent or an appropriately approved alternative
  • voluntary participation and the right to withdraw, subject to any clearly explained practical limitations
  • confidentiality and anonymity
  • protection from harm
  • secure data handling
  • appropriate debriefing
  • teacher approval

Observing children may require additional permission from parents, guardians, and relevant institutions. Students must not independently decide to use covert observation or bypass consent — any alternative to prior explicit consent requires teacher approval and must follow IB ethical guidelines. Use the documents above as directed by your teacher, depending on your investigation.

Conducting the observation

Before

Obtain the required approval and consent. Prepare the coding scheme, observation sheet, and timer. Confirm the recording rules and observer roles.

During

Record only observable behavior. Follow the planned event or time sampling procedure. Do not change categories halfway through. Avoid interfering with participants. Record consistently and independently.

After

Complete the observation record. Compare the observers' records. Remove identifying information. Secure the data and debrief as required.

5Analyze Your Results

After Collecting Your Data

Step 1 — Organize your records

Check that your observation sheets use the same category definitions and recording units throughout.

Step 2 — Calculate your totals

For event sampling, count the number of recorded occurrences. For time sampling, count the intervals in which each behavior was recorded according to your chosen rule.

Step 3 — Identify patterns

Which behaviors were most and least frequent? Did any patterns change during the observation?

Step 4 — Interpret your findings

Explain what the recorded patterns suggest about your research question. Stay close to what you actually observed.

Step 5 — Evaluate your observation

Identify a specific limitation, explain how it might have affected the results, and suggest a realistic improvement.

Worked example — event samplingOne observation using event sampling produced the following tallies. Event counts and interval counts use different units, so always report each type separately and never combine them into one total.
Behavior Number of events
Helps another 12
Requests help 3
Initiates conversation 7

Helping another person was the most frequently recorded behavior in this observation, occurring 12 times. Requests for help occurred only three times.

These results describe what happened during this observation. They do not establish why the behaviors occurred or whether the same pattern would appear in another group or setting.

Model findings table

Pattern Evidence What might this suggest?
Helping was the most frequent recorded event 12 helping events compared with 7 conversation initiations and 3 requests for help Helping occurred frequently in this particular observation, but this does not establish a general trait or cause.

Create your own findings table based on your actual records.

Worked example — evaluatingProblem: One observer counted verbal offers of help, while the second observer counted only physical assistance. Why it matters: They used different interpretations of the same category, reducing inter-observer reliability. Possible improvement: Rewrite the coding scheme to specify whether verbal offers count, then test the revised definitions before another observation.
Your taskIdentify a limitation that genuinely occurred in your observation. Explain how it could have influenced the data and what you would change. Keep reactivity (participants changing their behavior), observer bias, sampling limitations, and ecological validity in mind.

6Reflect & Prepare for Paper 2

Complete both downloadable worksheets above — both are compulsory. Use your actual observation for the reflection, and compare your observation with one other research method for the comparison worksheet. Both completed worksheets are required for submission.

Practical reflection

Use your actual observation to explain your aim and context, observation type and procedure, participants and sampling, coding scheme and recording method, findings, methodological strengths and limitations, ethical considerations, and what you learned.

Research methods comparison

Compare your observation with one other research method — experiment, interview, or survey/questionnaire. Compare the aim, procedure, sampling, type of data, methodological considerations, and ethical considerations. You do not conduct another investigation.

Paper 2 preparation

Paper 2 Section A examines the research method, not the Human Development topic. Prepare these four skills using your own practical:

Describe your practical

Explain the aim, observation type, coding scheme, sampling method, and procedure.

Apply a concept

Connect bias, measurement, or responsibility to a real decision in the observation.

Compare methods

Explain how another research method would investigate a similar question differently.

Design an alternative

Outline an appropriate alternative method, including procedure, data, sampling, and ethics.
