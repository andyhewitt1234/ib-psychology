Learning & Cognition

Experiment Practical

In this practical you will design and conduct an experiment on a safe Learning & Cognition question, analyze your results, and reflect on your methodology for Paper 2.

1. Overview & Research Question 2. Variables & Operationalization 3. Experimental Design 4. Controls & Standardization 5. Sampling & Allocation 6. Ethics & Data Collection 7. Analyze Your Data 8. Evaluate Your Experiment 9. Reflect & Prepare for Paper 2 10. Final Submission

1Overview & Research Question

Teacher-created requirements are never presented as official IB requirements.

From the IB

  • Experiment is one of the four class-practical research methods.
  • The required class practical for the Learning & Cognition context is an experiment.
  • The practical may use a true experiment or a quasi-experiment where appropriate.
  • IB ethical guidelines must be followed throughout.
  • Keep a brief record including the aim, procedure, sampling/sample, findings and ethical considerations.
  • Repeated measures: minimum 5 participants. Independent measures: minimum 10 participants.

Our class requirements

  • Research question and aim, clearly identified IV and DV, and operationalized variables.
  • Experimental design choice, control plan, and standardized procedure.
  • Anonymized results, findings summary, and practical reflection.
  • Required practical evidence submitted to the teacher.

Choose a Safe Topic

Keep your experiment simple, low-risk and feasible in class. Safe classroom examples include the effect of background noise, word organization, distraction, rehearsal strategy, presentation format, retrieval cues, or divided attention on memory and cognition.

Avoid sleep deprivation, caffeine or drug manipulation, significant stress, humiliating tasks, unnecessary deception, harmful sensory stimulation, and anything requiring medical or clinical judgment.

Write your research question

Strong example"Does organizing a word list into categories affect the number of words recalled by DP students?" IV: categorized vs. uncategorized word list. DV: number of words correctly recalled.
Weak example → better version"Does intelligence affect memory performance?" Problem: intelligence cannot be manipulated or compared as an experiment condition. Better: "Does presenting words in categories or arranged randomly affect the number of words recalled?"
Your taskWrite your research question and aim, identifying a manipulable IV and a measurable DV.

2Variables & Operationalization

Independent variable: what is manipulated or compared — the variable changed by the researcher to define the conditions. Dependent variable: what is measured — the outcome recorded for each participant.

Worked example — word recallParticipants study a 20-word list either sorted into categories or in a random order, then recall as many words as possible. IV: whether the words are studied in categories or in a random order. DV: the number of words correctly recalled. This example runs through the rest of this guide.

Operationalization

Operationalization: exactly how the variables are manipulated or measured, stated so precisely that another researcher could reproduce them. "Memory" is not enough — "number of correctly recalled words from a 20-word list after 60 seconds" is.

  • Memory performance → number of correctly recalled words from a 20-word list, after 60 seconds.
  • Background noise → instrumental audio at a fixed volume, during the study phase only.
  • Distraction → completing a secondary number-checking task while doing the memory task.
Your taskRecord exactly how your IV will be implemented and how your DV will be measured, in one clear sentence each.

3Experimental Design

A true experiment manipulates the IV under controlled conditions; a quasi-experiment compares conditions without full control (for example, using pre-existing groups). Your design also decides who completes which condition:

  • Repeated measures: the same participants take part in all conditions.
  • Independent measures: different participants take part in the different conditions.

If your class has already discussed matched pairs, you may also recognize it as a third option — but for this practical, the two main designs above are what you choose between.

Design Strength Limitation
Repeated measures Controls participant differences Order effects possible
Independent measures Avoids order effects Participant differences may influence results

Order effects and counterbalancing

With repeated measures, the order of conditions can change the results: practice effects (the first condition improves the second), fatigue effects (the first condition tires participants), and carryover effects (the first condition influences the second). Counterbalancing solves this: half the participants do Condition A then B, and the other half do B then A, so any benefit of going second spreads evenly across conditions. If everyone always does the categorized list first, any order effect stays attached to that condition.

Your taskRecord your design, why it suits your experiment, one limitation of that design, and your counterbalancing plan where relevant.

4Controls & Standardization

An extraneous variable is anything other than the IV that could influence the DV. A confounding variable is an extraneous variable that differs systematically between conditions and affects the DV — then you can no longer be sure the IV caused the difference. For a background-noise experiment, watch out for different word lists, study time, rooms, instructions, test delays, device volumes, and interruptions.

Variable to control How it will be controlled Why it matters
Study time Same 60 seconds for every participant Different times would confound the effect of the IV
Word-list difficulty Same or matched word lists Harder lists would lower recall regardless of the IV

Standardize the procedure

A standardized procedure means that, apart from the IV, everyone experiences the same study: the same instructions from the same script, the same timing, the same materials, the same testing conditions, and the same scoring rules. Write the complete procedure so that a different researcher could follow it, including participant instructions, condition sequence, timing, materials, measurement and scoring, and debriefing.

Your taskRecord at least three variables you will control (what, how, and why each matters) and write your full standardized procedure.

5Sampling & Allocation

Decide your target population (the wider group), your actual sample (who takes part — minimum 5 participants for repeated measures, minimum 10 for independent measures), your sampling technique (for example, opportunity or volunteer sampling), your design, and your allocation method where relevant — plus one sampling or design limitation.

Opportunity sampling approaches people who are readily available, such as students accessible during an approved classroom session. Volunteer sampling recruits participants who choose to take part after seeing an invitation. Both can introduce sampling bias: opportunity samples may over-represent easily accessible students, while volunteer samples may over-represent students particularly interested in the topic.

Random sampling is about recruiting participants from a population; random allocation is about assigning already-recruited participants to conditions (for example, by coin flip or drawing cards from a shuffled deck). Random allocation reduces systematic participant differences between conditions in independent-measures designs.

Your taskRecord your target population, sample, sampling technique, allocation method where relevant, and one limitation.

Before collecting any data, show your teacher your research question, design, controls, standardized procedure, sampling plan, and ethical arrangements. Do not collect data until approved.

6Ethics & Data Collection

PARTICIPANT CONSENT FORM For participants to give their explicit, informed consent. Open Consent Form → PARTICIPANT DEBRIEF FORM To give participants appropriate information after the practical. Open Debrief Form → REQUEST FOR PERMISSION Used where teacher / guardian permission is needed. Open Permission Form →

Meet these ethical requirements before collecting data:

  • informed consent after receiving appropriate study information
  • voluntary participation and the right to withdraw
  • confidentiality and anonymity of responses
  • protection from harm
  • debriefing with appropriate information afterwards
  • secure handling of data, deleted when no longer needed

Experiments follow the same ethics rules: do not expose participants to significant harm, unnecessary stress, humiliating tasks, or risky substances; do not use coercion; and stop if a participant wants to stop. Avoid unnecessary deception — where any deception is considered, it must comply with teacher guidance, be necessary, cause no harm, and include full debriefing. Do not plan unnecessary deception. Use the documents above as your teacher directs — not every investigation automatically requires every permission form. Teacher permission is particularly important if you propose collecting data outside normal classroom arrangements.

Collecting your data

Before the experiment

Confirm approval and consent. Prepare materials and recording sheets. Check the standardized procedure. Confirm condition allocation or order.

During the experiment

Follow the procedure. Maintain the planned controls. Record the dependent variable accurately. Respect participants' rights.

After the experiment

Debrief participants. Anonymize the records. Organize the results. Note any unexpected methodological problems.

7Analyze Your Data

After Collecting Your Data

Step 1 — Organize your results

Create a table showing the scores for each experimental condition. Use anonymous participant identifiers rather than names.

Step 2 — Calculate the mean

Calculate the average score for each condition by adding the scores and dividing by the number of valid scores.

Step 3 — Identify the pattern

Compare the conditions. Which had the higher mean? Did individual participants show a similar pattern?

Step 4 — Interpret your findings

Explain what the results suggest about your research question. Avoid conclusions that go beyond the evidence.

Step 5 — Evaluate your experiment

Identify a specific methodological limitation, explain how it might have affected your findings, and suggest a realistic improvement.

Worked example — word recall (repeated measures)Five participants studied categorized and uncategorized word lists. Mean recall was calculated for each condition:
Participant Uncategorized (words recalled) Categorized (words recalled)
P1 7 11
P2 8 12
P3 6 10
P4 9 11
P5 7 13
Mean 7.4 11.4

The mean recall score was 7.4 words for the uncategorized list and 11.4 words for the categorized list. All five participants recalled more words in the categorized condition.

These results suggest that categorization may have improved recall in this experiment. However, we must also consider whether order effects, differences between the word lists, or other uncontrolled variables could explain the difference.

This is a repeated-measures example: the same five participants completed both conditions. Students using independent measures must organize their results according to their own design, with separate participants in each condition. Report what the numbers descriptively show; do not claim more than the data supports, and do not use advanced statistical tests.

Your taskOrganize your results, calculate the mean for each condition, identify the pattern, interpret your findings cautiously, and evaluate one limitation.

8Evaluate Your Experiment

Evaluate your experiment against considerations such as confounding variables, participant differences, order effects, demand characteristics, researcher bias, standardization, reliability, validity, sampling, and ethics.

Worked exampleProblem: All participants completed the uncategorized condition first. Why it matters: Practice may have improved performance in the second condition, making it difficult to determine whether categorization alone explains the difference. Possible improvement: Counterbalance the order of conditions and use appropriately comparable word lists.
Your taskIdentify a limitation that actually occurred in your experiment. Explain how it could have affected your results and suggest a realistic improvement.

Paper 2 preparation

Paper 2 Section A examines the research method, not the Learning & Cognition topic. Prepare these four skills using your own practical:

Describe your practical

Explain the aim, IV, DV, design, sample, and procedure.

Apply a concept

Connect a concept such as causality, measurement, bias, or responsibility to an actual decision in your experiment.

Compare methods

Explain how another research method would investigate the question differently.

Design an alternative

Explain how you could investigate a related question using another method, including its procedure, data, sampling, and ethics.
