Experiment Practical

In this practical, you will design and conduct an experiment investigating a safe, feasible question connected to learning and cognition. You will identify and operationalize your independent and dependent variables, choose an experimental design, plan controls, standardize your procedure, collect data ethically, organize and interpret the results, evaluate your methodology and use your experience to prepare for Paper 2.

2Choose a Safe Experimental Question

Start from a working idea worth testing, then check it is safe and feasible to run in class. Some realistic classroom examples in the Learning & Cognition context:

  • effect of background noise on memory recall
  • effect of word organization on recall
  • effect of distraction on short-term memory performance
  • effect of rehearsal strategy on recall
  • effect of presentation format on memory
  • effect of retrieval cue availability on recall
  • effect of divided attention on a simple cognitive task
Keep your experiment simple, low-risk and feasible in class. Avoid: sleep deprivation, caffeine or drug manipulation, inducing significant stress, humiliating participants, unnecessary deception, harmful sensory stimulation, and anything requiring medical or clinical judgment.

Would This Work as a Class Experiment?

Classify each proposal as suitable or problematic for a class experiment. You will get instant feedback.

"Participants study a word list in silence or with soft instrumental audio, then recall as many words as possible."
Feasible, ethical, manipulable (noise condition) and measurable (words recalled).
"Participants must stay awake for 24 hours before the recall task to test the effect of sleep deprivation."
Problematic — genuinely risky and ethically unacceptable; creating harmful or unnecessary conditions is not allowed.
"To test the effect of caffeine, some participants drink strong coffee before the memory task."
Problematic — manipulating substances is not an appropriate class experiment; a caffeine-free design would just study natural differences.
"Participants sort 20 words into categories before recalling them, or read the same 20 words in a random order and recall them."
Suitable — the organizing strategy is manipulable and recall is measurable, with no risk.
"Participants who do badly on a memory test must stand in front of the class and explain their score."
Problematic — humiliating participants is never acceptable.
"The researcher secretly changes which condition participants believe they are in, without any deception plan, and does not intend to debrief."
Problematic — unnecessary deception with no debriefing conflicts with ethical guidance.
"Participants complete a recall task while also responding to a simple number-check, or while doing nothing else."
Suitable — divided attention is manipulable and performance is measurable; participants simply stop if it is too much.
"A very loud, abrupt sound is played at maximum volume during the study phase to test the effect of startling noise."
Problematic — harmful sensory stimulation must not be used.

Feedback above teaches feasibility, ethics, manipulability and measurability — the four checks every proposal must pass.

Your Practical Notes

List two or three safe experimental ideas in your own document. Keep what makes each one feasible, ethical, manipulable and measurable.

3Formulate the Research Question / Aim

A strong experimental question:

  • identifies a manipulable IV
  • identifies a measurable DV
  • is focused
  • is feasible
  • is ethically appropriate
  • allows a clear comparison between conditions
Strong example

"Does organizing a word list into categories affect the number of words recalled by DP students?"

IV: categorized vs. uncategorized word list  ·  DV: number of words correctly recalled

Weak examples

Open each one to see the problem and a better version.

"Does studying affect how good memory is?"

PROBLEM: Vague variables. "Studying" and "how good memory is" are not specific enough to operationalize.

BETTER VERSION: "Does studying a word list twice instead of once affect the number of words recalled?"

"Does intelligence affect memory performance?"

PROBLEM: A non-manipulable variable. "Intelligence" cannot be changed or compared as an experiment condition; it is studied, not manipulated.

BETTER VERSION: "Does presenting words in categories or arranged randomly affect the number of words recalled?"

"Does learning become better with practice?"

PROBLEM: An unmeasurable DV. "Learning" and "better" give no way to count or record the outcome.

BETTER VERSION: "Does an extra study trial affect the number of words correctly recalled from a 20-word list?"

Your Practical Notes

Write your final research question / aim in your own document.

4Identify the IV and DV

  • INDEPENDENT VARIABLE (IV) — the variable changed / manipulated or used to define the conditions.
  • DEPENDENT VARIABLE (DV) — the outcome that is measured.

IV or DV?

Classify each statement as describing the IV or the DV. You will get instant feedback.

"Participants study either a categorized or an uncategorized word list."
IV — the condition is controlled by the researcher.
"Number of words correctly recalled."
DV — the measured outcome.
"The level of background noise during the study phase."
IV — the researcher sets or compares the noise conditions.
"Reaction time in milliseconds on the number-checking task."
DV — the outcome being measured.
"Whether participants rehearse aloud or rehearse silently."
IV — the strategy is assigned or instructed by the researcher.
"Number of items correctly checked on the secondary task."
DV — the measured outcome.
"Words presented as a list or as a story."
IV — presentation format is decided by the researcher.
"Number of words recalled after a 30-second delay."
DV — the measured outcome, timed and scored.

Find the Variables

Read each scenario, then reveal the IV and DV.

Scenario A

Participants study a 20-word list either in silence or with soft instrumental audio playing, then write down as many words as they can remember.

Reveal the IV

IV: the presence or absence of background audio during the study phase.

Reveal the DV

DV: the number of words correctly recalled.

Scenario B

Half the participants sort words into category groups before studying; the other half study the same words in a random order. Everyone then recalls the words.

Reveal the IV

IV: whether the words are studied in categories or in a random order.

Reveal the DV

DV: the number of words recalled.

Scenario C

Everyone learns the same list of 15 words. During recall, half the participants see the first letter of each word (a retrieval cue); the other half recall with no cues.

Reveal the IV

IV: whether a retrieval cue (first letter) is available during recall.

Reveal the DV

DV: the number of words correctly recalled.

Your Practical Notes

State your IV and DV in your own document in one clear sentence each.

5Operationalize the Variables

"Memory" is not enough. An operational definition states exactly how a variable will be implemented or measured, so another researcher could reproduce it.

Example

"Memory" → "number of correctly recalled words from a 20-word list after 60 seconds."

Abstract variable → operational definition

MEMORY PERFORMANCEthe abstract variable
→
NUMBER OF CORRECTLY RECALLED WORDSfrom a 20-word list, after 60 seconds
BACKGROUND NOISEthe abstract variable
→
INSTRUMENTAL AUDIO AT A FIXED VOLUMEduring the study phase only
DISTRACTIONthe abstract variable
→
COMPLETING A SECONDARY NUMBER-CHECKING TASKwhile doing the memory task

Operationalized or Too Vague?

Classify each statement as operationalized or too vague. You will get instant feedback.

"More correctly recalled words."
Operationalized — a countable, specific measure.
"Better memory."
Too vague — "better" cannot be counted or recorded.
"No background audio during the learning phase."
Operationalized — a precise condition another researcher could reproduce.
"Quiet."
Too vague — "quiet" means different things in different rooms.
"Participant completes a secondary number-checking task."
Operationalized — the distraction task is described concretely.
"High distraction."
Too vague — "high" needs a concrete definition to be recreated.
Your Practical Notes

Record exactly how your IV will be implemented and how your DV will be measured in your own document.

6Choose Your Experimental Design

  • REPEATED MEASURES — the same participants take part in all conditions.
  • INDEPENDENT MEASURES — different participants take part in the different conditions.

If your class has already discussed matched pairs, you may also recognise it as a third option — but for this practical, the two main designs above are what you choose between.

Which Design?

Classify each description as repeated measures or independent measures. You will get instant feedback.

"Every participant completes the recall task in silence and then again with background audio."
Repeated measures — the same people do both conditions.
"One group studies a categorized list; a different group studies the same words in a random order."
Independent measures — different participants in each condition.
"The same participants first recall with retrieval cues and later recall without any cues."
Repeated measures — everyone experiences both conditions.
"Half the DP class completes a divided-attention task; the other half completes the same task with no distraction."
Independent measures — two separate groups.

The main trade-offs

Design Strength Limitation
Repeated measures controls participant differences order effects possible
Independent measures avoids order effects participant differences may influence results
Your Practical Notes

Record the design you choose, why it suits your experiment, and one likely limitation of that design — in your own document.

7Order Effects and Counterbalancing

If you choose repeated measures, the order in which participants do the conditions can change the results.

  • PRACTICE EFFECTS — doing the first condition improves performance in the second.
  • FATIGUE EFFECTS — doing the first condition tires participants, so the second is worse.
  • CARRYOVER EFFECTS — the first condition influences how participants respond in the second.
Counterbalancing

Half the participants do Condition A then Condition B; the other half do Condition B then Condition A. Any benefit of "going second" is spread evenly across the two conditions.

What Is the Problem?

Identify the main issue in each repeated-measures scenario. You will get instant feedback.

"Everyone does Condition A first. After one practice run, participants are much better at the second condition simply because they have already done the task once."
Practice effect — performance improves because the task was already tried.
"The second condition comes after a long, effortful task, and participants' scores drop mainly because they are tired."
Fatigue effect — tiredness, not the condition, explains the drop.
"Everyone always does the categorized list before the random list, so any order effect is completely attached to one condition."
Need counterbalancing — split the order so effects spread across conditions.

8Control Extraneous Variables

  • EXTRANEOUS VARIABLE — a variable other than the IV that could influence the DV.
  • CONFOUNDING VARIABLE — an extraneous variable that differs systematically between conditions and affects the DV, so you can no longer be sure the IV caused the difference.
Example

Experiment: effect of background noise on recall.

Potential extraneous variables: different word lists, different study time, different room, different instructions, different test delay, different device volume, interruptions.

Control It or Ignore It?

For each potential extraneous variable, decide whether it should be controlled or whether it can safely be ignored. You will get instant feedback.

"One condition gets more study time than the other."
Control it — give both conditions the same study time, otherwise time is confounded with the IV.
"One condition is tested in a quiet room and the other in a busy corridor."
Control it — keep the setting the same and manipulate only the intended IV.
"Different participants receive slightly different instructions."
Control it — standardize the instructions so everyone receives the same wording.
"The researcher scores some participants' recall while they are still talking, but counts other participants' answers only after they have finished."
Control it — apply the same scoring rules to everyone, after recall ends.
"Participants happen to be wearing different coloured shirts on the day."
Ignore it — irrelevant to the task unless it is somehow part of the manipulation.
"One condition uses a loud device volume and the other a barely audible one."
Control it — fix the volume precisely so "noise" means the same thing for everyone.

Your control plan

Build a short plan in your own document using this structure:

Variable to control How it will be controlled Why it matters
Study time Same 60 seconds for every participant Different times would confound the effect of the IV
Word-list difficulty Same or matched word lists Harder lists would lower recall regardless of the IV
Your Practical Notes

Write at least three rows for your own experiment — variable to control, how, and why it matters — in your own document.

9Standardize the Procedure

A standardized procedure means that, apart from the IV, everyone experiences the same study. Everyone should receive:

  • the same instructions
  • the same timing
  • the same materials where appropriate
  • the same testing conditions
  • the same scoring rules

Same or Different?

For each procedural decision, decide whether it should stay the same for everyone or be different because it is part of the IV. You will get instant feedback.

"Whether participants hear background audio during the study phase."
Different — this is the IV itself.
"The time allowed to study the word list."
Same — standardize study time for every participant.
"The exact wording of the instructions read aloud."
Same — identical instructions for everyone, from the same script.
"Whether a retrieval cue (first letter) is available during recall."
Different — it defines the two conditions.
"How many words count as correct in scoring."
Same — apply the same scoring rule to every participant.
"The delay between studying and recalling the words."
Same — equal test delay for everyone.
Your Practical Work

Write the complete procedure in your own document. A different researcher should be able to follow it. Include:

  • participant instructions
  • condition sequence
  • timing
  • materials
  • measurement / scoring
  • debriefing

10Participants, Sampling and Allocation

Apply what you already know from the Research Methods unit:

  • TARGET POPULATION — the wider group your findings might say something about.
  • ACTUAL SAMPLE — the people who actually took part.
  • SAMPLING TECHNIQUE — how the sample was obtained (e.g. opportunity, volunteer).
  • EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN — repeated measures or independent measures.
  • ALLOCATION TO CONDITIONS — how participants end up in each condition.
PARTICIPANT GUIDANCE
Repeated measures: minimum 5 participants  ·  Independent measures: minimum 10 participants
Use the numbers that match your design.

If you use independent measures, understand why random allocation reduces systematic participant differences. If you use repeated measures, the same participants complete both conditions, which controls for participant differences.

11Random Allocation

Random sampling is not the same as random allocation.
• Random sampling is about recruiting participants from a population.
• Random allocation is about assigning already-recruited participants to conditions.

Sampling or Allocation?

Classify each description as an example of sampling or allocation. You will get instant feedback.

"Names are drawn from the whole year group to recruit participants."
Sampling — choosing who is recruited.
"After recruitment, each participant is randomly assigned to the Noise or the Silence condition."
Allocation — assigning already-recruited people to conditions.
"A researcher asks the first 12 students who arrive at the psychology room to take part."
Sampling — opportunity sampling recruits whoever is available.
"Ten volunteers are divided into two groups by drawing cards from a shuffled deck."
Allocation — the volunteers were already recruited; drawing cards assigns them to conditions.
"The researcher emails a call for participants to the whole school and uses whoever volunteers."
Sampling — volunteer sampling shapes who takes part at all.
"Participants are numbered 1–12 and assigned to Condition A or B based on a coin flip."
Allocation — the coin flip randomly assigns participants to conditions.

12STOP: Ethics & ApprovalIB GUIDANCE

Complete this before collecting data. The IB ethical guidance below applies to every practical — including experiments.

Informed Consent Participants must explicitly agree to participate after receiving appropriate information about the investigation.
Right to Withdraw Participants may stop participating and withdraw their data.
Confidentiality & Anonymity Protect identifying and personal information.
Protection From Harm The investigation must not create unnecessary anxiety, stress, pain or discomfort.
Data Protection Research data must be securely handled and should not be retained unnecessarily.
Debriefing Participants should receive appropriate information following participation, particularly where information was withheld beforehand.

An experiment does not change the ethics rules. Experiment-specific reminders:

  • do not expose participants to significant harm
  • do not create unnecessary stress
  • do not use humiliating tasks
  • do not use risky substances
  • do not use coercion
  • do not continue if a participant wants to stop

Ethically Sound or Not?

Classify each description as ethically sound or not ethical. You will get instant feedback.

"Participants volunteer, are told the aim, and may leave the recall task at any time."
Sound — informed consent, voluntary participation and a right to stop.
"Participants are told the loud sound condition will be 'playful and fun' to make them take part, with no debrief planned."
Misleading participants without justification and without debriefing is not ethical.
"The researcher records participants' full names with their scores 'so the class can compare performance'."
Collecting unnecessary identifying information alongside data conflicts with anonymity.
"The task is short, harmless and never too hard; responses are recorded without names and stored securely."
Sound — low risk, anonymised and securely handled.
"Participants who answer slowly are publicly named and asked to explain why they found it difficult."
Public embarrassment and pressure are unacceptable.
"No participant is pressured: anyone who declines simply does not take part, with no penalty."
Sound — no coercion, declining has no consequences.

16Organize and Analyse the Results

Arrange the data, then compare the conditions descriptively. Focus on organizing data, descriptive comparison, identifying patterns and interpreting cautiously.

Worked example

Research question: does categorizing a word list improve recall?

Condition A: uncategorized list  ·  Condition B: categorized list

Design: repeated measures — the same 5 participants completed both conditions.

Model results table

Participant Uncategorized Categorized
P1 7 11
P2 8 12
P3 6 10
P4 9 11
P5 7 13
Mean 7.4 11.4
Summary

Mean recall: Uncategorized = 7.4  ·  Categorized = 11.4

Pattern: every participant recalled more words in the categorized condition, and the mean recall was higher by four words.

Simple descriptive comparison (means, and looking across the individual scores) is what the course needs for this practical. Do not introduce advanced inferential statistics unless your class has already been taught to use them.

Your Practical Work

Build your own results table in your own document. Record each participant's score per condition, calculate the mean for each condition, and note the pattern that appears.

17What Can We Conclude?

Supported or Overclaim?

Using the worked example above, classify each statement as supported by the data or an overclaim. You will get instant feedback.

"Participants recalled more words on average in the categorized condition."
Supported — a faithful summary of the means.
"Categorizing information permanently improves memory."
Overclaim — one small classroom study cannot support a sweeping, permanent claim.
"In this sample, recall scores were higher in the categorized condition."
Supported — stays within the sample.
"Categorization is the best memory strategy for everyone."
Overclaim — "best for everyone" goes far beyond this evidence.
"Most participants in this sample recalled more words from the categorized list."
Supported — all five participants scored higher; it is fair for this sample.
An important distinction

Because an experiment manipulates the IV and controls alternative explanations, it is better suited to causal inference than interviews, observations or questionnaires.

But that does not mean every experiment automatically proves causation. You still need to weigh confounds, control quality, sample limitations, reliability and validity before concluding anything.

A well-controlled experiment supports careful claims about cause-and-effect — but a flawed one, or one with a small unrepresentative sample, does not. Always check the evidence.

18Evaluate the Experiment

Evaluate your experiment against considerations specific to the method:

  • Confounding variables — did something other than the IV differ between conditions?
  • Participant variables — could participant differences affect the results?
  • Order effects — relevant if you used repeated measures.
  • Demand characteristics — could participants guess the aim and change their behaviour?
  • Researcher bias — could your expectations have influenced instructions or scoring?
  • Standardization — was the procedure applied consistently?
  • Reliability — would the procedure likely produce similar results again?
  • Validity — did the DV actually measure the intended cognitive process?
  • Ecological validity — how realistic was the task?
  • Sampling — can the findings reasonably generalize?
  • Ethics — were participants protected?

Spot the Methodological Problem

Each scenario below is either a methodological problem or not a problem. You will get instant feedback.

"The researcher scores some participants' recall more leniently because he already knows which condition they were in and expects better recall there."
Problem — researcher bias can influence scoring.
"Condition A is run in a quiet classroom; Condition B is run during lunch in a noisy cafeteria."
Problem — the setting is confounded with the IV.
"In a repeated-measures design, every participant always does the categorized condition first."
Problem — order effects are fully attached to one condition; counterbalance the order.
"Participants guess the aim is 'does categorizing help recall?' and try noticeably harder during the categorized condition."
Problem — demand characteristics can change behaviour.
"Early finishers recall the words five minutes later; everyone else recalls immediately."
Problem — the test delay is not standardized, so it varies between participants.
"The same stopwatch, the same word list, the same instruction script and the same scoring rule are used for every participant."
Not a problem — strong standardization protects the comparison.
Your Practical Notes

Write your evaluation in your own document — at least one strength and one limitation, plus one consideration from the list that actually applied when you ran the experiment.

19Improve the Experiment

Each flawed design below has possible improvements. Choose the best one. You will get instant feedback.

Design 1: Condition A completes a recall task in a quiet classroom. Condition B completes it during lunch in a noisy cafeteria.
Best: test both conditions in the same environment and manipulate only the IV — otherwise the setting is confounded with the IV.
Design 2: Participants get unlimited time to study the categorized list, but only 30 seconds for the uncategorized list.
Best: equal study time — time is otherwise confounded with categorization.
Design 3: Group A learns the list in the morning using one set of words; Group B learns a different, harder set in the afternoon.
Best: standardize time of day and use the same or matched word lists — two differences (list difficulty and time) both threaten the comparison; control both.
Design 4: The researcher counts any similar-sounding word as correct for friends, but only exact matches for everyone else.
Best: — inconsistent scoring injects researcher bias into the DV.
