Section A

Knowledge & Explanation

2 compulsory questions, 4 marks each, 8 marks total. Section A tests whether you can understand a psychological idea and explain one relevant example.

Section A — Knowledge and explanation

2 compulsory questions · 4 marks each · 8 marks total.

Section A = understand it + explain one example.

How to answer Section A

Worked example: Describe how one chemical messenger plays a role in one human behaviour. [4]

  1. 1. DEFINE — Clearly and accurately define the key psychological concept, including the important details needed to show you understand it.
  2. 2. EXPLAIN — Show detailed understanding of how the psychology works.
  3. 3. EXAMPLE — Give one relevant real-world, hypothetical, or research example.
  4. 4. EXPLAIN IT — Show exactly how your example demonstrates the psychology.
  5. 5. LINK BACK — Make the connection to the question clear.

This is a guide, not a five-part formula. Your goal is detailed psychological knowledge plus one relevant example that you explain.

Both routes are valid. Your example can be real-world/hypothetical or research-based. Detailed research-study knowledge is not required.

Worked model answers

Teacher-created model answer

Try writing your own answer to the worked example before revealing the model answers. Notice that both routes can produce a strong Section A response — a real-world / hypothetical situation, or a research example.

Model answer — real-world / hypothetical example

A chemical messenger is a substance that carries signals between cells. Acetylcholine (ACh) is a neurotransmitter, a type of chemical messenger that allows neurons to communicate. ACh receptors are found in abundance in the hippocampus, a brain area involved in consolidating information from short-term to long-term memory. This means ACh plays an important role in learning and memory.

For example, imagine someone trying to remember where they parked their car in a large shopping centre. Remembering the location requires the formation of a new spatial memory. If acetylcholine activity in the hippocampus were disrupted, the person would have greater difficulty forming that new memory and later recalling where the car was parked. The example therefore shows how acetylcholine is involved in forming spatial memories.

Model answer — research example

A chemical messenger is a substance that carries signals between cells. Acetylcholine (ACh) is a neurotransmitter, a type of chemical messenger that allows neurons to communicate. ACh receptors are found in abundance in the hippocampus, a brain area involved in consolidating information from short-term to long-term memory. This means ACh plays an important role in learning and memory.

For example, Antonova found that blocking ACh receptors reduced activity in the hippocampus during a spatial-memory task. Because blocking acetylcholine reduced hippocampal activity while participants were using spatial memory, the finding supports the role of normal ACh activity in forming spatial memories. The research therefore shows how acetylcholine contributes to human memory, particularly spatial memory.

Teacher-created practice

Question 2

Describe the role of one brain structure in one human behaviour. [4]

Show guidance

Define the structure you choose (for example the amygdala). Explain what it does. Give one behaviour it helps to produce, with one explained piece of supporting evidence, then link back.

Show model answer

Strong response (model): The amygdala is a brain structure involved in processing emotionally significant information, particularly potential threats. This can influence fear and avoidance behaviour. For example, a person who sees a threatening dog may rapidly experience fear and move away from it as the amygdala helps process the threat and contributes to the emotional response. This demonstrates how the amygdala can influence behaviour by helping the brain detect and respond to potential danger.

Why this satisfies the upper markband: detailed accurate knowledge with a relevant example that is explained (what the fear-and-avoidance example shows about the amygdala's role), closed with a direct link to the question.

Teacher-created practice

Question 3

Describe how one social factor plays a role in one human behaviour. [4]

Show guidance

Define the social factor (for example majority influence/conformity). Explain the mechanism — why people follow the group. Give one explained example from research or real life, then link back.

Show model answer

Strong response (model): Conformity is adjusting one's behaviour or judgments to match a group. One mechanism is normative social influence: people follow the majority because they want approval and fear rejection. For example, in Asch-type line-judgment tasks, participants frequently give an answer they know is wrong simply because the rest of the group gave it first — the desire to fit in overrides their own perception. This shows a social factor (the unanimous majority) directly shaping individual behaviour through the mechanism of normative pressure.

Why this satisfies the upper markband: the definition, the mechanism (why people conform), and an explained example that makes the mechanism-to-behaviour connection explicit.

Official specimen question

Question 4

Explain anchoring bias with reference to one example of human behaviour. [4]

Show guidance

Define anchoring bias: an initial number or value anchors subsequent judgments, with adjustment away from the anchor typically insufficient. Explain the mechanism. Give one example of behaviour it produces — for instance estimates drifting toward an arbitrary starting figure — explain why the example shows anchoring at work, then link back.

Show model answer

Strong response (model — teacher guidance on an official question): Anchoring bias is the tendency for an initial value to influence a later judgment, even when that starting value is arbitrary. For example, Tversky and Kahneman had participants see an arbitrary number from a wheel of fortune before estimating the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Participants exposed to higher numbers generally made higher estimates than those exposed to lower numbers. Because the arbitrary starting number shifted their later judgments, the example demonstrates how an anchor can bias human decision-making.

Why this satisfies the upper markband: detailed knowledge of the mechanism with a relevant example that is explained (why the drift demonstrates anchoring) rather than merely stated.
