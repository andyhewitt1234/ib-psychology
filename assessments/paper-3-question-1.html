Paper 3 · Question 1

Question 1

Interpretation of Graphs

3 marks. Explain one issue that limits interpretation of a source — and why it limits interpretation.

Overview Q1 Q2 Q3 Q4

Question 1 — what it wants

3 marks · one issue · explained.

Q1 is not “describe the graph”. Do not summarise what the source shows. You explain ONE issue that limits interpretation of the information from the given source — and why it limits interpretation.

IDENTIFY one issue→ CLEARLY DESCRIBE it→ EXPLAIN why it limits interpretation

Describe means tying the issue to a precise feature of the actual source. Explain means stating what the issue costs the interpretation — which claims the data can and cannot support.

EXAMPLE: Suppose a graph compares anxiety levels for students with “healthy” and “unhealthy” work–life balance, but does not explain how those two groups were determined.

DESCRIBE:The graph divides students into “healthy” and “unhealthy” work–life balance groups without explaining how students were placed into each group.
EXPLAIN:Because the two groups are not clearly defined, we cannot be confident about what difference in work–life balance is actually being compared. This limits how confidently we can interpret the difference in anxiety between the groups.

The response method

ISSUE→ SOURCE DETAIL→ CONSEQUENCE FOR INTERPRETATION
  • ISSUE — name the genuine problem or limitation.
  • SOURCE DETAIL — point to exactly where it appears in THIS source.
  • CONSEQUENCE — explain what the issue means for what we can or cannot confidently conclude from the source.

See What a Strong Answer Looks Like

One issue is that the graph does not explain how ‘healthy’ and ‘unhealthy’ work–life balance were determined. Without knowing how these categories were measured, we cannot tell what being placed in either group actually means. This limits interpretation because the differences in anxiety and depression cannot be confidently attributed to a clearly defined difference in work–life balance.

ISSUE“the graph does not explain how ‘healthy’ and ‘unhealthy’ work–life balance were determined”
SOURCE DETAIL“the graph divides students into these two categories without explaining how membership in either category was determined”
CONSEQUENCE“the anxiety and depression differences cannot be confidently interpreted as reflecting a clearly defined difference in work–life balance”

One issue explained against the data beats five issues named.

From 1 to 3 marks — one example

The same issue can earn 1, 2 or 3 marks, depending on how it is handled. Work through the source below, then see how the answer develops.

Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of the data in the source. [3]

1 markIssue merely identified: “There is an outlier.” The issue is named, but nothing is said about where it is or what it does.
2 marksIssue clearly described: “One student scored 28 on the memory task after only 4 hours of sleep, while the other students at similar sleep levels scored around 10. This point sits far above the rest of the cluster.”
3 marksIssue explained: “One student scored 28 after only four hours of sleep, far above the main pattern. Because an influential point can substantially affect the apparent relationship, the strength of the sleep–memory association is uncertain from this source.”

The move from description to explanation happens when the student states what the issue costs the interpretation — which claims the data can no longer support. A thinking prompt, not a compulsory template: after stating the issue, try continuing “…which means the source cannot support claims such as…”. If that clause comes out empty, the answer has stopped at description.

An outlier is not automatically “bad data”. It can be a genuine extreme value, a measurement error or a data-entry error. For Q1 you usually do not need to decide which explanation is correct unless the source provides that information — you point at the outlier and explain what it does to interpretation (for example, it can pull a mean or inflate a correlation).

Reading the six data displays

Paper 3 source material can contain other information — these are the six graph and table forms you need to know how to read. For each: what it shows, what to check, and one example of a genuine issue that could limit interpretation. The issue must actually be present in the supplied source.

Bar Box plot Frequency table Histogram Line graph Scatterplot

Bar graph

Categories · one summary value

What it shows

Compares separate, discrete categories using a summary value such as a mean, a count or a percentage. Bars are separated because the categories are distinct — a histogram’s bars touch instead, because its intervals are continuous.

What to check

  • Axes and units — what is being compared, and in what units?
  • Scale — does it accurately represent the size of the differences, or could it visually exaggerate or minimise them?
  • What each bar represents — means, counts or percentages?
  • Variability/error information, if shown — check what any error bars represent; overlap between error bars is not a significance test.
  • Sample size, if supplied.

Possible Q1 issue

A truncated scale that visually exaggerates a small real difference — but only if the scale actually distorts the interpretation in the source. The same values look very different depending on where the axis starts:

Box-and-whisker plot

Median · spread · outliers

What it shows

Summarises a distribution. The box holds the middle 50% of the data; the line inside it is the median. One box describes one group; two boxes side by side compare two groups.

What to check

  • Where the median sits inside its box, and how long the box and whiskers are.
  • Outliers plotted beyond the whiskers.
  • Which whisker convention is used — min–max, or a rule such as 1.5× IQR.
  • Sample size, if reported.

Possible Q1 issue

A visual gap or overlap treated as proof for or against a real group difference — overlap is a visual guide, not a significance test, and a visual gap does not prove significance either.

Frequency table

Counts · proportions · totals

What it shows

Shows how observations are distributed across the listed categories. Rows are categories; cells hold counts — or sometimes percentages — of how many participants fell into each. It gives counts (and proportions if supplied), but not individual scores, a direct measure such as the SD, or inferential results.

What to check

  • Frequencies or percentages?
  • Are the groups being compared the same size?
  • How were the category labels defined?
  • Is non-response or missing data mentioned?

Possible Q1 issue

Groups of very different sizes compared as raw counts, where proportions or percentages would give a fairer comparison.

Histogram

Continuous data · shape

Symmetrical — falls off equally on both sides

Right-skewed — the tail runs toward higher values

Left-skewed — the tail runs toward lower values

What it shows

Groups a continuous numeric variable into intervals (bins); the height of each bar shows how many values fell in that interval. Bars touch because the intervals are continuous and adjacent — the quick way to tell a histogram from a bar graph. Skew is named by its tail, not by its tallest bar.

What to check

  • Where the values cluster (the peak) and how quickly the bars fall away on each side.
  • The tail — right-skewed data have a long tail toward higher values; left-skewed toward lower.
  • Bin widths — unequal widths or badly chosen boundaries can hide or exaggerate features.
  • Summary statistics (mean, median, SD, sample size), if given.

Possible Q1 issue

Bin widths chosen so the grouping itself shapes the pattern — different bins, different peak — so the apparent shape depends partly on how the researcher grouped the data.

Line graph

Change across ordered points

What it shows

Shows how a variable changes across an ordered series — usually time. Each point is one measurement (often a group mean); the points are joined so the trend can be seen at a glance. Only the plotted points are data — the line between them is a way of joining the points, not evidence that the values moved smoothly between them.

What to check

  • Direction (up, flat, down) and how steep each segment is.
  • Whether the time intervals are truly equal — unequal intervals plotted at equal spacing distort the slope.
  • Whether the points carry error bars or are bare means.
  • How many points are plotted — sparse points joined by a line imply unsampled change.

Possible Q1 issue

Only group means plotted, with no variability information — so the trend cannot be judged against how spread the individual scores were.

Scatterplot

Two variables · association

What it shows

Shows the relationship between two variables. Each point is one paired observation — often one participant’s values on the two variables. Rising from left to right is a positive association; falling is negative. How tightly the points hug a straight line indicates the strength of a linear association.

What to check

  • Direction and tightness of the cloud.
  • An unusually influential outlier.
  • Whether the correlation coefficient r is reported — r runs from −1 to +1; the closer |r| is to 1, the tighter the straight-line association. r near zero means little LINEAR association, not necessarily no relationship.
  • Axes, units, sample size and labels.

Possible Q1 issue

A claim of cause and effect from a correlational display — an association does not establish causation; the relationship could run the other way, or a third variable could drive both.

Find the strongest issue

Do not memorise one limitation for each graph. Look at the actual source and find the strongest issue that genuinely limits its interpretation.

What to check — a quick search routine

  1. Read the axes and labels — what is measured, and in what units?
  2. Check every scale — does any axis exaggerate or minimise the differences?
  3. Find the spread — is any variability information reported (error bars, whiskers)?
  4. Spot the outliers — values sitting apart from the pattern?
  5. Ask what is missing — sample size, definitions, sample, statistics?

The issue bank

  • Missing information — the source leaves out something needed to judge it: sample size, how a variable was defined or measured, where the data came from, or any inferential statistic. Example: two groups compared, but never how many participants were in each.
  • Variability / uncertainty — too little information about spread or uncertainty to judge an apparent difference confidently. Example: bare group means with no SDs or error bars.
  • Outliers — an unusually extreme value that could materially affect a mean, a correlation or the apparent pattern. Example: one very high score pulling a correlation.
  • Scale — axis choices that visually exaggerate or minimise a pattern. Example: a truncated axis making a six-point difference look huge.
  • Causality — an association does not by itself establish cause and effect. Example: screen time and wellbeing measured together.
  • Operationalisation — a construct is poorly or unclearly defined/measured, so it is unclear what the values actually represent. Example: a mean “stress score” of 7 with no explanation of how stress was measured.
  • Context / sample — participant or setting characteristics limit what can reasonably be inferred beyond the source. Example: a pattern found in students from one school only.

This bank is a SEARCH LIST, not an answer sheet. An issue earns marks only if it fits the source you are given.

Exam practice — building independence

Now apply the full skill. Try each question before revealing anything — the scaffolding decreases as the tasks progress.

Practice 1 · guided

Daily Screen Time and Self-Reported Wellbeing

Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of the data in Source 1. [3]

Show guidance

The sharpest issue here is causality: the display only shows that the two variables were measured together. State the issue, then explain the consequence — the direction of the relationship and possible third variables cannot be ruled out, so the graph cannot support a cause-and-effect claim.

Show model approach

Issue → source detail → consequence

The scatterplot shows a weak negative relationship, but both variables were only measured together. More screen time may lower wellbeing, lower wellbeing may raise screen time, or a third variable may drive both. The display therefore supports a relationship claim but not a claim that screen time caused the change.

Practice 2 · less guided

Excessive Daytime Sleepiness by Year Group

Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of the data in Source 2. [3]

Show a hint

The year groups are made of very different numbers of students — 60, 28 and 15. Check what the raw counts seem to show, then what the proportions show.

Show model approach

Issue → source detail → consequence

The year groups are different sizes, so the raw counts cannot be compared fairly. Year 12 has 24 sleepy students out of 60 (40%), whereas Year 10 has only 9 out of 15 (60%). The raw counts therefore suggest the opposite pattern from the proportions, so using counts alone could mislead the interpretation.

Final unseen challenge

This last source is new. Identify the issue entirely on your own before revealing the model approach — it is the closest thing here to the real exam.

Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of the data in the source. [3]

Show model approach

Issue → source detail → consequence

The axis plots unequal intervals at equal spacing: one hour between 12:00 and 13:00 takes the same width as five hours between 15:00 and 20:00. The midday rise therefore looks as steep as the afternoon fall, though one took an hour and the other five. Because the axis misrepresents the rate of change, the graph exaggerates how quickly stress shifts.
