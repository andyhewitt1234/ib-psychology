Interpretation of Graphs

3 marks. Explain one issue that limits interpretation of a source — and why it limits interpretation.

Question 1 — Interpretation of graphs

3 marks · one issue · explained.

Q1 is not "describe the graph". Do not summarise what the source shows. You explain ONE issue that limits interpretation of the information from the given source — and why it limits interpretation.

IDENTIFY one issue→ CLEARLY DESCRIBE it→ EXPLAIN why it limits interpretation

Describe means tying the issue to a precise feature of the actual source. Explain means stating what the issue costs the interpretation — which claims the data can and cannot support.

Official IB markband

1 markA relevant issue is identified.
2 marksA relevant issue is clearly described.
3 marksA relevant issue is explained.

These are the 1–3 mark descriptors, reproduced verbatim from the IB Psychology Paper 3 markscheme. The complete markband (including the 0 descriptor) is shown again in a collapsible at the foot of this page.

ONLY THE FIRST ISSUE IS CREDITED. Choose the strongest issue you can support from the source and explain it fully. Do not list several limitations. The markscheme is applied to the first relevant issue stated.

Important

The issue must actually fit the source. One issue explained against the data beats five issues named.

Reading the six data displays

Paper 3 source material can contain other information — these are the six graph and table forms you are expected to know how to interpret. Each card shows the display first, then how to read it, and ends with an exam-style Question 1 and a short model response.

Bar Box plot Frequency table Histogram Line graph Scatterplot

Bar graph

Categories · one summary value

Look at this first. Two categories, two bars — taller means a larger summary value. The axis underneath decides how large that gap really is.

What you’re looking at

A bar graph compares separate, discrete categories. Each bar’s height shows one summary value for that category — a mean, a count or a percentage.

Because the categories are distinct rather than continuous, the bars are separated. A histogram’s bars touch instead, because its intervals are continuous.

How to read it

Start with the axes: what is being compared, and in what units? Then compare the bar heights. Check whether each bar is a raw count or a group statistic such as a mean.

If error bars are shown, first check what they represent. Depending on the source they may show standard deviation, standard error or confidence intervals. Overlap between error bars is not a significance test.

What to notice

  • Does the y-axis scale accurately represent the size of the differences, or could it visually exaggerate or minimise them?
  • Are error bars shown, and if so, what do they represent?
  • Are the bars means, counts or percentages?
  • Is the sample size reported anywhere?

What it can — and can’t — show

Can. Which category has the higher or lower displayed summary value. If error bars are included, they provide additional information about variability or uncertainty depending on what the error bars represent.

Cannot. The full underlying distribution, any individual’s score, or whether an observed group difference is statistically significant. That requires the appropriate statistical analysis, which a bar graph alone does not contain.

When interpretation may be limited

Only use one of these when the feature is actually present in the source — and you can explain why it matters.

  • A scale that exaggerates the difference between bars (for example a truncated axis).
  • Variability not reported where it would matter for judging how meaningful the apparent difference is.
  • Bars of different kinds compared as if they were the same (counts against means).
  • No sample size reported.

Box-and-whisker plot

Median · spread · outliers

Look at this first. One distribution: the box holds the middle 50%, the line inside it is the median, and the whiskers reach the extremes in this example.

What you’re looking at

A box-and-whisker plot summarises a distribution. The box itself always shows the same three values: the lower quartile (Q1), the median and the upper quartile (Q3). The box holds the middle 50% of the data, and its length is the interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1).

The whiskers extend according to the convention used in that particular plot. Some run to the minimum and maximum; others use a rule such as 1.5× IQR, with more extreme observations plotted separately as outliers. Always check the labels before interpreting a box plot.

How to read it

Compare medians (the middle lines) across groups, and compare box lengths as a guide to how spread the middle 50% is. Where the median sits inside its box hints at skew, and whiskers of very different lengths hint at unequal spread. One box describes one group; two boxes side by side compare two groups.

What to notice

  • Where does the median sit inside its box?
  • How long are the box and the whiskers?
  • Are outlier dots plotted beyond the whiskers?
  • Is the sample size reported? If it is not, this may limit how confidently you can judge how representative or stable the pattern is.

What it can — and can’t — show

Can. The median, the middle 50% of scores through the IQR, the spread represented by the whiskers, possible outliers, and how distributions compare. If the whiskers explicitly run to the minimum and maximum, then the full range can be read too.

Cannot. Individual scores, or whether a group difference is statistically significant. Substantial overlap means the visual alone may not justify claiming a clear group difference — and a visual gap does not prove significance either.

When interpretation may be limited

Only use one of these when the feature is actually present in the source — and you can explain why it matters.

  • A visual gap or overlap treated as proof for or against a real group difference.
  • An unusually extreme outlier, if it materially changes how the pattern should be interpreted.
  • No sample size reported, which limits how confidently the pattern can be judged.
  • Whisker convention undefined: without knowing what the whiskers represent, the spread cannot be read precisely.
  • A scale that exaggerates how far apart the boxes appear.

Frequency table

Counts · proportions · totals

Look at this first. Three categories, three counts. “Positive” is the biggest count — but counts alone don’t give percentages or tell you whether groups are the same size.

What you’re looking at

A frequency table shows how observations are distributed across the listed categories. Rows are categories; the cells hold counts — or sometimes percentages — of how many participants fell into each one.

Unlike a histogram, it does not show that distribution as a continuous visual shape. The table gives counts, and proportions or percentages if supplied — but it may not give individual scores, a direct measure such as the SD, or inferential results.

How to read it

Read across the rows to compare categories, then check you are comparing like with like. Are these frequencies or percentages? Are the groups the same size? How were the categories defined? Proportions and percentages are the reader’s job unless the source provides them.

What to notice

  • If groups differ in size, raw counts alone may make comparisons difficult or misleading. Would proportions or percentages give a fairer comparison?
  • Are these frequencies or percentages?
  • Are the category labels defined anywhere?
  • Is non-response or missing data mentioned?

What it can — and can’t — show

Can. Which category was most and least common in that sample, and how many participants were counted.

Cannot. Why the pattern exists (that depends on the design, not the table), whether it would replicate, individual scores, or whether a difference between counts is statistically meaningful. A table of counts reports no significance test.

When interpretation may be limited

Only use one of these when the feature is actually present in the source — and you can explain why it matters.

  • Groups of different sizes compared as raw counts, where proportions would be fairer.
  • Frequencies where a percentage (and its base) is needed to judge the claim.
  • Category labels with no definition of what was counted.
  • Percentages given without their base (“% of what?”).
  • No mention of sample size or missing data.

Histogram

Continuous data · shape

Symmetrical — falls off equally on both sides

Right-skewed — the tail runs toward higher values

Left-skewed — the tail runs toward lower values

A common error is naming the skew from the tallest bar. The tail decides: right-skewed data have a long tail toward higher values, while left-skewed data have a long tail toward lower values.

Look at this first. Each bar is one interval, and the bars touch because the scale is continuous. The tallest bars are the peak; the long thin side is the tail.

What you’re looking at

A histogram groups a continuous numeric variable into intervals (bins), and the height of each bar shows how many values fell in that interval. Because the intervals are continuous and adjacent, the bars touch — that is the quick way to tell a histogram from a bar graph, whose separate-category bars are gapped.

How to read it

Read the shape. Where the bars are tallest is where the values cluster; how quickly the bars fall away on each side is the spread; and a tail that runs on longer on one side is the skew. Skew is named by its tail, not by its tallest bar.

What to notice

  • If bin widths differ, check how frequency is represented. Unequal-width bins can be misleading if their heights are compared as though the widths were equal.
  • Could the chosen bin boundaries hide or exaggerate features of the distribution?
  • Are summary statistics (mean, median, SD, sample size) given?

What it can — and can’t — show

Can. Where the values cluster, how spread they are, whether the distribution looks symmetric or skewed, and any gaps in the data.

Cannot. The exact data points — values inside a bin are invisible — individual scores, or summary statistics unless they are printed on the graph.

When interpretation may be limited

Only use one of these when the feature is actually present in the source — and you can explain why it matters.

  • Bin widths chosen so the grouping itself shapes the pattern (different bins, different peak).
  • Very broad intervals that combine distinct values and hide clusters or gaps.
  • No sample size or summary statistics reported.

Line graph

Change across ordered points

Look at this first. One line joining ordered measurements. Follow it left to right — up, flat, down — but only the dots are data.

What you’re looking at

A line graph shows how a variable changes across an ordered series — usually time. Each point is one measurement (often a group mean), and the points are joined so the trend can be seen at a glance.

How to read it

Follow the line's direction to read the trend, and compare how steep each segment is to read the rate of change between measurements. Two lines on the same axes allow one condition to be compared with another at each time point. Remember that only the plotted points are data: the line between them is a way of joining the points, not evidence that the values moved smoothly between them.

What to notice

  • Are the time intervals truly equal? Unequal intervals plotted at equal gaps distort the slope.
  • Does the vertical scale accurately represent the size of the changes, or does it exaggerate the slope?
  • Do the points carry error bars, or are they bare means?
  • How many points are there? Sparse measurements joined by a line imply unsampled change.

What it can — and can’t — show

Can. Whether values trend up, down or stay level across the measured points, and roughly how quickly.

Cannot. Cause and effect: the line shows change across recorded points, but whether that change is causal depends on the study design. It also cannot show unsampled values between the points, or whether a change is statistically significant — without variability or significance information the difference between two lines cannot be judged.

When interpretation may be limited

Only use one of these when the feature is actually present in the source — and you can explain why it matters.

  • Irregular time intervals plotted at equal spacing.
  • Only group means plotted, with no variability reported to judge the trend against.
  • Sparse points joined as if change between them were continuous.
  • A compressed or truncated scale that exaggerates the slope.
  • No sample sizes reported.

Scatterplot

Two variables · association

Look at this first. Each dot is one student. A rising cloud is a positive association; the tighter the cloud, the stronger the straight-line pattern.

What you’re looking at

A scatterplot shows the relationship between two variables. Each point represents one paired observation — often one participant’s values on the two variables — with one variable on the horizontal axis and the other on the vertical axis. The cloud of points shows whether the two variables move together.

How to read it

Read the direction of the cloud — rising from left to right is a positive association, falling is negative — and read its tightness: points hugging a straight line mean a stronger linear association, while a loose cloud means a weaker one. A single point far from the cloud is an outlier, and in a small sample it can be influential.

What to notice

  • Is there a trend, and is it positive or negative?
  • Is there an unusually influential outlier — one that materially changes how the trend should be read?
  • Is the correlation coefficient r reported?
  • Are axes, units, sample size and labels given?

What it can — and can’t — show

Can. The direction and rough strength of a linear association between two measured variables.

Cannot. Cause and effect. The relationship could run the other way, or both variables could be driven by a third factor. And r near zero only means the points do not follow a straight line — a curved (non-linear) relationship may still exist.

The correlation coefficient r runs from −1 to +1. The closer |r| is to 1, the tighter the straight-line association; r near 0 means little linear association. There are no universal cut-off values separating "strong" from "weak" — read r in the context of the source.

When interpretation may be limited

Only use one of these when the feature is actually present in the source — and you can explain why it matters.

  • A claim of causality from a correlational display.
  • An influential outlier.
  • No r, no sample size, or a very small sample.
  • A truncated axis that exaggerates the steepness of the trend.
  • Points that follow a curve being described as a straight-line relationship.

How a full answer climbs from 1 to 3 marks

The same issue can earn 1, 2 or 3 marks, depending on how it is handled. Work through the source below: identify the issue, describe it precisely, then explain what it does to interpretation.

Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of the data in the source. [3]

1Identify. "There is an outlier." The issue is named, but nothing is said about where it is or what it does.
2Clearly describe. "One student scored 28 on the memory task after only 4 hours of sleep, while the other students at similar sleep levels scored around 10. This point sits far above the rest of the cluster."
3Explain. "One student scored 28 after only four hours of sleep, far above the main pattern. Because an influential point can substantially affect the apparent relationship, the strength of the sleep–memory association is uncertain from this source."

What changes between 2 and 3 marks: the 3-mark answer states a consequence (“conclusions about the strength of the relationship are uncertain”) and ties it to a precise feature of the source (the isolated point). Describing the data is not the skill — explaining what it costs the interpretation is.

An outlier is not automatically “bad data”. It can be a genuine extreme value, a measurement error or a data-entry error — and a data set is not wrong for containing one. For Q1 you name the outlier, point at it, and explain what it does to interpretation (for example, it can pull a mean or inflate a correlation).

Skills that transfer across every source

Whatever the display, three issues appear again and again in Paper 3 sources. Knowing the general patterns stops you from hunting for a unique limitation in every graph — you look for the issue the actual source raises.

Outliers

An outlier is a value that sits clearly apart from the rest of the data. Before you treat one as a mistake, remember the three standard causes: a genuine extreme value (an unusual but real participant), a measurement error (a faulty instrument or procedure), and a data-entry error (a value typed or recorded wrongly).

A single influential value can pull a mean or inflate a correlation, so in a small sample an outlier can change the whole story. For Q1 you do not have to decide which cause is right — the source will not say — you say what the outlier does to interpretation.

Scale manipulation

A truncated axis — one that starts part-way up the scale — is not automatically a problem; plenty of plots legitimately zoom in. The issue is whether the truncated scale distorts what the data imply. The same two values look very different depending on where the axis starts:

Read the axis before you judge the pattern. If you name a scale problem, state where the axis actually starts and explain how that changes the apparent size of the effect.

Missing information

A source can limit interpretation by what it leaves out: the sample size, how the variables were defined or measured, who the participants were and where the data came from, and whether any inferential statistic was calculated. These are legitimate issues even when the graph itself looks tidy — they limit the claims the source can support.

What should you check?

  1. Read the axes and labels — what is measured, and in what units?
  2. Check every scale — does any axis scale exaggerate or minimise the differences?
  3. Find the spread — is any information about variability reported (for example error bars or whiskers)?
  4. Spot the outliers — values sitting apart from the pattern?
  5. Ask what is missing — sample size, definitions, sample, statistics?

Do not memorise one limitation for each graph. An issue earns marks only if it fits the source you are given. The bank above is a search list, not an answer sheet.

Describing the issue is not explaining it

The difference between 2 and 3 marks is usually the consequence for interpretation. Compare:

2“The y-axis begins at 80 rather than 0, so the six-point difference fills most of the graph.” — a clear description: the feature is named and located, but nothing is said about what it does to the conclusion.
3“The y-axis begins at 80 rather than 0, so the six-point difference fills most of the graph. This visually exaggerates the size of the group difference and could lead the reader to overestimate the magnitude of the effect.” — the consequence is stated.

A reliable habit: after stating the issue, add the clause “…which means the source cannot support claims such as…” and complete it. If that clause comes out empty, the answer has stopped at description.

Final independent challenge

This last source is new. Try it entirely on your own before revealing the guidance or the model approach — it is the closest thing here to an unseen Question 1.

Explain one issue that limits the interpretation of the data in the source. [3]

Show guidance

The horizontal axis is not to scale: 12:00 to 13:00 is one hour, yet it occupies the same distance as 15:00 to 20:00, which is five hours. Think about what that does to how steep each part of the line looks.

Show model approach

Issue → source detail → why it matters

The axis plots unequal intervals at equal spacing: one hour between 12:00 and 13:00 takes the same width as five hours between 15:00 and 20:00. The midday rise therefore looks as steep as the afternoon fall, though one took an hour and the other five. Because the axis misrepresents the rate of change, the graph exaggerates how quickly stress shifts.
