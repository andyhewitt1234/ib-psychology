Question 2

Data Analysis

6 marks. Analyse the findings using the actual data and state a conclusion.

Overview Q1 Q2 Q3 Q4

Q2 is not “describe the findings”.

Analyse the findings from one of the supplied sources and state a conclusion. That means: read the pattern, support it with the specific data that are actually supplied, interpret what the data mean, and link your conclusion to those findings.

Q1 vs Q2

Q1: What limits interpretation of this source?   Q2: What do the findings show, and what conclusion do they support? Q2 is not primarily a limitation hunt.

The analysis chain

READ→ COMPARE / IDENTIFY→ SUPPORT→ INTERPRET→ CONCLUDE

READ the findings that are actually provided → IDENTIFY the important pattern → SUPPORT it with specific values → INTERPRET what the values mean (including any supplied variability or statistics) → CONCLUDE with a claim explicitly linked to the findings.

Use whatever findings are actually provided — for example means, medians, percentages, frequencies, ranges, SDs, IQRs, correlations, p-values or confidence intervals. Do not treat absent statistics as mandatory.

Detailed analysis usually needs specific evidence from the source. Use the actual values where they help you show the pattern clearly.

Important

The conclusion must be explicitly linked to the analysed findings. Its strength should match the evidence.

Official IB markband — Question 2

0

1–2
  • There is limited analysis of the data or the analysis contains inaccuracies.
  • A conclusion is attempted but it is not relevant.
3–4
  • Analysis of the data is accurate but lacks detail or development.

  • A conclusion is stated that is explicitly linked to the findings.

What “analysed in detail” looks like

Analysis is data + meaning — not just listing numbers.

Description“Group A scored higher.”
Analysis“Group A scored 78 compared with 70 for Group B, an 8-point difference.”
Developed“Group A scored 78 compared with 70 for Group B, an 8-point difference. However, the supplied variability information shows considerable spread, so the group difference should be interpreted cautiously.”

Conclusion: “The findings suggest Group A performed better, but the strength of that conclusion depends on the rest of the supplied evidence.” The conclusion is explicitly linked to the analysed findings.

A tiny worked example

Description“Group A scored higher.”
Analysis“Group A scored 72 compared with 64 for Group B, an 8-point difference.”
Developed“Group A scored 72 compared with 64 for Group B, an 8-point difference. If the supplied variability/statistical information also supports that difference, the evidence becomes stronger.”

What type of findings am I looking at?

Start with the most important pattern in the source, then use the relevant statistics provided to develop your analysis. The source determines the order.

MeansCompare group averages.
MediansCompare typical central values — especially useful when distributions may be skewed.
FrequenciesCompare counts/percentages carefully and check whether group sizes differ.
VariabilityUse SD, range, IQR or error information only if supplied — it describes spread around the summary value.
CorrelationsUse direction (positive/negative) and strength (|r| closer to 1 = stronger linear association; r near 0 = weak linear association). Do not infer causation.
InferentialIf p-values or confidence intervals are supplied, interpret them accurately (see below).

Read the statistic correctly — mini-reference

Statistical significance

If p is below the stated or conventional alpha level, the result is statistically significant under that criterion. A small difference can be statistically significant; a larger-looking difference can fail to reach significance. Statistical significance is not the same as practical importance.

Variability

Variability helps you judge how spread out the scores are around the summary value. If two groups have a modest mean difference but wide distributions, the difference may represent only part of the story. Whether that difference is statistically significant depends on the inferential statistics, not on eyeballing SD overlap.

Correlation

Positive r = variables increase together; negative r = one rises as the other falls; |r| closer to 1 = stronger linear association; r near 0 = weak linear association. Do not use universal weak/moderate/strong cut-offs. Correlation does not establish causation — but the core Q2 task is still what do the findings show?, not a methodology essay.

What can I say? — conclusion guide

Match conclusion strength to the evidence. Stronger evidence → “The findings support…”; moderate/mixed → “The findings suggest…”; limited → “The findings provide limited evidence…”. The conclusion must be explicitly linked to the analysed findings — an evidence-appropriate conclusion, not always a cautious one.

Solved example — smartphone presence and working memory

In a study of smartphone presence, students completed an OSpan working-memory task (scored 1–5) with their own phone either kept away or left face up on the desk nearby.

Example question:

Analyse the findings shown in Source 1 and state a conclusion about the effect of smartphone presence on working memory. [6]

How to answer: Start with the most important pattern in the source, then use the relevant statistics provided to develop your analysis. The source determines the order.

Show model analysis

The phone-absent group had a higher mean OSpan score (4.2) than the phone-present group (3.6), a difference of 0.6 points. However, both groups had the same median of 4, so the median does not show a difference between the groups even though the means differ. This suggests the difference between groups is not captured by the median alone.

The phone-present condition has a larger SD (1.8 compared with 1.3), showing greater spread in scores. The reported p value was .06, which is above the conventional .05 threshold. Using the conventional alpha level of .05, p = .06 would not be considered statistically significant.

Overall, the findings suggest slightly lower mean working-memory performance when the phone was present, but the evidence is not statistically significant under the conventional criterion.

Common mistakes

VagueVague description with no source evidence.
ListingListing numbers without interpreting them.
IgnoringIgnoring relevant variability or statistics when they are supplied.
p-valuesMisreading p-values (treating p = .06 as significant, or treating significance as practical importance).
CorrelationTreating correlation as causation.
OverreachGiving a conclusion that goes beyond the findings.
No conclusionForgetting to state a conclusion, or stating one that is not linked to the findings.
SignificanceTreating statistical significance as practical importance, or assuming a larger-looking difference must be significant.

Question 1 — Caffeine

Analyse the findings shown in Source 1 and state a conclusion about the effect of caffeine on reaction time. [6]

Show guidance

Compare the two means (320 ms vs 305 ms), note the similar SDs, then interpret p = .03 against the conventional .05 threshold. State a conclusion explicitly linked to those findings.

Show model approach

The caffeine group had a faster mean reaction time (305 ms) than the control group (320 ms), a 15 ms difference. The SDs were similar (30 and 28 ms). The reported p value was .03, which is below the conventional .05 threshold.

The findings therefore support a statistically significant difference in mean reaction time between the two conditions under the conventional criterion.

Question 2 — Correlations

Analyse the correlations shown in Source 2 and state a conclusion about the relationship between daily social-media time and wellbeing. [6]

Show guidance

Compare the direction and relative strength of the three r values. Together they show associations — because no experiment was performed, do not claim causation.

Show model approach

The strongest of the three correlations is with daytime sleepiness (r = .52), followed closely by sleep duration (r = −.48). The mood relationship is weaker (r = −.22).

Because these are correlations and no experiment was performed, the findings show associations but do not establish causation.

Question 3 — Study breaks

Analyse the findings shown in Source 3 and state a conclusion about the effect of study breaks on retention of material. [6]

Show guidance

Compare the means (68% vs 62%), note the SDs, and observe that no inferential statistic is reported. Consider what that means for the conclusion you can state.

Show model approach

Mean retention was 6 percentage points higher with scheduled breaks (68%) than with continuous study (62%). The SDs were similar (15% and 14%).

No inferential statistic is reported, so the source does not establish whether the observed 6-point difference is statistically significant. The findings show higher mean retention in the scheduled-break condition, but whether this supports a causal effect depends on the study design, which is not stated here.
