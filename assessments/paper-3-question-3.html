Paper 3 · Question 3

Question 3

Research Considerations

6 marks. Discuss credibility, bias or transferability, applied to a qualitative source.

Overview Q1 Q2 Q3 Q4

Question 3 concerns QUALITATIVE research considerations.

The question concerns qualitative research — findings may come from sources such as interviews, observations, focus groups, diaries or open-ended responses. Qualitative observation data are one example, not the definition. The exam specifies which consideration you must discuss. You do not choose.

Q1 vs Q3

Q1: Explain ONE issue limiting interpretation of a graph/source.   Q3: Discuss the SPECIFIED qualitative research consideration using source evidence. Q3 is not another generic “find a limitation” question.

Identify which consideration the QUESTION asks about, then use details from the qualitative source to answer that specific question.

The exam will ask ONE of these

Credibility“Discuss how the researcher could improve the credibility of the findings.”
Bias“Discuss how the researcher could avoid bias.”
Transferability“To what extent are the findings transferable to other populations or contexts?”

Each prompt demands a different answer. Credibility → how to improve; Bias → how to avoid/reduce; Transferability → to what extent.

How to answer — the core chain

READ THE QUESTION→ FIND RELEVANT SOURCE DETAILS→ APPLY THE CONSIDERATION→ EXPLAIN WHY IT MATTERS→ ANSWER THE EXACT QUESTION
CredibilitySOURCE DETAIL → WHAT CREDIBILITY ISSUE/OPPORTUNITY EXISTS → HOW CREDIBILITY COULD BE IMPROVED → WHY IT WOULD HELP
BiasSOURCE DETAIL → WHERE BIAS COULD ENTER → HOW THE RESEARCHER COULD REDUCE/AVOID IT → WHY IT WOULD HELP
TransferabilitySOURCE DETAIL → WHAT IT TELLS US ABOUT POPULATION/CONTEXT → COMPARE LIKELY CONTEXTS → WHY TRANSFER MAY/MAY NOT WORK → OVERALL EXTENT

For transferability a reasoned overall judgement is especially important because the question explicitly asks “to what extent”. For credibility and bias, discuss practical, source-specific ways the researcher could improve credibility or avoid/reduce bias — a brief overall statement may help, but do not force a “to what extent” conclusion when not asked.

What explicit source use looks like

Weak“Use member checking.”
Better“The researcher could use member checking.”
Developed“The researcher could return the interview interpretations to participants and ask whether they accurately reflect their experiences. This would give participants an opportunity to correct misunderstandings, improving the credibility of the interpretation.”

Three questions, three demands

Credibility

Asks: How could it be improved?
Think: What would make the interpretation more trustworthy?

Bias

Asks: How could bias be avoided/reduced?
Think: Where could influence enter, and what could change?

Transferability

Asks: To what extent does it transfer?
Think: How similar are other populations/settings to this one?

Credibility

Credibility concerns how trustworthy and convincing the qualitative findings are as an account of participants’ experiences or the setting studied.

Not mechanically reliability, internal validity or “truth”. The source may simply not report a technique — distinguish not reported from definitely not done. No technique automatically guarantees credibility.

Member checkingParticipants are invited to comment on transcripts, summaries or interpretations so the researcher can identify misunderstandings or disagreement. It may help identify errors; it does not automatically confirm findings are correct or eliminate interpretation.
Example: After analysing interviews about school stress, the researcher gives participants a summary of the themes and asks whether these represent their experiences accurately.
TriangulationTriangulation uses more than one method, data source, researcher or perspective to examine the phenomenon. Two methods do not automatically equal useful triangulation — the researcher must actually compare or integrate the evidence. Agreement may strengthen confidence; differences may reveal complexity.
Example: Interview students about classroom distraction AND observe classroom behaviour, then compare what the two sources suggest.
Prolonged engagementSpending sustained time in the research setting can help the researcher understand the context, build trust and distinguish typical patterns from one-off events. It may also reduce some forms of reactivity as participants become more accustomed to the researcher; it does not automatically eliminate reactivity.
Example: Instead of observing one lesson, the researcher spends several weeks in the school before drawing conclusions.
Peer debriefingAnother knowledgeable researcher examines the developing analysis, questions assumptions and challenges interpretations. It does not guarantee an objective interpretation.
Example: A second researcher reviews how interview excerpts were interpreted and asks whether alternative explanations fit the data.
Negative case analysisThe researcher deliberately searches for cases that do not fit the emerging pattern and considers whether the explanation needs revising.
Example: Most students say smartphones distract them, but two say phones improve concentration. The researcher analyses those contradictory cases rather than ignoring them.
ReflexivityThe researcher reflects on how their background, role, expectations and relationship with participants may influence data collection and interpretation. It helps recognise and manage influence; it does not eliminate bias.
Example: A teacher interviewing their own students records how the teacher–student relationship could affect what students are willing to say.

Spot it → Fix it

Source: One researcher coded every interview alone.
Spot: Interpretation depends on one analyst.
Fix: Add peer debriefing or independent scrutiny.
Why: Alternative interpretations can be challenged.

Bias

The official question is “Discuss how the researcher could avoid bias.” Use: SOURCE FEATURE → POSSIBLE BIAS → PRACTICAL WAY TO REDUCE/AVOID IT → WHY IT WOULD HELP. Discuss how to reduce/avoid — even though the prompt says “avoid”, use reduce / minimise / manage accurately; do not claim bias can be completely eliminated or that multiple coders automatically remove bias.

ExpectationsProblem: Researcher expects technology to damage attention before interviews.
Response: Reflexive notes, neutral prompts, independent scrutiny of analysis.
Why: Makes expectations visible and creates checks against interpreting every response in the expected direction.
Leading questionsProblem: “Why does social media make you anxious?” presupposes causation.
Fix: “How, if at all, does social media affect how you feel?”
Neutral/open phrasing reduces the extent to which the question pushes participants toward a predetermined answer.
ReactivityProblem: Students know their teacher is interviewing them.
Fix: Independent interviewer, confidentiality/anonymity, neutral setting, prolonged engagement where appropriate.
Socially desirable answers are less likely when the dual role is removed.
InterpretationProblem: Only quotations supporting the expected theme are reported.
Fix: Negative case analysis, transparent coding, reflexivity, peer debriefing.
Creates checks against selective reporting.

Spot it → Fix it

Source: Teacher interviews own students.
Spot: Power relationship may shape responses.
Fix: Use an independent interviewer.
Why: Students may feel freer to answer honestly.

Transferability

Transferability concerns the extent to which qualitative findings may be meaningful or applicable in other populations or contexts. Transferability is not the same as statistical generalisation. Qualitative studies often use small, purposive samples to understand a particular context deeply — a small sample is not automatically a failure.

The question is how similar another population/context is to the one studied, and whether the source provides enough detail to judge that similarity.

Consider: participant characteristics, setting, cultural/social context, sampling strategy, specificity of the phenomenon, thick description, similarities/differences between original and target.

Thick description

Thick description provides detailed information about the participants, setting, procedures and social/cultural context so readers can judge whether findings may apply elsewhere.

Example: Instead of “Students were interviewed” the source tells us ages, school type, location, relevant cultural norms, interview context.

Source → Transfer

Source: Six students from one Helsinki school with low grading pressure.
Similar target: students in schools with similar assessment cultures → transfer more plausible.
Very different target: younger children in a highly exam-driven system → transfer less certain.

Solved example — smartphone use in an Indonesian high school

Example question:

To what extent are the findings transferable to other populations or contexts? [6]

Show model approach

The findings are most transferable to educational settings that resemble the urban Jakarta school studied. The qualitative sample consisted of 11 participants — 8 students and 3 teachers — from one school, and all participants came from the same school community in one urban educational setting in Jakarta. School rules, norms around classroom phone use and the wider educational context may differ substantially elsewhere.

The study collected data across two terms, which provides an opportunity for prolonged engagement, and both students and teachers were included. Smartphone rules and school practices may differ elsewhere. The findings may therefore offer useful insight for comparable urban secondary schools, but transfer to very different age groups, countries or non-school settings should be made cautiously.

The same study, the other considerations

CredibilityUsing both observations and interviews could strengthen credibility if the researcher compares the findings across the two methods. The two-term period provides an opportunity for prolonged engagement.
BiasParticipants may respond differently because they know they are being observed/interviewed; researcher interpretation may introduce bias unless checks such as reflexivity or peer debriefing are used.
TransferabilityOne school, one city, one cultural/educational context and 11 participants — the findings travel only where the context is closely comparable.

Each item below gives you a qualitative source and a Question 3 prompt targeting one consideration. All are teacher-created practice in the Paper 3 format. Write your answer before revealing the guidance and model approach.

Question 1 — Credibility

Discuss how the researcher could improve the credibility of the findings. [6]

Show guidance

Identify where credibility could be improved: no participant review of transcripts/interpretations, one researcher analysing alone, and whether diary and interview themes are actually compared.

Show model approach

Point 1 — Member checking: No participant reviewed transcripts/interpretations. The researcher could ask participants to review transcript summaries or interpreted themes. Participants could then identify misunderstandings or disagreement.

Point 2 — Peer debriefing: One researcher analysed all material alone. Another researcher could review coding/themes and challenge interpretations, providing scrutiny beyond one analyst’s perspective.

Point 3 — Triangulation: Daily diaries and interviews provide two forms of data. The researcher could explicitly compare themes from diaries with interview accounts rather than merely collecting both. Convergence or divergence across the two sources could strengthen or refine the interpretation.

Question 2 — Bias

Discuss how the researcher could avoid bias. [6]

Show guidance

Focus on how to reduce/avoid bias: the teacher’s dual role, the loaded “push” wording, the researcher’s expectation, and selective extract choice. For each, tie a practical response explicitly to the source.

Show model approach

1. Independent interviewer: Use an interviewer who is not the child’s teacher. This would reduce social desirability/reactivity created by the dual role.

2. Neutral wording: Replace “push” with neutral open-ended questions. This reduces interviewer influence on participants’ responses.

3. Reflexivity and independent scrutiny: Keep reflexive notes and have another researcher review coding/extract selection, including negative cases. This challenges the expectation that effort-based talk dominates and the selective reporting that follows.

Question 3 — Transferability

To what extent are the findings transferable to other populations or contexts? [6]

Show guidance

Consider the six students, one Helsinki school, and the distinctive low-grading-pressure culture. Judge more plausible vs less certain transfer targets.

Show model approach

The study involved six students from one upper-secondary school in Helsinki — a school described as having low external grading pressure and where motivation was described as largely internal.

Findings may transfer more readily to students in comparable secondary settings with similar assessment cultures. Transfer to settings with strong external grading pressure or very different educational/cultural contexts is less certain. Transferability is not statistical representativeness; the question is whether another context is similar enough for these findings to be meaningful there.

Common mistakes

Wrong questionAnswering credibility when the exam asks bias.
Definition onlyDefining “transferability” without applying it to the source.
Generic method“Use triangulation” without explaining what would be triangulated in this source.
Invented detailClaiming purposive sampling, leading questions or selective quotations when the source does not say so.
Magic fixSaying member checking or a second coder “eliminates bias.”
GeneralisationTreating qualitative transferability as if it were statistical representativeness.
No source evidenceWriting a generic methods essay without explicit source details.
