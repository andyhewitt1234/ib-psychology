Paper 3 · Question 4

Question 4

Synthesis

15 marks. Evaluate the central claim using the source material and your own knowledge.

Overview Q1 Q2 Q3 Q4

Q4 requires you to interpret AT LEAST THREE of the supplied sources.

Interpret at least three sources, combine them with your own psychological knowledge, and discuss how valid the central claim is.

One or two sources are not enough for the full task. Three is the minimum — additional sources may be used where useful. Depth, relevance and synthesis matter more than counting sources.

Q4 is not a sequence of mini source summaries. Do not structure the answer as Source 1 says… Source 2 says… Source 3 says… Instead, organise around the CLAIM: support / contradiction / qualification / limitation / own knowledge / judgement.

Students interpret at least THREE of the supplied sources from the resource booklet and use their own knowledge to discuss the validity of the claim stated in the question.

The goal is not What does each source say? The goal is How strong is the overall case for the claim?

The synthesis chain

CLAIM→ INTERPRET SOURCES→ CONNECT SOURCES→ WEIGH THE EVIDENCE→ ADD OWN KNOWLEDGE→ EVALUATE DIFFERENT VIEWS→ JUDGE VALIDITY

A thinking framework, not a sentence template — not every paragraph must contain all seven steps.

What 13–15 actually looks like

  1. Answer the actual claim throughout.
  2. Use at least three sources.
  3. Interpret rather than merely quote/describe them.
  4. Connect evidence across sources.
  5. Use relevant own knowledge.
  6. Critically analyse how strong each piece of evidence is.
  7. Identify and evaluate different points of view.
  8. Reach a reasoned conclusion that follows from the argument.

No one-mark-per-feature formula — the bands describe the whole response.

Synthesis vs summary

Summary“Source 1 shows more stress with more screen use. Source 2 says a student struggled to switch off. Source 3 shows a correlation with less sleep.” — sources sit separately.
Synthesis“Across Sources 1–3, technology is associated with several negative outcomes, particularly stress and sleep disruption. However, Source 2 also reports a benefit and Source 3 is correlational, so the combined evidence supports a conditional rather than universal negative effect.” — sources are combined to answer the claim.

Organise by argument, not source number

Argument 1Evidence supporting the claim — Source 1 + Source 3 + own knowledge
Argument 2Evidence that qualifies or contradicts — Source 2 + Source 3 + own knowledge
Argument 3How far the evidence allows the claim to go — research design / context / own knowledge
ConclusionOverall validity

Paragraphs should have a purpose in the argument — not exactly three body paragraphs, but purposeful ones.

Different points of view

Different points of view must be psychologically relevant and evidence-based — not two random opinions. They may come from:

  • different sources pointing in different directions
  • competing psychological explanations
  • different interpretations of the same evidence
  • benefits vs harms; individual vs sociocultural explanations
  • evidence that supports vs qualifies the claim
  • own knowledge that challenges the booklet’s apparent message

Own knowledge — what counts

Not: random facts or unrelated theory dumps. Own knowledge may be research studies, theories, concepts or psychological knowledge — only if it helps judge the claim.

Should: support a source, challenge a source, explain a pattern, introduce an alternative explanation, qualify generalisation, or show conditions under which the claim may or may not hold.

Own knowledge is not “add a study” — a study is useful only if it contributes to evaluating the claim.

How much weight should this source carry?

Not every source deserves equal weight. Consider where relevant: research design, sample, measurement, statistical evidence, correlation vs experiment, qualitative depth, consistency with other sources, contradictory findings, applicability to the claim, whether the source actually measures the construct named in the claim.

How much does this source contribute to the validity of the CENTRAL CLAIM? — not another Q1 limitation hunt.

Not downgradeA single interview cannot establish prevalence or causality, but it can contribute lived experience, mechanisms, context and alternative perspectives. Ask What can this source contribute? not Is this source weak?
Not privilegeStatistical data are not automatically superior — a quantitative source may still be correlational, use self-report, measure a narrow construct or have limited context.
Source typeSource type determines what kind of claim it can support.

Does this source actually measure the claim? — Claim match

What does this source allow?

Claim validity as a scale

STRONGLY SUPPORTED→ PARTLY SUPPORTED→ CONDITIONAL→ LIMITED SUPPORT→ NOT WELL SUPPORTED

Not Is the claim true or false? — the strongest conclusions are often conditional.

Claim scope

“Technology harms mental health” is much broader than “High levels of disruptive technology use may increase stress in some students.” Teach students to narrow or qualify claims where evidence only supports a more limited version.

Claim-strength ladder

Worked example — technology in education and student mental health

Teacher-created example

Central claim: the use of technology in education may have a negative effect on the mental health of students.

Question: To what extent can we conclude that the use of technology in education has a negative effect on the mental health of students? [15]

Source 1

Weight: Source 1 shows higher self-reported test anxiety in high-technology classrooms (3.4 vs 2.8, p = .04). The p value indicates statistical significance under the stated criterion for this group difference; it does not show that technology caused the difference, nor does it speak to mental health as a whole — the measure is narrower than the claim. Students in high-technology classrooms reported higher test anxiety; causal language is not justified unless random assignment and experimental control are explicitly stated.

Source 2

Source 3

More app-based learning time is associated with less reported sleep (r = −.31) and greater self-reported stress (r = +.24) — both consistent with a negative pathway — but also with greater reported engagement (r = +.19), which qualifies a uniformly negative interpretation. Correlations do not establish causation; describe relative strength without fixed weak/moderate cut-offs.

Own knowledge — tightly linked

Relevant knowledge must stay tightly linked to technology + student mental health (not learning efficiency): for example social comparison/FOMO and sleep displacement may increase stress, while online social support or access to mental-health resources may buffer it. Individual differences (active vs passive use, purpose of use) condition the effect — presence of technology is not the same as harmful use.

An actual synthesized paragraph

Convergence and contradiction

Convergence can increase confidence, but ask: are sources independent? Do they measure the same construct? Do they use different methods? Are they subject to same limitation? Do they support same version of claim? Contradiction: identify what differs, consider why (population, operationalisation, method, context), and decide how much it changes the overall argument — do not force false balance.

Different points of view in this example

View 1Technology may harm wellbeing through stress and sleep disruption.
View 2Technology may also support students through access, communication, engagement or support.

Conclusion guide

A strong Q4 conclusion: 1) Answers how valid the claim is, 2) States conditions/limits, 3) Reflects the balance already discussed — no new evidence.

Show teacher-created planning example

Teacher-created planning example — not an official model answer

Supports: Source 1 higher test anxiety (3.4 vs 2.8, p = .04) — statistically significant for this group difference but narrow construct; Source 3 links more app use with less sleep (r = −.31) and more stress (+.24); Source 2 lived account of not switching off.

Limits/contradictions: Six classrooms/one region/self-report; Source 3 cross-sectional correlational; Source 2 single voice; Source 3 engagement +.19 and Source 2 benefit qualify the claim; p = .04 shows difference, not whole mental health.

Own knowledge: Sleep displacement, social comparison/FOMO and constant connectivity may increase stress; online support/access to resources may buffer; active vs passive use and purpose matter — technology presence ≠ harmful use.

Overall judgement: The careful wording “may have a negative effect” is partly supported — possible negative associations for some students/conditions, especially where use displaces sleep or focus — but the sources are few, self-reported/correlational/single-voice and contain counter-evidence, so a modest, conditional negative effect is plausible, not a clear general negative effect on mental health.

Micro-activities

Source weight — which carries most weight?

Show guidance

For a causal concentration claim, the experiment (B) is most relevant if its manipulation and measure directly target concentration — but check whether attention test really measures concentration as claimed. Weight depends on the claim.

Synthesis pair — combine in one sentence

Show model

“Although greater app use is associated with higher stress in Source A, Source B suggests technology may also provide social support, indicating that its effect on wellbeing is likely to depend on how it is used.”

Common mistakes

Source-by-sourceWriting three separate source summaries.
No synthesisUsing sources but never connecting them.
No own knowledgeStaying entirely inside the booklet.
Unrelated knowledgeAdding theory/studies that do not help judge the claim.
No different viewOnly presenting evidence that supports one side.
Methodology dumpListing limitations without explaining how they affect the claim.
Causal overreachTreating correlations or group differences as causal without support.
Claim driftDiscussing “technology” generally when the claim is specifically about mental health / concentration / motivation etc.
No judgementEnding without stating how valid the claim actually is.

Teacher-created practice — Technology

Question 1

Central claim: screen time during the school day harms students' ability to concentrate.

To what extent can we conclude that screen time during the school day harms students' ability to concentrate? [15]

Show guidance

The correlations are relatively modest in magnitude, with the strongest absolute value being .28. Source 1’s means differ by 0.8 points and p = .07 does not reach the conventional .05 criterion. The positive association with homework completion suggests screen use may also have academic benefits, but it does not directly measure concentration. Weigh, then judge.

Show teacher-created planning example

Teacher-created planning example — not an official model answer

Supports: Source 1’s means point in the claim’s direction (6.3 against 7.1); Source 3 links more screen minutes with lower attention scores (r = −0.28) and higher restlessness (r = +0.26); Source 2 describes divided attention in tablet-led lessons.

Limits and contradictions: Source 1’s means differ by 0.8 points, but p = .07 does not reach the conventional .05 criterion (SDs 1.6 and 1.9 show substantial spread, but SDs are not a significance test); Source 3 is cross-sectional and cannot assign causality; the positive association with homework completion (+0.14) suggests screen use may also have academic benefits, but it does not directly measure concentration; Source 2 also credits videos with helping.

Own knowledge: Divided attention and notification cueing can impair sustained focus, but well-designed digital tasks can support concentration through offloading and retrieval. Level of use and type of task matter more than screen time as such.

Judgement: A modest, context-dependent association in the direction claimed, but three relatively modest / non-significant sources with counter-evidence do not establish that screen time during the school day harms concentration.

Teacher-created practice — Culture

Question 2

Central claim: cultural values shape how students attribute their academic success.

To what extent can we conclude that cultural values shape how students attribute their academic success? [15]

Show guidance

The cultural contrast shows clearly (Sources 1 and 3), but two countries cannot prove culture caused the difference — country differences may also reflect education systems, norms, language, socioeconomic factors. Treat self-report conditionally.

Show teacher-created planning example

Teacher-created planning example — not an official model answer

Supports: Source 1 shows the predicted contrast — internal attributions dominate in Country A (64%), external in Country B (62%); Source 3 shows a correlation between higher collectivism and more external attribution (r = +.55) and less internal (−.41); Source 2 illustrates the theme in a lived account.

Limits: Two countries cannot represent culture as a whole; the surveys rely on self-report which may be influenced by how participants wish to present their explanations; Source 3 is correlational and cannot show that collectivism caused attribution — country differences may also reflect education systems, socioeconomic factors etc.; individual variation within cultures remains.

Own knowledge: Research on cultural differences in attribution and self-enhancement suggests that collectivist contexts tend to emphasise situational and social explanations and show less self-serving bias than individualist contexts, but these are tendencies and patterns, not deterministic rules — culture can influence attribution.

Judgement: Evidence is consistent with the claim and links collectivism scores with attribution patterns, but the two-country design and correlational data mean the claim is supported as a general tendency, not a firm causal rule.

Teacher-created practice — Motivation

Question 3

Central claim: rewarding students for reading reduces their intrinsic motivation to read.

To what extent can we conclude that rewarding students for reading reduces their intrinsic motivation to read? [15]

Show guidance

Source 1 p = .08 is not significant; Source 3 is correlational; Source 2 is one voice. Balance nuanced undermining-effect knowledge against conditions where rewards do not reduce motivation.

Show teacher-created planning example

Teacher-created planning example — not an official model answer

Supports: Source 1 mean enjoyment lower with reward (4.4 vs 4.8); Source 2 quote reports shift from reading for activity to reading for reward; Source 3 links greater reliance on rewards with fewer outside hours (r = −.35) and lower enjoyment (−.29).

Limits: Source 1 means differ by 0.4 points, while p = .08 does not reach the conventional .05 criterion (SDs 1.2 and 1.1 indicate appreciable spread, but SDs are not a significance test); Source 3 is correlational — students who read less may rely more on rewards; Source 2 single account.

Own knowledge: Research on the overjustification effect suggests that some expected, controlling rewards can reduce intrinsic motivation, especially when they shift perceived reasons for engaging, but rewards experienced as competence feedback or where intrinsic interest was initially low may preserve or raise motivation.

Judgement: Modest, non-significant or correlational support in the claimed direction; the undermining effect holds under specific conditions, so the claim is supported as a conditional tendency, not as a rule that rewarding necessarily reduces intrinsic motivation.
